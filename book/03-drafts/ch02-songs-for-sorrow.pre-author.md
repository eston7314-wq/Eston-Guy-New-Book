## Our ancestors made their masters' hymns into songs of their own

Our part of this story begins in 1619, when twenty African men were taken to Jamestown, Virginia. Over many years others were taken to Britain's colonies. They used musical instruments and spirituals to comfort themselves and to build support for one another, in their suffering and in their struggle for freedom. The rhythms comforted them and gave them the mental strength to carry on.<!-- note: thesis 2446-2451 (author's statement; no source in the thesis; ch02-evidence.md D3) -->

The spirituals began with the masters' hymns. On the plantations of the American South, the people who owned slaves sang Christian hymns in small churches. Enslaved Africans took some of those hymns and blended them with African songs, and out of the blend came work-songs. These became known first as "Negro spirituals" and later as African American spirituals. They joined African musical traditions to Christian themes, and our ancestors sang them for spiritual and mental upliftment, resilience and protest.<!-- note: thesis 2456-2462 (history; "Caucasian slave masters or plantation owners") and 3781-3786 (purpose phrase, "spiritual and mental upliftment, resilience and protest"); ch01 cut-list row 10, carried forward to ch02 by author approval 2026-11-13 -->

The same thing happened further north. In Canada, as in the USA, the spirituals grew up among the enslaved as a form of resistance and resilience. Chapter 1 told how African rituals there were woven into baptisms, prayers and hymns, and how drums could carry messages the masters could not read.<!-- note: thesis 3964-3965 (Canada section, 4.3.4); ch01 cut-list row 22, carried forward to ch02 by author approval 2026-11-13. They were the same people who took the master's hymns, mixed them with the songs of Africa and sang the result as comfort and as protest. Those hymns came first from the people who enslaved our ancestors, and some of our ancestors were forced to sing them. In chapter 1 I wrote about enslaved people who looked for ways to refuse forced hymns and forced baptisms. The spirituals show what others did with the same hymns. As I read it, they kept the words of faith, set them to African sound and turned them towards freedom. Here the double-edged inheritance is in the music itself: hymns sung by the masters came to carry the comfort and the protest of the people they owned.<!-- note: counterweight from ch02-evidence.md section 8.1: thesis 3958-3961 (forced participation in hymns), already used in ch01; referred back -->

If you grew up in a Black church, you may have sung spirituals long before anyone told you where they came from. Knowing their history can change how you sing them. The words in your mouth were first sung by people the law called property, and they sang them to lift their spirits, to stay strong and to protest.

Those spirituals were the forerunners of gospel music, because they joined a strong emotional impact to religious fervour.<!-- note: thesis 2462-2464 -->

## Gospel carried the spirituals into the cities and onto records

Gospel music began early in the twentieth century in the USA, among African Americans, and mostly in church. Blues, jazz and hymns all shaped it, and the blend reflected the social and political life of the time. It became possible when African Americans moved from the South to the cities of the North in what is called the Great Migration. There, churches became one of the main ways the music spread, and they gave choirs and solo artists a platform.<!-- note: thesis 2454-2455, 2473-2478. No dates for the Great Migration in the thesis; none added (ch02-evidence.md D5) -->

Organisations carried it further. James Cleveland founded the Gospel Music Workshop of America in 1968, and it helped to shape the music and to grow it, to the benefit of one generation of artists after another.<!-- note: thesis 2479-2482 -->

The years from the 1930s to the 1960s are called gospel's Golden Age. Mahalia Jackson, Thomas Dorsey and Sister Rosetta Tharpe stand out among the African Americans who made it. Dorsey, who is often referred to as the "Father of Gospel Music", brought elements of the blues into spiritual tunes and made a sound that appealed both to churchgoers and to listeners outside the church. Tharpe, a singer and songwriter, was the first great recording artist of gospel music, and among the first gospel musicians to reach rhythm and blues and rock and roll audiences.<!-- note: thesis 2484-2494 (author's statement; no source in the thesis). "1930s-1960s" corrected from the thesis's own heading at 2484 (2486 reads "1930s–960s"). Existence only, content not checked: Harris, The Rise of Gospel Blues (OUP; sources.md); Wald 2003, American Quarterly 55(3), doi 10.1353/aq.2003.0031 -->

Many of the best-known singers of the last century began in the same pews. Mahalia Jackson, Ray Charles, Aretha Franklin, Little Richard (Richard Wayne Penniman), Roberta Flack, Whitney Houston and Jimmy Cliff, to name a few, started their careers as young children singing hymns and spiritual tunes in Black churches. Later, jazz, soul and then R&B were added, and that revolution in gospel music helped to carry their careers around the world. I count this among gospel's gifts to our communities: it lifted them socially and culturally, and it gave them a voice for celebration.<!-- note: thesis 2585-2593 (author's statement; "mainly in the U.S.A."; Jimmy Cliff is from Jamaica, 2543, so no country is given for the churches). In the Caribbean, gospel meets the islands' own music and makes new expressions of faith. ## Song gave our people a voice for justice

Gospel has long been one of the ways Black people make themselves heard in the fight for equality. Many spirituals and gospel songs were sung during the civil rights movement in America, when activists of different races campaigned for justice and equality. Chapter 1 told that movement's story through Dr King; here I add only that it had its songs. I think it matters that they sang. People singing together in a crowd can find a courage that each of them might not find alone, and some of the songs they sang were spirituals, carried down from the plantations.<!-- note: thesis 2570-2574. Ch01 owns Dr King; one clause only (blueprint v2) -->

That tradition has not ended. In the UK, the USA, the Caribbean and Canada, gospel is still used to speak against racial inequality and poverty and to build up communities, and it inspires movements that aim to change conditions for the better. It remains, as I see it, a powerful tool for social transformation, and for building resilience.<!-- note: thesis 2576-2581, framed as the author's view -->

The record shows that our ancestors used these songs to comfort one another, to protest and to keep going, and the songs say so in their own words. When I call that a way of caring for the mind, I am speaking as a believer who has read the history, and I have measured nothing. Researchers have studied religious song, choir singing and mental health, and I have not yet weighed what they found, so I make no claim from their work here.<!-- note: ch02-evidence.md sections 3 and 8.3 (studies existence-confirmed only; findings not described, CLAUDE.md fact-checking rule 2). -->

## The old hymns still bring people out of isolation

Moreover, the churches of the African diaspora in the UK, the USA and Canada are an important source of social connection for African immigrants, who need the support of a community to keep their emotional balance. I believe that by reducing loneliness and isolation, these churches promote mental health. Music is a large part of how they do it. Many Black churches have brought gospel into their services and added Afrobeat and R&B to favourite and traditional songs, so that younger people in our diaspora and in multi-ethnic communities can take them up.<!-- note: thesis 2597-2605, mental-health claim framed as the author's belief (config rule 5) -->

If you came to Britain, the USA or Canada from Africa or the Caribbean, or your parents did, you may know what that support is worth. A new country can be lonely, and a church where the songs are familiar can be the first place where someone knows your name.

## Reflection and practice

Think of a song that has carried you: a hymn from childhood, a gospel song from a hard year, or a chorus someone in your family sang around the house. This week, sing it or play it once, slowly, and listen to the words as if you were hearing them for the first time. Notice what the song says about God, and what it says Jesus taught. Notice too how your body and your mood respond. If no song comes to mind, ask an older member of your family or your church which songs kept them going, and learn one of them.

### For church leaders

- Tell the story behind the songs. When your choir sings a spiritual or an old gospel hymn, say in a sentence or two where it came from and what it meant to the people who first sang it.
- Use music to reach people who are alone. A choir, a singing group or a midweek hymn gathering can draw isolated members and newcomers from abroad into the life of the church.

### For families

- Pass the songs down. Sing with your children and grandchildren the hymns and choruses that carried your family, and tell them when you needed them.
- Let the young bring their music. When younger members set the old hymns to Afrobeat or R&B, welcome it as the same faith in a new sound.

### For clinicians

- Ask about music. A patient of African heritage may use hymns, gospel or sung prayer to cope with distress. Asking what they sing, and when, can tell you a good deal about their supports.
- Treat singing as a support alongside care. If a patient is trying to sing or pray their way through symptoms that are getting worse, gently explore what other help they would accept.

### Questions for small groups

1. Which song has carried you, or someone you love, through a dark time? What did its words give you?
2. Our ancestors took hymns sung by the people who enslaved them and made them songs of comfort and protest. What does that say to you about a faith that has been misused?
3. In "Redemption Song", Bob Marley calls on his listeners to free their own minds. What might that mean for you, or for your church, today?
