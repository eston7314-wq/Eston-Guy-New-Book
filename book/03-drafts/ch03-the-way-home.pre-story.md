# The Way Home: The Teachings and Parables of Jesus

The songs in the last chapter named Jesus as their teacher, and they left a question: what did he teach that could hold a troubled mind together? When a scribe asked him which commandment came first, he gave two answers. Love God with everything you have, and love your neighbour as yourself. Then he went further and told his followers to love their enemies.<!-- note: picks up ch02's closing question (05-final/ch02, last paragraph before the closer). Mark 12:28-31 and Matthew 5:44, paraphrased here and quoted in section 1 (thesis 1871-1872) -->

For people of African heritage, that is a great deal to ask. Our ancestors were enslaved, and many of us still meet discrimination and exclusion in the countries we call home. For us, these teachings go against the grain of straightforward personal forgiveness.<!-- note: thesis 1874-1876, the author's sentence; "these sermons" becomes "these teachings" because Mark 12:31 is an answer to a scribe (Mark 12:28), not a sermon (ch03-evidence.md C3) --> Two thoughts can follow. One is that to forgive means pretending it did not hurt. I believe the teachings and parables of Jesus speak to both. I follow them here in turn: first his commandments of love and his teaching on forgiveness, then three of his parables, the Good Samaritan, the Sower and the Lost Son.

## Jesus asked us to love even the people who wronged us

## Forgiveness can free the one who forgives without excusing the wrong

Forgiveness is another central theme in Jesus's teaching. In the prayer he gave his followers, the one we call the Lord's Prayer, he joined our forgiving of others to God's forgiving of us: "And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors" (Matthew 6:12).<!-- note: thesis 1892-1894; Matthew 6:9 ("After this manner therefore pray ye") and 6:12 KJV, "debts ... debtors" by author decision (ch03-evidence.md section 3) --> Many of us learned to say "trespasses" in that line. I believe that practising forgiveness can help alleviate feelings of anger, resentment and bitterness that are detrimental to mental health.<!-- note: thesis 1894-1896, the author's sentence, framed as belief (config rule 5). Forgiving is also different from excusing. Jesus said: "Take heed to yourselves: If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him" (Luke 17:3). ## The Good Samaritan showed mercy across a line drawn by prejudice

The parables of Jesus, found in the Gospels, are full of moral and ethical teachings that can guide personal conduct and how we live together.<!-- note: thesis 1953-1955; "rich with" becomes "full of" (humanizer §16); the "this research ... discovers" framing (1952-1953) is on the cut list --> The first I want to open answers a question about the commandment we have just read. In Luke's Gospel a lawyer recites it to Jesus, love of God and "thy neighbour as thyself" (Luke 10:27). Then, "willing to justify himself", he asks, "And who is my neighbour?" (Luke 10:29).

Jesus answers with a story. "A certain man went down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and fell among thieves, which stripped him of his raiment, and wounded him, and departed, leaving him half dead" (Luke 10:30). A priest came down that road, saw him and "passed by on the other side" (Luke 10:31). A Levite did the same.

"But a certain Samaritan, as he journeyed, came where he was: and when he saw him, he had compassion on him" (Luke 10:33). He bound up the man's wounds, "pouring in oil and wine", set him on his own animal, brought him to an inn "and took care of him" (Luke 10:34).<!-- note: Luke 10:25-37 KJV, key verses from ch03-evidence.md section 3; thesis 1956 gives the reference only. 10:32 (the Levite) and 10:34 "beast" paraphrased -->

The man who stopped came from a people that Jesus's Jewish listeners kept apart from. John's Gospel says it in a single line: "for the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans" (John 4:9).<!-- note: John 4:9 KJV, background for "challenges social prejudices" (ch03-evidence.md C14; not in the thesis) --> At the end of the story Jesus turns the lawyer's question round. "Which now of these three, thinkest thou, was neighbour unto him that fell among the thieves?" The lawyer answers, "He that shewed mercy on him." "Then said Jesus unto him, Go, and do thou likewise" (Luke 10:36-37).

I believe this parable challenges social prejudices and puts compassion and mercy first. For individuals of African heritage who may face racial prejudices, it can reinforce the values of empathy and social justice, promoting psychological resilience.<!-- note: thesis 1956-1960, framed as the author's belief (config rule 5) --> If you have ever been the one others passed by on the other side, the story shows you a stranger who stopped. If you have ever been tempted to pass by, it gives you your instruction.

## Faith that puts down roots can outlast trouble and worry

Above all, the Kingdom of God is a central theme in the Gospels, and Jesus uses parables to illustrate its mystery. Mark's Gospel uses that very word, "the mystery of the kingdom of God" (Mark 4:11). Christians hold that the Kingdom of God is both a present reality and a future event.<!-- note: thesis 1738-1740 (what Christian theology holds, config rule 5); Mark 4:11 KJV fragment from ch03-evidence.md C21. "Kingdom of God" capitalised in narration as the thesis has it; KJV "kingdom" lower case inside quotations (author decision) -->

Furthermore, the parable of the Sower shows the varying responses to that message.<!-- note: thesis 1980-1981 ("Mathew" typo corrected; "illustrates" kept as "shows") --> Matthew tells it like this. "Behold, a sower went forth to sow" (Matthew 13:3). Some seed fell by the way side, and the birds came and ate it. Some fell among thorns, and the thorns choked it. "But other fell into good ground, and brought forth fruit, some an hundredfold, some sixtyfold, some thirtyfold" (Matthew 13:8).<!-- note: Matthew 13:3-8 KJV (ch03-evidence.md section 3); 13:4 and 13:7 paraphrased -->

In Matthew's telling Jesus speaks of "the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 13:11) and calls the message "the word of the kingdom" (Matthew 13:19); Mark's telling of the same parable speaks of the Kingdom of God. Jesus explains the stony places himself. Of the one who receives the word there, he says: "Yet hath he not root in himself, but dureth for a while: for when tribulation or persecution ariseth because of the word, by and by he is offended" (Matthew 13:21).<!-- note: author decision: Sower = "kingdom of heaven" in Matthew, "kingdom of God" in Mark (Mark 4:11). ## The father ran to meet a son who was still a great way off

The parable of the Prodigal Son, which some call the Lost Son, is about repentance, forgiveness and unconditional love.<!-- note: thesis 1962-1965 ("highlights themes of" becomes "is about"; humanizer §12) --> A younger son asks his father for his share of the inheritance, then "took his journey into a far country, and there wasted his substance with riotous living" (Luke 15:13). When the money is gone and famine comes, he ends up hungry. "And when he came to himself, he said, How many hired servants of my father's have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger!" (Luke 15:17). He decides, "I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee".<!-- note: Luke 15:11-18 KJV (ch03-evidence.md section 3); 15:12 and 15:14-16 paraphrased. Full stop outside: 15:18 goes on -->

"And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). He runs before the son has said a word of the speech he prepared. Then he calls for a feast: "For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found. And they began to be merry" (Luke 15:24).<!-- note: Luke 15:20, 15:24 KJV; "before the son has said a word" follows the order of 15:20-21 -->

I believe the narrative of a father welcoming back his wayward son can resonate with individuals who feel estranged or marginalised, offering a powerful message of reconciliation and acceptance. The message of this parable applies to individuals, families and church members who may have felt rejected for mistakes they had made. The hope of forgiveness can foster a sense of self-worth and belonging, which are essential for feelings of well-being and better mental health.<!-- note: thesis 1965-1972, the author's sentences, framed as belief (config rule 5) --> This is true, I believe, of many people of African heritage, especially the younger generations who may have been caught up in the misuse of drugs and alcohol, or who may have been isolated by parents or other elders for disapproved behaviours and decisions like teenage pregnancy.<!-- note: thesis 1972-1976, the author's observation, no number (ch03-evidence.md C18) -->

There is a second son in the story. The elder brother comes in from the field to the sound of music and dancing, and a servant tells him that his brother has come back. "And he was angry, and would not go in: therefore came his father out, and intreated him" (Luke 15:28). He is the one who stayed and did what was asked, and he finds it hard to welcome back the one who left. Many families have someone in that place, and sometimes it is a parent. The father goes out to both his sons. The parable never tells us whether the elder brother went in. It ends with the father's plea: "for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found" (Luke 15:32).<!-- note: Luke 15:25-32 KJV (15:28 and 15:32 quoted; 15:25-27 and 15:29 paraphrased). ## Reflection and practice

Bring to mind one person you find it hard to forgive, or one person you fear will never forgive you. You do not have to do anything about it today. Pray the Lord's Prayer slowly, and stop at "forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors". Say that person's name to God, and say honestly what they did, or what you did. Forgiving may take a long time, and it never means going back to someone who is still harming you. Then read the parable of the Lost Son (Luke 15:11-32) and ask yourself which of its three people you are closest to today: the son in the far country, the father watching the road or the brother outside the door.

### For church leaders

- When you preach on forgiveness, say in the same sermon that forgiving a wrong does not mean excusing it or staying where someone is being harmed, and say where people can find help.
- Be the father who goes out. Make sure that a young person who has been cut off, over a pregnancy, drugs or anything else, has someone in the church who will reach out to them and to their family.

### For families

- If you have closed the door on a son, a daughter or another relative, read the parable of the Lost Son with someone you trust, and ask what one step back towards them would look like.
- Teach children the Good Samaritan by living it. Notice together who in your street or your church is being passed by, and do one kind thing for them.

### For clinicians

- A patient of African heritage may feel that their faith requires them to forgive quickly. Ask what forgiveness means to them, and whether anyone is pressing them to forgive.
- If a patient speaks of forgiving someone who is still hurting them, treat it first as a question of safety.

### Questions for small groups

1. Jesus prayed "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" while he was still on the cross. What makes forgiveness hardest for you, or for our communities?
2. Luke 17:3 asks us to rebuke a wrong and to forgive when there is repentance. How can a church help people do both?
3. In the parable of the Lost Son, the elder brother "would not go in". Who might be standing outside the door of your family or your church today?
