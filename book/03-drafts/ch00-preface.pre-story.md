# Preface: Felt Before It Is Understood

I was coming to the end of my research when I heard that Pope Francis had died. He died on Easter Monday, 21 April 2025, the Bishop of Rome whom so many called "the People's Pope". The news sent me back to a single sentence in the Gospel of Luke.

## One sentence from Jesus brought my research home

In the days that followed I kept thinking about his humility. His concern for the oppressed and the marginalised reminded me of Christ's love for those who had lost hope. And I remembered what Jesus said to the woman with the issue of blood: "thy faith hath made thee whole" (Luke 8:48, KJV). Her story belongs to chapter 4, and I will tell it there. Here I only want you to hear the line, because it is the line I was carrying when I finished. In some unique way, Pope Francis made my research seem so relevant to today's challenges.

That research was a doctoral thesis for Selinus University of Science and Literature, completed in 2025 for a PhD in Religion. Its title asked one long question. Do religious awareness, religious knowledge and religious practice derived from New Testament Christianity influence the mental health of people of African heritage living in the United Kingdom, the United States of America and Canada? This book is my attempt to answer that question in plain words, and to say what the answer might mean for you and for the people you love. When I signed the thesis declaration, I wrote that its contents were the result of my readings, my personal experiences and the research I had done. This book draws on the same three, and the experiences come first.

The question itself began much earlier, with my family and in school.

My wife, my sons, my grandsons, my granddaughter, my daughter-in-law and the wider family all inspired me to undertake the study. Professor Salvatore Fava [AUTHOR TO CONFIRM: may this person be named?], who supervised it, motivated me to develop and keep a high standard of research.

Three other people shaped the years before it, and all three have now died. Father F. W. G. Gould [AUTHOR TO CONFIRM: may this person be named?] was the headteacher who supervised my probationary year as a newly qualified teacher in North London. ## Faith gives many people a home, and a home can also hurt

New Testament Christianity plays a very significant role in the lives of many people of African heritage. Its messages of redemption, hope and community are the ground on which their faith and practice stand. For generations its teachings, parables and miracles have given Christians hope, a spirit of community and moral guidance. I believe it does something more as well. In the African communities of the UK, the USA and Canada, Christianity gives people a connection to their cultural identity and to one another that is hard to replace.

When something sits that close to a person, it reaches into how they think, feel and cope. A verse read at the right moment can steady a frightened mind for a night. The same verse, used carelessly by someone in authority, can leave a person who is unwell believing that the illness is their own fault.

I will be honest about both sides. Part Three of this book is about the times faith hurts: guilt, talk of demons, the stigma that keeps people silent, and the hardest conversations a church can have. I will not ask you to choose between your faith and your doctor. Prayer and professional care can work together, and the later chapters show how.

I have written mainly for people of African heritage in the UK, the USA and Canada, and for the families and church members around them. If that is not you, you are welcome all the same. You do not need to be a Christian to read this book, and you do not need to know any theology or psychology. If you lead a church, care for someone at home or work in mental-health services, there is something here for you too. My hope from the start was that churches and mental-health services would understand each other better, so that the support people of African heritage receive could be joined up and fit their culture and their faith.

## Words mean what we use them to mean

The philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein suggested that, in many cases, the meaning of a word is its use. A word is defined by how people use it in daily life, more than by any logical structure that lies beneath it. So before we go further, here is how I use the words this book leans on most.

By African heritage I mean people often described as Black, or as mixed-race with one parent of African descent. The term also takes in people born on the African continent and those who have migrated from it. Where I need to be more particular, I use African British, Afro-Caribbean, African American and African Canadian for people of African heritage living in particular communities.

By mental health I mean the part of our overall health that covers our emotional, psychological and social well-being. In plain words, it is how we think, feel and act, how we handle stress, how we get on with other people and the choices we make. You can see it in an ordinary week: whether you can get up and face the day, and whether worry lets you sleep at night. The World Health Organization describes good mental health as a state of well-being that lets us cope with the stresses of life, realise our abilities, learn and work, and contribute to our families and communities [UNVERIFIED: WHO wording; author to choose WHO alone or WHO+CDC]. Mind and body are bound up together, too. Long-lasting conditions such as diabetes, heart disease and stroke affect how we behave, and living with a chronic condition can raise the risk of developing a mental-health condition [UNVERIFIED: thesis 1184-1188 gives no source].

By faith I mean, for now, a strong personal belief in God's power and in Jesus incarnate: confidence in what is hoped for. Chapter 4 gives the fuller definition.

My thesis title named three more terms, and together they are the lens I use all through this book. I call them the three strands. Religious awareness is recognising and acknowledging your own faith. Religious knowledge is how deeply you understand its scriptures, teachings and practices. Religious practice is what you do: praying, worshipping, reading scripture, Bible study and celebrating the Eucharist.

The strands overlap in real lives. Think of a man who can still recite the psalms he learned as a boy but has not been inside a church for years. His knowledge is strong, his practice has lapsed, and his awareness of himself as a believer may come and go with his mood. Each strand can help him, and each can be bent against him. The three strands are my way of looking, a lens I chose for the research, and I ask you to hold them as that. Faith is untidier than any three words.

## Fifty people answered my questions

I began with reading, in scripture and theology and in what other researchers had found. Then I wanted to hear from people themselves. I set out to learn how aware people are of their faith, how much of it they know and practise, which New Testament teachings matter most to their mental health, and where culture and faith meet, sometimes helping each other and sometimes pulling apart. I also wanted to give something back: guidance that would help mental-health practitioners take culture and faith seriously, and ideas for churches and community groups that want to support people better.

Fifty people of African heritage completed my survey: 20 in the UK, 16 in the USA and 14 in Canada, thirty women and twenty men. Later I spoke with twenty of them in interviews [AUTHOR TO CONFIRM: did you conduct the 20 interviews yourself?].

Two of the overall numbers belong here at the start. Across the whole group, 38 of the 50 said they believe their faith and religious practices help them manage mental-health challenges. Only 23 of the 50 described their church community as supportive or very supportive about mental health. I set those two numbers side by side and leave them there for now.

Fifty people cannot speak for everyone of African heritage, and I will not pretend they do. These are answers people gave about themselves. Everyone who took part did so for academic research, so in this book I report only numbers across the group and never one person's answer or words. Chapter 10 tells what the fifty reported, honestly and with its limits, and the afterword says plainly what the research could not show.

## Faith is felt before it is understood

When I came to write up the research, I found that language itself set a boundary. Faith is often felt before it is understood, and mental health is lived before it is named or identified.

A grandmother may pray every morning of her life without ever being asked to explain what prayer is. A young man may live with low mood for years before anyone, himself included, calls it depression.

The same idea sets the order of this book. It starts with history and song, where faith was carried and felt, and moves step by step towards practice. Part One, Roots, asks where this faith came from and how it was sung. Part Two looks at what the New Testament offers: the teaching of Jesus, the healing stories and letters, prayer and worship, and the church as a family. Part Three names the harm plainly and asks what helps. Part Four reports what fifty people told me, sets out how church and clinic can work together, and ends with what is still unknown. Two short appendices follow, one on how the research was done and one on where to find help.

You can read straight through. If you or someone you love is struggling now, you may prefer to go first to Part Three and Appendix B and come back to the beginning later. Each chapter ends with a short reflection, a few points for church leaders, families and clinicians, and questions a small group can talk over.

Before any of that comes an older question. How did this faith reach the ancestors of so many people of African heritage, and how did it come to be handed down in chains?

## Reflection and practice

Think of one time your faith steadied you, and one time something said in the name of faith made a hard season harder. You may not have words for either yet. Many people feel these things long before they can name them, and this book is one place to start finding the words.

### For church leaders

- Ask where in your church a person could say "I am not coping" without being judged for it, and who would hear them.
- Find out the nearest mental-health service and the crisis line for your country before someone in your congregation needs them.

### For families

- Notice which words your family uses for low mood, worry or breakdown, and which words it avoids.
- If someone at home seems low or withdrawn, ask how they are, and then ask a second time.

### For clinicians

- When a patient of African heritage tells you their faith matters, ask how it helps them, and whether anything said in church has made things harder.
- Ask whether there is someone at church they trust, and whether they would like that person involved in their care.

### Questions for small groups

1. Which of the three strands, awareness, knowledge or practice, is strongest in your life at the moment, and which is weakest?
2. How would you have answered the two survey questions in this preface?
3. When has your faith felt like a home to you, and when has it felt heavy?
