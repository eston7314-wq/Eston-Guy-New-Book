## Scripture read slowly and often can give a weary mind rest

Scripture reading and meditation are common practices among Christians. Reading the Bible, or studying a New Testament text, encourages believers to reflect on their lives in the light of God's word. It lets them think deeply about Jesus's teachings, his healings and his attitude towards the children of God. I believe that meditation on passages such as Matthew 11:28-30, where Jesus invites the weary to find rest in him, can promote mental and spiritual healing: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matthew 11:28-30). A yoke is the wooden frame that joins working animals so that they can pull a load together. As I read it, Jesus offers to share the load and to teach us how to carry it: "learn of me" (Matthew 11:29). The word itself appears in the first letter to Timothy: "Meditate upon these things; give thyself wholly to them" (1 Timothy 4:15). Much of what a praying person knows is learned in this way, by doing it. The philosopher Michael Polanyi brought the ideas of tacit knowledge and personal knowledge to the centre of academic inquiry. In *Personal Knowledge* (1958), as I read him, Polanyi recognised the personal side of knowing. He held that tacit knowledge lives in people as experience and skill, and that it must be put into language or models before others can understand it. He proposed that people can know more than they are told, so they build knowledge by working with others, in groups and in organisations; tacit knowledge is gained through practice and experience and passed on through shared knowing. I believe that view fits the experiential nature of religious knowledge, which centres on a person's direct experience of God. Faith and practice strengthen each other. Faith can deepen the meaning of prayer and make it a stronger source of comfort, whether a person prays alone or with others. The practices in turn reinforce faith, and the two make a cycle of support. ## Reflection and practice

Set aside ten quiet minutes this week. Read Philippians 4:6-7 slowly, then read it again. Before you pray, write down the cares you are carrying, and put beside each one something you can thank God for, since the verse asks for "prayer and supplication with thanksgiving". Then bring them to God one by one, in your own words. If a psalm or a verse has helped you before, say it aloud, more than once. Notice whether anything changes in you, and whether any of those cares also needs a person: a friend, your pastor or a doctor. If one does, name the step you could take, and ask God to go with you as you take it.

### For church leaders

- Teach prayer as a practice, and practise it with your people: in prayer meetings, at the Eucharist and in the ordinary weekly hymn. When you preach on James 5:15, read 5:14 with it, and never let "the prayer of faith" become a verdict on someone who is still unwell.
- Make your church a place where a person can say "I am praying, and I am also seeing a doctor" without being asked whether their faith is strong enough.

### For families

- Pray together, even briefly, and let children hear you bring real worries to God. Read Matthew 11:28-30 aloud at the end of a hard day.
- If someone in your home is praying hard and still struggling, pray with them, and help them find other help as well.

### For clinicians

- Ask a patient of African heritage what they do when they are distressed. Prayer, a psalm said again and again, a hymn or a candle may be part of how they cope, and you can work with it.
- Listen for prayer that has become a reason to put off care, or a source of guilt when healing has not come.

### Questions for small groups

1. Jesus prayed "Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me". An angel came to strengthen him, and he still went to the cross. What has prayer been like for you in a hard season?
2. Which of the practices in this chapter do you return to most, and which have you never tried?
3. Colossians 3:16 asks us to teach and admonish "one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs". How could our church make its singing a place where people are cared for?
