The thesis gives the chapter a clear, warm line: the spirituals came from the hymns of the enslavers blended with African song (2458-2462, 3781-3786), and became gospel in the early twentieth century (2454); gospel grew through the Great Migration, Dorsey, Tharpe, Jackson and the GMWA (2473-2494); it travelled with Windrush to Britain (2498-2502), met calypso, reggae and soca in the Caribbean (2535-2552) and found a home in Canada's Black churches and the GMA Canada Covenant Awards (2557-2566); it served protest and resilience (2570-2581); and today Afrobeats and R&B bring young people back to the old hymns, inside churches that ease loneliness (2597-2605). All of that is thesis-supported and the writer can carry it on the author's authority.

The evidence is weaker than the outline assumes in four places. **First**, the thesis gives no source for any claim in these lines, and none of its gospel bibliography entries is cited in the text; three of them (Davis 2003, Hyman 2019, Kearney 2012) could not be found at all, and Davis is not at the citation given. The chapter therefore rests on the author's statement, not on documented sources. **Second**, the blueprint's shift, "Singing has been a mental-health practice for our people for four hundred years", is a mental-health claim, but nothing in the thesis or the survey measures music and mental health: the thesis says the songs gave "mental strength to carry-on" (2450-2451) and "mental relief" (2444, inside the unconfirmed F1 passage), and the survey asked no music question. The writer should frame the mental-health benefit as the author's belief and the tradition's testimony (config rule 5), not as a finding. Section 3 lists five studies that exist on the topic; if the author reads them and wants one, it can be cited then. **Third**, the story slot needs a song that carried *a person* through; the only concrete case in the thesis (the Liverpool lockdown choir) is about a community and names no one, and three of its details are unclear. **Fourth**, the UK paragraph has two of its three examples unconfirmed (Tharpe in Liverpool; Elevation Worship), leaving the London Community Gospel Choir and the Liverpool choirs.

## 8. Strongest counter-evidence

Under the rules I cannot report what outside studies found, so the strongest counterweights here are from the thesis itself and from the state of the evidence:

1. **Hymn-singing was also imposed.** The thesis says enslaved Africans looked for ways of "rejecting forced participation in Christian rituals- whether they were baptisms, prayers, singing hymns or 'breaking bread'" (3958-3961), and that for some, Christianity was "a tool of control, pacifying and suppressing their desires for freedom" (3967-3970). The same hymns the spirituals grew from were part of the system of control. The chapter should hold both: song as resistance, and song as something sung under compulsion. Ch01 already used 3958-3961; ch02 can refer back to it.
2. **No measured link in this book's own research.** The 50-person survey has no question on music or singing, so the chapter cannot say what participants reported about gospel (config rule 10 and the survey-aggregate).
3. **The outside evidence is not yet weighed.** Daykin, Norma, Louise Mansfield, Catherine Meads, Guy Julier, Alan Tomlinson and Annette Payne, "What Works for Wellbeing? A Systematic Review of Wellbeing Outcomes for Music and Singing in Adults", *Perspectives in Public Health* 138 (1): 39-46 (Crossref issued 2017), doi 10.1177/1757913917740391; and Williams, Elyse, Genevieve A. Dingle and Stephen Clift, "A Systematic Review of Mental Health and Wellbeing Outcomes of Group Singing for Adults with a Mental Health Condition", *European Journal of Public Health* 28 (6): 1035-1042 (2018), doi 10.1093/eurpub/cky115, are systematic reviews that exist (existence only). Systematic reviews are where the strength and limits of the evidence on singing would be stated; I have not read them and do not report their conclusions. If the author wants the chapter to say singing helps mental health as a fact rather than a belief, he should read these first.
