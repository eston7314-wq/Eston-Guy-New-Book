## 1. The brief's specific points

### 1.1 The opening: ch04's hook must be met

Approved ch04 ends (05-final/ch04-thy-faith-hath-made-thee-whole.md, line 101): "She reached for the border of his garment. Most of us reach for God in prayer. The next chapter turns to prayer, scripture and worship, and to what they can do for a troubled mind."

So ch05 owes the reader **prayer first**, and "what they can do for a troubled mind". The thesis offers no scene to open on; what it offers:

- The title verse is not in the thesis. The KJV wording is **1 Peter 5:7**: "Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you." Note the KJV has **"care"**, singular; the chapter title's "Cares" is the title's own wording, so a quotation must keep "care". The thesis's own sentence is close to it: "New Testament texts often encourage believers to cast their anxieties upon God" (2226-2228). 1 Peter 5:8 belongs to ch08 (blueprint); 5:7 is not assigned elsewhere, and the title makes it ch05's natural first verse. Psalm 55:22 ("Cast thy burden upon the LORD, and he shall sustain thee") is the Old Testament parallel, if wanted.
- The strongest picture of prayer in a hard hour is Jesus in Gethsemane (thesis 2234-2236; section 3.1): he "began to be sore amazed, and to be very heavy" and said "My soul is exceeding sorrowful unto death" (Mark 14:33-34), then prayed. This meets the "troubled mind" of the hook in the text's own words.
- The candles and incense (2395-2398) are the only physical texture of prayer in the thesis (blueprint story-slot note).

**For the writer.** Ch04's last three paragraphs already quote scripture; the blueprint warns against opening three chapters running on a Bible quotation. Opening on the Gethsemane scene told in narration (with the KJV words inside it) or on the candles and incense, rather than a bare verse, meets the hook and keeps the pattern varied.

## 7. Synthesis (and where the evidence is weaker than the outline assumes)

The scripture carries the practice half of this chapter well, and better than the thesis's own references. Philippians 4:6-7 is the right centre: "Be careful for nothing" names the anxious mind, and "the peace of God, which passeth all understanding" is what prayer is said to bring. 1 Peter 5:7 gives the title its verse ("Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you"). The texts show Jesus praying alone as a habit (Mark 1:35; Luke 5:16; Matthew 14:23; "as he was wont", Luke 22:39). Gethsemane shows prayer in distress at its most honest: "My soul is exceeding sorrowful unto death" (Mark 14:34), and three friends asked to "watch" with him (14:33-34). James 5:13-16 joins prayer, singing, the elders and confession "one to another". Colossians 3:16 makes singing a way the word of Christ dwells "richly" among people, and Matthew 25:35-40 makes service a meeting with Christ himself. The author's definition of a spiritual practice (2180-2186), his eight daily practices (2199-2202), his candles and incense (2395-2398) and his integration paragraph (2341-2351) give the chapter its frame on his own authority. Polanyi gives the "known by practising" paragraph, credited to *Personal Knowledge* (1958) as the author reads it.

The evidence is weaker than the outline assumes in five places. **First, argument 5.** The outline treats Smith, Koenig, Ali and Thompson and Larcom as four published studies that can be "verified". Under the rules only one can be tied to a real work with confidence (the UK lockdown study, now Iyer, Larcom, Shah and She 2025), and even its citation is wrong in year, lead author and co-authors, and its USA half cannot be found. Smith 2003 probably exists but is not identified. Koenig 2009 has no bibliography entry and two possible candidates. Ali and Thompson 2019 cannot be found, and the journal's own records leave no room for it at the cited pages. Every finding in argument 5 is the thesis's report, not checked. The honest chapter says "studies I cited in my research report ...", keeps "associated", carries the flags, and lets the author check the four papers himself. **Second, the eight practices** (argument 1) rest on an unnamed "Research shows" (2197); they are best written as the author's observation. **Third, causal language.** "To treat, cure or reduce" (2253), "thus reducing" (2387) and "demonstrating effectiveness" (1590-1591) claim more than the thesis's own sources could, even if confirmed: the evidence it cites is about association. **Fourth, the scripture references in the thesis are loose** (forty days, Luke 22:39, Mark 13:32, Philippians 4:6, Matthew 25:35): every one is fixable from the KJV, and the corrected verses are stronger for the argument than the slips. **Fifth, no story and no case.** The slot needs the author's own prayer or scripture habit in a hard season; nothing in the thesis or in his two saved stories fills it without repeating ch02 or ch03.

## 8. Strongest counter-evidence

Under the rules I cannot report what outside studies found, so the strongest counterweights come from the thesis itself and from the scripture.

1. **The thesis says prayer is used instead of treatment.** Church leaders "often advising congregants to rely mainly on prayer and faith for healing" (2668-2671); the religious framework "often leads to the rejection of professional mental health services in favour of prayer and spiritual remedies, including water baptism and fasting", and "may deter some individuals from seeking the professional care that they need" (2736-2739); "faith leaders often address mental distress through prayer and deliverance" (3651-3655, used in ch04). This is the harm side of ch05's shift ("Prayer is all I need"), in the author's own work. Ch07 and ch08 own it; ch05 names it once and keeps the promise "I will be honest about when prayer is not enough on its own" for ch08.
2. **James 5:15 can blame the unhealed.** "The prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up" read alone tells the person still ill that their prayer, or their faith, failed. The same passage answers it: the sick are told to "call for the elders" (5:14), so healing is sought in company, and the thesis's own line puts prayer beside professional care (2349-2351). Ch04 has already guarded the same risk for Mark 11:24 ("Never tell someone who is still ill that they lacked faith", 05-final/ch04 line 110).
3. **Prayer did not take Jesus's distress away at once.** In Gethsemane he prayed "take away this cup from me" (Mark 14:36), was "in an agony" and "prayed more earnestly" (Luke 22:44), and went on to the cross. The text gives strength in the trial ("there appeared an angel unto him from heaven, strengthening him", Luke 22:43) and the prayer "nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done" (22:42), not the removal of the trial. A chapter that promises peace from prayer must leave room for prayer that is still in agony.
4. **"Associated" is not "causes".** The thesis's own studies (as it reports them) are about association, and "thus reducing" and "effectiveness" overstate them. People who are well may find it easier to attend and pray; the chapter cannot rule that out and should not try. Li et al. 2016 (R9) is a prospective design on exactly this question; its finding is not checked, and the author should read it before the chapter leans on attendance.
5. **Intercessory prayer as a physical cure has been tested.** Benson et al. 2006 (R10, the STEP trial) and Roberts, Ahmed and Davison 2009 (R11, Cochrane) are trials and a review of prayer for the sick. **Their findings are not checked here**; their existence means the author should read them before the chapter writes that prayer "can bring healing ... physically" (2263-2265) as anything other than his belief.
6. **The struggle side.** Religious and spiritual struggle (Pargament et al. 2001; Exline et al. 2014; sources.md 44, 47; titles only) and the thesis's own "duality" (4899-4903, ch07 and 99 own the sentence) say faith practices can also carry guilt and fear. Ch05 should point to ch07 in a sentence.
