## Change log (line, original / revision / why)

### 1. Line 7, opening: new first sentence (direct address)

- **Original:** "Some believers carry a thought they seldom say out loud. If I am struggling, it must be my sin or the devil, and if I go to a doctor I will be turning my back on God. A person who thinks this ..."
- **Revision:** "Perhaps you know the moment after a service when someone on the way out asks how you are, and you say "fine", because the true answer would feel like a confession. Underneath it may sit a thought that some believers carry and seldom say out loud: if I am struggling, it must be my sin or the devil, and if I go to a doctor I will be turning my back on God. A person who thinks this ..."
- **Why:** the opening stated the belief but had no moment. Ch06 ended on "a weight they carry alone". The new sentence puts that weight in a scene most churchgoers will recognise: the casual question after a service and the answer held back. "Would feel like a confession" points ahead to sin and guilt. The chapter's question (paragraph 2, sentence 1, unchanged: "The last chapter ended by asking what happens when the faith that holds a person up becomes that kind of weight ...") now comes at sentence four. I wrote "on the way out", not "at the door", so the sentence does not echo ch06's "first door". **Writer's sentence, for Eston's ear.**

### 2. New paragraph after line 44 (section 3, before the stigma definition): the blind man's parents, John 9:20-22, 9:34-35

- **Original:** the Adams paragraph ("... seeking professional care may be seen as undermining local, traditional, collective healing.") was followed by "Stigma is the name for what happens next. In my thesis I defined ..."
- **Revision:** a new paragraph (189 words), then "Stigma is the name for that fear. In my thesis I defined ...":
  "John's Gospel shows a family kept quiet by the judgement of its community. After the man born blind was given his sight, the authorities questioned his parents, and they would say only that he was their son and had been born blind: "he is of age; ask him: he shall speak for himself" (John 9:21). John gives the reason. The authorities "had agreed already, that if any man did confess that he was Christ, he should be put out of the synagogue" (John 9:22). Their fear was not about illness, and their son had just been healed. Even so, the threat of being put out of their congregation kept them from speaking of it. Their son spoke for himself and was told, "Thou wast altogether born in sins, and dost thou teach us?" Then "they cast him out" (John 9:34). The disciples' question had come back as a verdict. John adds that when Jesus heard they had cast him out, he found him (John 9:35). As I read it, a family today can fall silent about an illness out of the same fear of what the congregation will say."
- **Why:** section 3 was the chapter's most abstract stretch: pride, a "common African worldview", communal handling and a definition, with no one in it. The section's claim is that silence is kept for the community's sake, and John 9 shows it happening. The parents stay quiet because they fear their congregation's verdict, and the son, who speaks, is told his blindness was sin and is put out. That ties back to the disciples' question in section 1 (John 9:2). Jesus finding the man who was cast out answers it from the text, without promising anything about any real church. **Honesty guard:** "Their fear was not about illness" says plainly that 9:22 is about confessing Christ, so the scene stands for fear of a congregation's judgement and claims no more. **Wording:** in the narration I wrote "the authorities" for the KJV's "the Pharisees" (9:13-16) and "the Jews" (9:18, 22). The quoted spans begin after "the Jews", so the scene cannot read as a charge against a people; the author may prefer "the Pharisees". **Reworded writer's sentence:** "Stigma is the name for what happens next" became "Stigma is the name for that fear", so that "that" points to the scene's last words. **Writer's sentences, for Eston's ear:** the lead-in, "Their fear was not about illness ...", "Even so ...", "The disciples' question had come back as a verdict" and the last sentence (which rests on thesis 2678-2683 and 2717-2726).

### 3. New paragraph after line 52 (section 4, after "In extreme cases ... weak faith"): the father's cry, Mark 9:18, 9:22-25

- **Original:** the Akinbobola and Olorunnisola paragraph, ending "In extreme cases, I wrote, people may feel that seeking professional help shows weak faith, or a lack of trust in God's power to heal.", was followed by "In chapter 4 I said I would come back to the woman with the issue of blood."
- **Revision:** a new paragraph between them (159 words):
  "The father of the boy in Mark's Gospel came to Jesus after help had already failed him once. He had brought his son to the disciples, "and they could not" (Mark 9:18). By the time he reached Jesus his hope was down to an "if": "if thou canst do any thing, have compassion on us, and help us" (Mark 9:22). Jesus answered that all things are possible to the one who believes (Mark 9:23). "And straightway the father of the child cried out, and said with tears, Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief" (Mark 9:24). In front of a crowd he owned that his faith was not enough, and in the same breath he asked for help. Mark records no rebuke of that confession. The next thing he tells is Jesus turning to the boy (Mark 9:25). As I read it, the father's asking was part of his faith, and the Gospel does not count it against him."
- **Why:** the section's first paragraph is the most lecture-dense in the chapter (two flagged studies, "worldly" and "secular" counselling, "weak faith"), and it shows no one. The father is the same boy's father that section 2 already brings in (Matthew 17:15-18; Mark 9:29), so no new figure enters. He shows, from the text the deliverance readings rest on, that admitting weak faith and asking for help were not held against him. That answers the "weak faith" belief directly. The ch04 woman paragraph that follows answers it a second way, from her going to physicians. **Guards:** 9:23 is paraphrased, so that "all things are possible to him that believeth" cannot read as a promise that enough faith brings a cure (ch04's guard). The healing (9:25-27) is not told. "Mark records no rebuke" was checked against 9:19-27: the "O faithless generation" rebuke (9:19) comes before the father's cry, and 9:25's rebuke is of the spirit. Here it is told only as "turning to the boy" (config rule 5). The paragraph does not mention doctors, so it does not stretch the text. "I believe doubt of that kind can live inside faith" (spent in ch04, of Mark 15:34) is not echoed. **Writer's sentences, for Eston's ear:** the first sentence, "In front of a crowd ...", and the last.
