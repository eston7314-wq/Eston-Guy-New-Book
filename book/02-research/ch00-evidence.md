### 1.1 The WHO description of mental health (thesis 1172-1188)

**Thesis text (1172-1182), attributed to "WHO, 2010":** mental health is "a component of
behavioural health that includes a person's emotional, psychological and social well-being. It
affects how we think, feel and act ... how individuals manage stress, relate to others, and make
healthy choices ... a state of well-being that enables us to cope with the stresses of life while
realising our abilities ... learn, work and contribute to our social groups, families and
communities." Bibliography entry 129 (5466-5469): World Health Organisation (2010), "Mental
Health: Strengthening our Response", https://www.who.int/health-topics/mental-health-definition,
accessed 5 July 2024.

**Finding: corrected (attribution).** The thesis passage blends two bodies' wording and credits
it all to WHO.
- *The first half is CDC wording, not WHO.* Search of cdc.gov returned the CDC Mental Health
  Data Channel's definition: "Mental health is the component of behavioral health that includes
  our emotional, psychological, and social well-being."
  https://www.cdc.gov/mental-health/about-data/ . The "think, feel, and act ... handle stress,
  relate to others, and make healthy choices" sentences are the CDC's long-standing "About Mental
  Health" wording; the search did **not** return them from a current cdc.gov page (only from an
  archived 2012 CDC page, https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20121007032041/http:/www.cdc.gov/mentalhealth/basics.htm ,
  and a non-CDC site). Status of those two sentences on a current CDC page: **could not verify**.
- *The second half is WHO's.* The current WHO fact sheet "Mental health" (formerly titled "Mental
  health: strengthening our response"; search reports the page as updated 8 October 2025) defines
  mental health as a state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of
  life, realise their abilities, learn and work well, and contribute to their community.
  https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-strengthening-our-response
  Substance: **verified** (several searches agree). **Exact wording: could not verify.** Search
  results disagreed on "learn and work well" against "learn well and work well". Re-open the page
  before quoting.
- *The 2010 date.* The older WHO fact sheet No. 220 (https://www.who.int/mediacentre/factsheets/fs220/en/)
  used different wording ("a state of well-being in which an individual realizes his or her own
  abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and
  is able to make a contribution to his or her community"). That wording is reproduced in a copy of
  the old fact sheet, but the search could not confirm that it is the 2010 text. Search could not
  find the 2010 version. The bibliography URL (".../health-topics/mental-health-definition") did
  not appear in any result. **Could not verify** "WHO, 2010" as a source for any of the thesis
  wording.

**Does the thesis wording match WHO's current wording?** No. Only the "cope with the stresses of
life ... realise abilities ... contribute" part is WHO. The "emotional, psychological and social
well-being / think, feel and act" part is CDC.

**Safe ways to write it in the preface (pick one; author decides):**
1. Quote WHO's own definition only, once the exact wording is re-confirmed, cited "(World Health
   Organization 2025)" with the fact-sheet URL. Recommended.
2. Paraphrase both and name both: the WHO describes mental health as a state of well-being that
   lets us cope with life's stresses, use our abilities, learn and work, and play our part in our
   community; the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention adds that it takes in our
   emotional, psychological and social well-being. Cite WHO and CDC separately.
- **Book Bible fix needed.** The "Mental health" key-term row attributes "emotional,
  psychological and social well-being ... think, feel, act, cope and relate" to WHO. That is CDC
  wording. The row needs correcting (proposed in book-bible.md; owner: orchestrator).

### 1.2 "African heritage" and the Wittgenstein reference (thesis 66-78)

**Thesis:** "The Austrian philosopher, Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889) stated that the meaning of a word
is its use."

- **1889 is his birth year: verified.** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ("Ludwig
  Wittgenstein", substantive revision 20 October 2021) gives his birth as 26 April 1889, Vienna.
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/
- **The right work: identified, verified.** The idea is from *Philosophical Investigations*, §43,
  first published 1953 (posthumously; trans. G. E. M. Anscombe, Blackwell). The thesis itself
  cites the 2009 edition at line 890 ("Philosophical Investigations" (2009)): the fourth edition,
  revised by P. M. S. Hacker and Joachim Schulte, Wiley-Blackwell, 2009, ISBN 978-1-4051-5928-9
  (publisher listing: https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/philosophical-investigations-978-1-4051-5928-9 ;
  overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Investigations ). Neither edition is in
  the thesis bibliography (5000-5483).
- **Correct citation (Chicago author-date):** Wittgenstein, Ludwig. 2009. *Philosophical
  Investigations*. Rev. 4th ed. Translated by G. E. M. Anscombe, P. M. S. Hacker and Joachim
  Schulte. Edited by P. M. S. Hacker and Joachim Schulte. Chichester: Wiley-Blackwell. First
  published 1953. In text: "(Wittgenstein [1953] 2009, §43)".
- **The quoted words.** The clause "the meaning of a word is its use in the language" is the
  standard rendering (agreed across the sources found). **The thesis drops Wittgenstein's own
  qualifier.** §43 limits the claim to "a large class of cases ... though not for all". Exact
  full-sentence wording in either translation: **could not verify** (the book could not be
  opened). Safe preface wording: *The philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein suggested that, in many
  cases, the meaning of a word is its use.* That is accurate without needing the full quotation.
- **The definition itself** (66-78) is the author's stipulated usage, not an empirical claim. It
  needs no source. It stays as the author's definition (rule 5).
- Minor wording point for the drafter: 74-76 lists "Africa and the Caribbean" among "communities
  like the UK, Canada, USA"; the book's scope is the three countries (config Topic). This is for
  the author, not a correction.

## 2. Evidence for the preface's central argument

The preface argues that faith matters greatly to many people of African heritage, and that it can
both help and harm mental health. The tone rules keep evidence in the background, so the preface
needs at most one outside fact. **None of the items below could be opened** (fetch blocked), so all
are **could not verify** under rule 5. They are listed so that the chapter owners (ch01, ch05, ch06,
ch07) and a later pass with network access do not start from nothing.

### Studies (could not open; search-level details)

1. Smith, Timothy B., Michael E. McCullough and Justin Poll. 2003. "Religiousness and Depression:
   Evidence for a Main Effect and the Moderating Influence of Stressful Life Events."
   *Psychological Bulletin* 129 (4): 614-636. PubMed 12848223. Author PDF:
   https://pages.ucsd.edu/~memccullough/Papers/Relig_Depress_Psyc%20Bull.pdf .
   Finding as reported in search: 147 studies, N = 98,975; overall r = -.096 between
   religiousness and depressive symptoms; stronger under stressful life events; ethnicity did not
   moderate; extrinsic orientation and negative religious coping went with *more* depression.
   Use: the honest size of the "faith helps" effect (small but real) and the "faith can harm" side.
2. Ano, Gene G. and Erin B. Vasconcelles. 2005. "Religious Coping and Psychological Adjustment to
   Stress: A Meta-Analysis." *Journal of Clinical Psychology* 61 (4): 461-480.
   https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.20049 . Reported: 49 studies; positive religious coping linked
   with better adjustment, negative religious coping (e.g. feeling punished or abandoned by God)
   with worse. Effect sizes not obtained. Use: ch07's owner; the preface may allude, not cite.

### Synthesis

The preface's factual load is light, and most of it holds. The survey counts are exact. The help
lines, Pope Francis's death and the KJV text are confirmed. Two source items are weaker than the
outline assumes. First, the "WHO description" is not WHO's: about half of it is CDC wording, and
the "2010" source cannot be traced. The definition can still be used, but it needs re-attribution,
and the Book Bible row inherits the error. Second, "Wittgenstein (1889)" is a birth year standing
in for a 1953 book, and the thesis drops his own "though not for all". Neither problem threatens the
argument; both would embarrass the author in print if left. On the survey, the two recommended
numbers are safe if worded as self-report from 50 people. 38 of 50 is a statement of belief about
faith, not evidence that faith works.

### Strongest counter-evidence

- **The effect of religiousness on depression is small.** Smith, McCullough and Poll (2003)
  report r of about -.10 across 147 studies: about 1% of variance. Faith is associated with fewer
  depressive symptoms, modestly, and some forms of religiousness (negative religious coping,
  extrinsic orientation) go with more. A preface that implies faith is a strong protector would
  overclaim. The both/and framing is the right one; "Thy faith hath made thee whole" should stay
  scripture the author treasures, not a claim about outcomes.
- **The survey cannot carry weight.** 50 self-selected, mostly churchgoing people; no age data; no
  unemployed or retired participants; the association claim (4658-4663) has no reported
  statistics (open-claims row 5). 38 of 50 believing that faith helps is what one would expect
  from a sample recruited through faith communities.
- **Negative religious coping** (Ano and Vasconcelles 2005) is the published basis for the
  "sometimes harm" half of the book. The preface promise "honest about where faith harms" should
  be kept in ch07 with that literature, once opened.
