## 4. Studies: the mental-health legacy (all LEADS, not opened)

None of these was opened (hosts blocked). Citations and headline findings come from index or abstract records in search results. Mode B must open each.

**S1. Racism harms mental health (meta-analysis).**
Paradies, [first name], et al. [full author list to be taken from the paper]. 2015. "Racism as a Determinant of Health: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *PLoS ONE* 10 (9): e0138511. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0138511
- Plain English: across 293 studies (333 articles, 1983-2013, mostly US), people who report racism have worse mental health. The link is clearest for depression, anxiety and stress.
- The number: r = -0.23 for negative mental health (227 studies).
- Caution: the effect is stronger in cross-sectional than longitudinal data, and in non-representative samples. Only "Paradies et al." is confirmed by search; take the full author list from the paper.
- Status: **Lead.**

**S2. Racism in the UK, measured over time.**
Wallace, [first name], [first name] Nazroo, and Laia Bécares. 2016. "Cumulative Effect of Racial Discrimination on the Mental Health of Ethnic Minorities in the United Kingdom." *American Journal of Public Health* 106 (7): 1294-1300. https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2016.303121 (PMC4984732)
- Plain English: in the UK Household Longitudinal Study (2009-2013), people who met discrimination repeatedly, in more than one part of life, had worse mental health years later, after allowing for earlier distress.
- The number: 8.26 points lower on the SF-12 mental score for two or more domains at two time points (search-level; one record gives 1.93 for a single time point). **Check in the paper.**
- Why it matters: the chapter's UK readers. It shows present-day racism as a measurable stressor, not only history.
- Status: **Lead.**

**S3. Historical trauma as an idea, honestly described.**
Mohatt, Nathaniel Vincent, [first name] Thompson, [first name] Thai, and [first name] Tebes. 2014. "Historical Trauma as Public Narrative: A Conceptual Review of How History Impacts Present-Day Health." *Social Science & Medicine* 106: 128-136. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2014.01.043 (PMC4001826)
- Plain English: historical trauma works through the stories a community tells about its past. Those stories link today's hardships to the old wound, and they can feed both distress and resilience.
- Why it matters: this is the most honest frame for argument 4. It lets the chapter say that the memory of slavery shapes health without claiming a proven biological inheritance.
- Status: **Lead.**

**S4. Intergenerational trauma: what biology has and has not shown.**
Yehuda, Rachel, and Amy Lehrner. 2018. "Intergenerational Transmission of Trauma Effects: Putative Role of Epigenetic Mechanisms." *World Psychiatry* 17 (3): 243-257. https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20568 (PMC6127768)
- Plain English: in animals, the effects of adversity can pass to offspring. In humans, studies "have not yet demonstrated" that trauma effects are inherited through epigenetic (non-genetic) mechanisms. The authors warn that "transmission" has been used loosely.
- Why it matters: both evidence and counter-evidence. Trauma clearly passes through families by upbringing, poverty and story. A biological "inherited trauma" is not established.
- Status: **Lead.**

**S5. Does religion buffer racism? (Black Church as refuge)**
Ellison, C. G., M. A. Musick, and A. K. Henderson. 2008. "Balm in Gilead: Racism, Religious Involvement, and Psychological Distress among African-American Adults." *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion* 47 (2): 291-309. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-5906.2008.00408.x
- Citation verified (search-level). **Findings not seen**: no abstract came back in two searches. Do not describe its result until it is opened.
- Companion: Bierman, Alex. 2006. "Does Religion Buffer the Effects of Discrimination on Mental Health? Differing Effects by Race." *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion* 45 (4): 551-565. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-5906.2006.00327.x. Abstract (search-level): discrimination goes with more negative and less positive feeling, and **only attendance at religious services** moderated that link; the protective effect differed by race (direction not seen).
- A later review by Ellison and colleagues calls the buffering findings "somewhat discrepant" (search-level).
- Status: **Leads.**

**S6. The church as refuge, with a warning in the data.**
Chatters, Linda M., Robert Joseph Taylor, Karen D. Lincoln, Ann Nguyen, and Sean Joe. 2011. "Church-Based Social Support and Suicidality among African Americans and Black Caribbeans." *Archives of Suicide Research* 15 (4): 337-353. https://doi.org/10.1080/13811118.2011.615703 (full text: University of Michigan Deep Blue)
- Plain English: in the National Survey of American Life, feeling **close to church members** went with lower odds of suicidal thoughts. But more frequent contact went with **higher** odds of suicide attempts, and emotional support and attendance showed no link.
- Why it matters: belonging, not mere attendance, seems to be what helps. Suicide material: Samaritans guidelines and a help line if this reaches ch01; it may sit better in ch06 or ch09.
- Status: **Lead.** (Different paper from Chatters et al. 2008, used in ch04.)

**S7. The "paradox" (main counter-evidence; see section 9).**
Williams, [first name], [first name] González, [first name] Neighbors, et al. 2007. "Prevalence and Distribution of Major Depressive Disorder in African Americans, Caribbean Blacks, and Non-Hispanic Whites." [Journal, volume, pages and DOI to be taken from the paper: search showed only a March 2007 publication.] Repository record: https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/22657 ; NIMH science update, 2007, https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/science-updates/2007/african-americans-black-caribbeans-and-whites-differ-in-depression-risk-treatment
- Plain English: lifetime depression was **lower** in African Americans (10.4%) and Black Caribbeans (12.9%) than in White Americans (17.9%), though 12-month rates were similar. When Black Americans were depressed, it was more often chronic (about 56% vs 38.6%), more severe, and less often treated (45% of African Americans and 24.3% of Black Caribbeans with depression got any treatment).
- N = 6,082 (3,570 African American, 1,621 Caribbean Black, 891 White).
- Venue and full author list deliberately left blank: they did not appear in search results and are not cited from memory.
- Status: **Lead.**

## 7. Case candidates (real, documented)

The opening slot stays `[AUTHOR STORY NEEDED: how Christianity came into your own family line ...]`, as the blueprint says. These are for the documented voice that follows it.

**Recommended: Mary Prince (born about 1788: the 1831 editor says she is "about forty-three"), Bermuda, Antigua and London.** Verified (opened), with search-level context.
- Born at Brackish-Pond, Bermuda; enslaved in Bermuda, on Turks Island and in Antigua (her text).
- At a Methodist prayer meeting at Christmas, "the first prayers I ever understood", she heard a Black driver named Henry ask forgiveness for the beatings his master made him give. That "led my spirit to the Moravian church". Moravian women "taught me to read in the class". She was baptised in the English Church in August 1817 by the Rev. Mr Curtin, who would not let her attend his Sunday school without her owner's written permission. She married Daniel James, a free carpenter, about Christmas 1826 in the Moravian chapel at Spring Gardens, because "English marriage is not allowed to slaves" (her text).
- On Turks Island, enslaved people "built up a place with boughs and leaves, where they might meet for prayers, but the white people pulled it down twice" (her text). That is a Caribbean picture of the "invisible institution".
- Brought to London, she was turned out by her owners. The only people she knew in England were the Moravian missionaries at Hatton Garden, who took in her trunk (her text).
- Her closing words carry the mental cost plainly: "I endeavour to keep down my fretting, and to leave all to Him ... Yet, I must confess, I find it a hard and heavy task to do so." Her pastor told her to pray "and the truth will make me free" (John 8:32).
- Context (search-level): the first account of a Black woman's life published in Britain; dictated to Susanna Strickland (later Susanna Moodie, who emigrated to Canada in 1832); a bestseller for the abolition campaign (London Museum, https://londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/the-life-story-of-mary-prince ; Wikipedia).
- Why her: she joins the Moravian thread the source already has (3836-3853), the UK, the Caribbean and a Canadian link. She also shows the faith as both refuge (the Moravians who taught and sheltered her) and gatekeeping (the clergyman who wanted her owner's note). The text is public domain. Caution: Pringle edited her words, and scholars debate how much (search-level).

**Second: Frederick Douglass (born 1818), Maryland and Britain.** Verified (opened). Already named in the source (4026-4027). Use Q1-Q3. After the *Narrative* appeared he went to Great Britain, fearing re-enslavement (Britannica, search-level), which gives UK readers a link. Risk: he is the most familiar voice, so he gives the least surprise.

**Third: Samuel Sharpe (c. 1801-1832), Jamaica.** Verified (search-level). Baptist deacon in Montego Bay; led the 1831-32 Christmas Rebellion (the "Baptist War"), which began as a strike on 25 December 1831; hanged at Montego Bay on 23 May 1832; a National Hero of Jamaica (1975 or 1982, sources differ). His reported words, "I would rather die upon yonder gallows than live in slavery", come from the Methodist missionary Henry Bleby, who visited him in his cell (*Death Struggles of Slavery*, London, 1853; 3rd ed. 1868; pp. 116-17 per a Baptist source). **Wording varies across sources and Bleby was not opened**: open-claims 34 if quoted. Sources: Baptists Together, https://baptist.org.uk/Groups/379663/About_Sam_Sharpe.aspx ; *The Gazette*, https://thegazette.co.uk/all-notices/content/103864 ; NYPL digital collections record for Bleby. Why him: a Christian leader whose reading of scripture led to resistance, and a link to British Baptists. Overlap risk: none recorded in the Book Bible.

**Not recommended for ch01:** Richard Allen's walkout. It is a strong scene, but the date is disputed and I could not open his text. It suits a short paragraph in the Black Church section without quotation.

## 8. Synthesis

The chapter's history rests on firm ground. Missions came with empire. The "obey your masters" verses (Ephesians 6:5, Colossians 3:22, Titus 2:9) were preached to enslaved people. In 1807 a London-printed Bible for the West Indies cut almost all of Exodus. Enslaved Christians answered with a counter-reading, and two of them can say so in their own published words: Douglass's "Christianity of this land" against "the Christianity of Christ", and Mary Prince's "to be free is very sweet". The Black Church's origins are just as well documented: Raboteau on the "invisible institution", Lincoln and Mamiya, and Allen and the AME Church in 1816. Argument 1, argument 2 and argument 5 can all be written with confidence once the slips in section 1 are fixed.

**Where the evidence is weaker than the outline assumes:**
1. **Argument 4, the mental cost.** The thesis's own sentences are unsourced: PTSD among some individuals (3674-3677), "most enslaved Africans noted" the repression of their coping (3954-3956), and the Civil Rights Acts "resulting in improved mental health" (1578-1583). The research supports a narrower claim. Present-day racism is a measurable mental-health stressor, in the US (Paradies 2015) and in the UK (Wallace 2016). The past works on health through families, poverty and the story a community tells (Mohatt 2014). A proven biological inheritance of trauma is **not** established in humans (Yehuda and Lehrner 2018). The chapter should say "the wound is carried in memory, story and present-day racism" and avoid "trauma is in our genes".
2. **The Black Church as refuge** is well supported as history. As a measured mental-health effect, the evidence is mixed. Buffering findings are "discrepant". Belonging to a church seems to protect more than simply attending one (Chatters et al. 2011). Say "many found refuge" rather than "the church protected their mental health".
3. **The "mission differed" argument (3).** The Moravian part is real, but the source over-praises it. The slave-labour story is a myth, the 13,000 figure is unconfirmed, and the thesis itself says the Moravians "did not challenge the institution of slavery". Write them as spiritual equality within slavery, which is what the blueprint already says.
4. **Liberation theology.** Cone's Black theology is contemporary with, not derived from, Latin American liberation theology. Several of the source's supporting citations (Hintzen 2002, Graham 2010, West 2004, Collins and Smith, Clarke and Thompson) cannot be traced.

## 9. Strongest counter-evidence

1. **The Black-White depression paradox (Williams et al. 2007; lead).** If slavery's legacy simply damaged Black mental health, Black Americans should show more depression. Lifetime depression is in fact **lower** among African Americans (10.4%) and Black Caribbeans (12.9%) than among White Americans (17.9%). What is worse is chronicity, severity and lack of treatment. A sceptical reader can use this against any chapter that implies "our people are more depressed because of slavery". The honest answer, and one the book can own: resilience is real (and faith may be part of it), and the harm shows up in how long illness lasts and how rarely it is treated.
2. **No proven biological inheritance (Yehuda and Lehrner 2018; lead).** The popular idea that slavery's trauma is passed down in the body is not shown in human studies. Joy DeGruy's "Post Traumatic Slave Syndrome" is widely cited but is criticised for lacking empirical support, including by Ibram X. Kendi. A response by Guy Emerson Mount is at AAIHS *Black Perspectives*, https://www.aaihs.org/are-trumps-black-delegates-suffering-from-post-traumatic-slave-syndrome/ (search-level; the Kendi passage was not opened). If ch01 uses the PTSS term, it must be framed as a contested theory.
3. **Christianity as the enslavers' religion (Fanon; Douglass).** Fanon's critique, that the colonial Church called people to the master's ways, is the strongest form of the view the chapter's "shift" sets out to answer. The chapter should state it fairly, not cite Fanon for the opposite.
4. **The "Slave Bible" story may be overstated** (Hicks-Keeton 2020, *The Revealer*). Use it as one striking artefact, not as proof of a general policy.
5. **Church involvement is not uniformly protective** (Chatters et al. 2011; Bierman 2006).
