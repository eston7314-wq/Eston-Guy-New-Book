## Change log (original / revision / why)

### 1. Opening, paragraph 1: start on the scene at Calvary

- **Original:** "The songs in the last chapter named Jesus as their teacher, and they left a question: what
  did he teach that could hold a troubled mind together? When a scribe asked him which commandment came
  first, he gave two answers. ..."
- **Revision:** "At the place called Calvary, Jesus prayed for the people who had crucified him: "Father,
  forgive them; for they know not what they do." In the same verse Luke adds, "And they parted his raiment,
  and cast lots" (Luke 23:33-34). The songs in the last chapter named Jesus as their teacher, and they left a
  question: what did he teach that could hold a troubled mind together? Part of the answer is in that
  prayer, and it is the hardest part to live. When a scribe asked him which commandment came first, he gave
  two answers. ..." (the rest of the paragraph and its comment unchanged)
- **Why:** the old opening summarised the last chapter, and its first scene did not arrive until sentence
  two. Calvary is the strongest real scene in the chapter, with a person, a moment and a stake, and it is
  the hardest case of what the chapter teaches: forgiving people while they are still doing the harm.
  Paragraph 2 ("For people of African heritage, that is a great deal to ask") now answers it directly.
  Ch02's closing question stays, word for word, as sentence three, and the evidence pack advises opening
  "on the hardness of loving an enemy" (section 10). "who had crucified him" follows 23:33 ("there they
  crucified him"). Luke's raiment clause is now quoted in full in KJV form ("And they parted ...") with
  the reference 23:33-34, because 23:33 supplies the place. "Part of the answer is in that prayer, and it
  is the hardest part to live" is my joining sentence; it needs the author's ear. Question 1 for small
  groups and the closer's Lord's Prayer exercise now return to the opening.

### 2. Section 1, "Yet Jesus's teachings call for..." paragraph: the Calvary quotation moved, not cut

- **Original:** "At the place called Calvary he prayed, "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they
  do" (Luke 23:34). In the same verse, the Gospel adds, "they parted his raiment, and cast lots." The people
  he prayed for went on dividing his clothes while he forgave them."
- **Revision:** "That is the prayer at Calvary with which this chapter opened. The people he prayed for went
  on dividing his clothes while he forgave them."
- **Why:** after change 1, quoting the same verse twice in three paragraphs would repeat it. The quotation
  and its reference are now in the opening, so nothing is lost. The author's sentences (thesis 1877-1881),
  the writer's reading of the verse's order and the existing comment (which cites thesis 1881-1882 and the
  KJV correction) all stay in place. A note records the move.

### 3. Section 1, last paragraph: the woman at the well, placed after the gloss of "internalised stereotypes"

- **Original:** "By internalised stereotypes I mean the low opinion of us that racism can plant inside us,
  until part of us believes it. Love that does not depend on what we have done, or on the colour of our skin,
  speaks against that voice."
- **Revision:** both sentences kept. Between them: "John's Gospel has a scene where you can hear that voice.
  At Jacob's well in Samaria, about the sixth hour, Jesus sat down, "wearied with his journey". When a woman
  came to draw water, he said to her, "Give me to drink." She answered with a question: "How is it that thou,
  being a Jew, askest drink of me, which am a woman of Samaria?" (John 4:6-9). He had not refused her, yet
  she spoke the refusal for him. Jesus did not take up the old line between their peoples. He told her that
  if she had known who was asking, she would have asked him, "and he would have given thee living water"
  (John 4:10)."
- **Why:** this was the most abstract stretch in the chapter. The author's belief that unconditional love
  can "reduce internalised negative stereotypes" (thesis 1885-1888) was stated and glossed but never shown.
  In the KJV scene the woman says the line drawn against her people herself, before any refusal comes, and
  Jesus answers with an offer. That leads straight into the draft's next sentence. It is a Gospel scene, as
  the author prefers, and it is 107 words (under the 200-word limit for an illustration). No other chapter
  uses John 4 (Book Bible, blueprint v2 and 05-final searched), and ch03 already owns John 4:9. No
  mental-health claim is added. Two sentences are my reading, not quotation, and need the author's ear: "He
  had not refused her, yet she spoke the refusal for him" and "Jesus did not take up the old line between
  their peoples".

### 4. Section 3, the John 4:9 sentence: call back the woman at the well

- **Original:** "John's Gospel says it in a single line: "for the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans"
  (John 4:9)."
- **Revision:** "John's Gospel gives the reason for the woman's surprise at the well, in the same verse: "for
  the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans" (John 4:9)."
- **Why:** after change 3 the verse comes back here, and the callback ties the two Samaritan scenes
  together: the man who stopped and the woman who expected to be refused. The quotation, the reference and
  the comment are unchanged.

### 5. Section 2, the forgiveness belief paragraph: one sentence of direct address in front of it

- **Original:** the paragraph opened "I believe that practising forgiveness can help alleviate feelings of
  anger, resentment and bitterness that are detrimental to mental health."
- **Revision:** the same paragraph, with this sentence in front: "You may know the weight this teaching asks
  you to put down: a wrong done to you years ago that still comes back at night, word for word, with the
  anger as fresh as the first time."
- **Why:** this was the second-most abstract stretch: three sentences of the author's belief about
  forgiveness, with nothing the reader can see. One sentence of direct address gives "anger, resentment and
  bitterness" a moment the reader recognises, and it sets up the draft's "a deep wound can take a long time
  to forgive". It invents no person and states no fact ("You may know ..."). The author's sentences (thesis
  1894-1901) and their comments are unchanged, and the guardrail paragraph follows soon after.
