### P1. Opening: start on the scene at Calvary (hook in two sentences; the question at sentence three)

- **Where:** chapter opening, paragraph 1 (draft line 3), in front of the current first sentence. The
  existing comment stays at the end of the paragraph.
- **Current:** "The songs in the last chapter named Jesus as their teacher, and they left a question:
  what did he teach that could hold a troubled mind together? When a scribe asked him which commandment
  came first, he gave two answers. Love God with everything you have, and love your neighbour as
  yourself. Then he went further and told his followers to love their enemies."
- **Proposed:** "At the place called Calvary, Jesus prayed for the people who had crucified him:
  "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do." In the same verse Luke adds, "And they parted
  his raiment, and cast lots" (Luke 23:33-34). The songs in the last chapter named Jesus as their
  teacher, and they left a question: what did he teach that could hold a troubled mind together? Part of
  the answer is in that prayer, and it is the hardest part to live. When a scribe asked him which
  commandment came first, he gave two answers. Love God with everything you have, and love your
  neighbour as yourself. Then he went further and told his followers to love their enemies."

### P3. Section 1: the woman at the well, to show "internalised stereotypes" instead of only defining them

- **Where:** section 1, last paragraph (draft line 19). It goes after "By internalised stereotypes I mean
  the low opinion of us that racism can plant inside us, until part of us believes it." and before "Love
  that does not depend on what we have done, or on the colour of our skin, speaks against that voice."
  Both sentences and all comments stay.
- **Proposed:** "John's Gospel has a scene where you can hear that voice. At Jacob's well in Samaria,
  about the sixth hour, Jesus sat down, "wearied with his journey". When a woman came to draw water, he
  said to her, "Give me to drink." She answered with a question: "How is it that thou, being a Jew,
  askest drink of me, which am a woman of Samaria?" (John 4:6-9). He had not refused her, yet she spoke
  the refusal for him. Jesus did not take up the old line between their peoples. He told her that if she
  had known who was asking, she would have asked him, "and he would have given thee living water"
  (John 4:10)."
  (New comment: `<!-- note: story pass: John 4:6-10 KJV (4:9 in ch03-evidence.md section 3; 4:6-7, 4:10 read in the GITenberg mirror of Gutenberg #10, 2026-11-15). Scripture scene, not a case. "He had not refused her, yet she spoke the refusal for him" is the writer's reading -->`)
- **Why:** this is the most abstract stretch in the chapter. The author's belief that unconditional love
  can "reduce internalised negative stereotypes" (thesis 1885-1888) is stated, then glossed, but never
  shown. The KJV scene shows it: before Jesus has refused her anything, the woman says the line drawn
  against her people herself, and he answers with an offer. That leads straight into the draft's next
  sentence ("Love that does not depend ... speaks against that voice"). It is a Gospel scene, as the
  author prefers. It also plants John 4:9, which section 3 already quotes. No other chapter uses John 4
  (Book Bible; blueprint v2; 05-final searched), and ch03 already owns John 4:9. Two sentences are
  readings, not quotations, and need the author's ear: "He had not refused her, yet she spoke the
  refusal for him" and "Jesus did not take up the old line between their peoples." No claim about
  mental health is added.
- **Words:** +107 (under the 200-word limit for an illustration).

### P5. Section 2: turn the lecture on forgiveness into direct address (the reader's own moment)

- **Where:** section 2, the paragraph that begins "I believe that practising forgiveness can help
  alleviate feelings of anger" (draft line 25). One sentence goes in front of it. The author's sentences
  (thesis 1894-1901) and their comments stay as they are.
- **Proposed:** "You may know the weight this teaching asks you to put down: a wrong done to you years
  ago that still comes back at night, word for word, with the anger as fresh as the first time."
- **Why:** this paragraph is the second-most abstract stretch: three sentences of what forgiveness
  can do, with nothing the reader can see. One sentence of direct address gives the reader their own
  recognisable moment, so that "anger, resentment and bitterness" has something to attach to. It
  invents no person and claims no fact ("You may know..."). It also sets up the draft's own "a deep wound
  can take a long time to forgive". The guardrail paragraph follows soon after and is unchanged.
- **Words:** +36.
