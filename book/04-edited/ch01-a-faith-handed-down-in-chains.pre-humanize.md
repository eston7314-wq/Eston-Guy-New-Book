# A Faith Handed Down in Chains

The question I left at the end of the preface begins, for me, in my own family. Christianity came into my family line from my paternal grandparents who embraced Christianity through the Moravian Church in the Caribbean island of Tobago, while my maternal grandparents followed Christianity through the Anglican Church. My wife, children and I also embraced the Church of England practices while paying homage also to the Pentecostal and Spiritual Baptist practices. <!-- author-confirmed 2026-11-11 -->

Two of those churches, the Anglican and the Moravian, were carried to the Caribbean from Europe, and when I follow them back far enough I come to the plantations and to the ships that brought our ancestors there. What did our ancestors do with a faith that the people who enslaved them also claimed?

The answer has two edges. The faith was used against our ancestors, and they took it back and made it their own. I call this the double-edged inheritance, and I believe that holding both truths at once is part of being well. Many of us were raised on only one half of the story. Some were told that Christianity was the slave master's religion and can only hurt us. Others were told that our faith has nothing to do with that history at all. The record shows something more complicated, and more hopeful, than either.

## The faith arrived in the company of empire

Christian mission moved with European empire from the sixteenth century, and in the British colonies from the seventeenth century until emancipation in 1834. Britain built plantations across the Caribbean and North America, and missionaries followed, building schools, hospitals and churches. Those were real goods. They were also part of an enterprise that ran on stolen labour. The British colonies grew rich on tobacco, rice and cotton, and the plantations that grew them demanded an ever larger workforce.

Disease and violence had killed many of the indigenous peoples of the Caribbean and the Americas, so the empire turned to Africa for people to enslave. About 12.5 million Africans were put on ships for the Americas, and about 10.7 million arrived. Most of the difference is the people who died at sea.<!-- note: Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, SlaveVoyages, "Estimates", https://www.slavevoyages.org/assessment/estimates (modelled estimates; search-level, ch01-evidence.md T1) --> Missionaries came close behind, sent to "save" the souls of the enslaved and of the indigenous people who had survived, the Arawaks and Caribs in the Caribbean and the Cree, Iroquois and Ojibwa in Canada.

Many of those missionaries took their charge from the last words of Jesus in Matthew's Gospel: "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world" (Matthew 28:19-20, KJV). Christians call this the Great Commission. In the hands of empire it became a duty to convert and "civilise" people of African heritage, who were seen as heathen and primitive.

Other verses were put to darker use. "Servants, be obedient to them that are your masters according to the flesh, with fear and trembling, in singleness of your heart, as unto Christ" (Ephesians 6:5, KJV). The same instruction appears in two other letters (Colossians 3:22, KJV; Titus 2:9, KJV). Preached to enslaved people, these verses taught that obeying an earthly master was part of God's ordained order, and so Christianity was made to justify the enslaving of Africans.

Frederick Douglass saw what that looked like in practice. He was born into slavery in Maryland in 1818, escaped in 1838 and, after his *Narrative* appeared in 1845, lectured in Britain and Ireland.<!-- note: Library of Congress Douglass timeline (search-level) --> In the *Narrative* he tells how his master, Thomas Auld, came home from a Methodist camp meeting in 1832 a converted man, and in Douglass's judgement a worse one, because he now had religious sanction for his cruelty. Douglass watched him tie up a lame young woman and whip her until the blood ran, then quote scripture to justify it: "He that knoweth his master's will, and doeth it not, shall be beaten with many stripes."<!-- note: Douglass 1845, Narrative, ch. IX (Project Gutenberg #23, opened; conversion at the August 1832 camp meeting and "religious sanction" re-read in the same file by story-injector 2026-11-11, paraphrased). Douglass's own wording of his master's words; compare Luke 12:47, KJV --> The words are close to a saying of Jesus in Luke's Gospel, turned into a licence for cruelty.

The same religious community could not bear to see enslaved people read for themselves. A young white man named Wilson started a Sabbath school for slaves who wanted "to learn to read the New Testament". They met only three times before two class-leaders of the church and others came upon them "with sticks and other missiles" and drove them off. Douglass came to a hard conclusion: "of all slaveholders with whom I have ever met, religious slaveholders are the worst."<!-- note: Douglass 1845, chs. IX and X (Project Gutenberg #23, opened) -->

Canada had its own part in this history. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the British and French colonial powers spread Catholicism and Anglicanism to strengthen their hold over land and people. Slavery came too, first in the Atlantic regions, where enslaved Africans worked on farms, in homes and in trades. Missionaries, often with the colonial authorities' approval, set out to convert them as a moral and civilising duty, believing their lives would improve if they learned Western ideas of "order" and "obedience". In Nova Scotia and Quebec, enslaved Africans were openly treated as inferior, and that racism added to their anguish. When Britain's Slavery Abolition Act came into force on 1 August 1834, it freed fewer than 50 people in British North America. In 2021 Canada's House of Commons named 1 August Emancipation Day.<!-- note: UK National Archives; Canadian Encyclopedia; canada.ca Emancipation Day (search-level, ch01-evidence.md section 2) -->

In the twentieth century the writer Frantz Fanon, in *The Wretched of the Earth*, argued that the colonial Church called the colonised to the ways of the master and the oppressor.<!-- note: Fanon, Les Damnés de la terre (Paris: Maspero, 1961); English trans. Constance Farrington (New York: Grove Press, 1963). Gist only; wording not verified, do not quote (ch01-evidence.md F4; open-claims 25) --> If you have heard it said that Christianity can only hurt us, this is that view at its strongest, and it deserves a hearing. Missionary work did press Western customs on the people it reached, and much of their own culture and belief was worn away in the process. Any honest account of our faith has to begin there.

## Our ancestors took the book back

Enslaved Africans and their descendants heard the same sermons and drew something else from them. They took the scriptures that were meant to keep them subdued and found in them reasons to resist, as a people, and to hope for liberation.

The story of the Exodus, though rooted in the Old Testament, spoke to them as the story of God bringing a people out of bondage. In it God says, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; for I know their sorrows" (Exodus 3:7, KJV). Then Moses carries God's demand to Pharaoh: "Let my people go" (Exodus 5:1, KJV). A people in chains did not need anyone to explain what that story meant. Chapter 2 tells how they turned it into song.

The New Testament gave them another verse to hold up against the master's verses. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, KJV). In the King James wording, "bond" means a slave. Here was Paul putting the slave and the free side by side, as equals in Christ. Black Christians have often invoked this verse to challenge racial inequality and to affirm the dignity of every person.

The teaching of Jesus about the Kingdom of God gave them a whole story to set against the plantation. The Kingdom promised equality and justice, and the plantation divided people into owners and property. If God's Kingdom had no such order, then the plantation was not God's will, whatever the preacher said on Sunday.

Our ancestors also carried their African spiritual heritage into Christian worship. As I read this history, across the Caribbean, the USA and Canada, many African rituals were woven into baptisms, prayers and hymns. Drums and other instruments could carry messages the masters could not read. These blended forms of faith kept a cultural identity alive under a system designed to erase it.

Douglass worried that his attacks on the religion of the slaveholders might be taken for an attack on all religion, so he added an appendix to the *Narrative*: "between the Christianity of this land, and the Christianity of Christ, I recognize the widest possible difference." He went on: "I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ."<!-- note: Douglass 1845, Narrative, Appendix (Project Gutenberg #23, opened). "recognize" is his US spelling. ## Missions differed, and even the Moravians worked within slavery

Anglicans, Catholics and Baptists provided education and basic health care to people who already had their own ways of treating illness, particularly with herbs. Yet their work was bound up with colonial interests. Religion propped up the social order, and British missions in the Caribbean served the empire's economy as much as they served God.

On the American mainland, most missions worked among Native American peoples. Spanish Franciscan friars entered New Mexico with an expedition in 1581, and Junípero Serra later founded nine missions in California, beginning with San Diego in 1769. They converted indigenous people, sometimes by force, and enslaved Africans brought by the Spanish laboured alongside them in missions from Florida to California. The Puritans, Protestants who wanted to purify the Church of England of Roman Catholic practices, came to New England in the early 1600s hoping to build "a city on a hill". They set up "praying towns" for Native Americans and kept enslaved Africans in their homes and on their farms. Methodists and Presbyterians reached Oregon in the 1830s.<!-- note: ch01-evidence.md E4 (California 1769, not 1541) and section 2, Oregon (search-level). Dev edit: Serra's nine missions 1769-1782; Methodists, Willamette Valley 1834; Presbyterians 1836; compressed in prose, cut-list row 36 --> Conversion, for most of these missions, was about conformity as well as salvation.

The Moravians were different in one important way. They were a small Protestant church renewed at Herrnhut in Germany under Count Zinzendorf, and they became the first large-scale Protestant missionary movement to send unordained lay people to enslaved communities in the Caribbean. Two young Moravians, Johann Leonhard Dober, a potter, and David Nitschmann, a carpenter, landed on St Thomas on 13 December 1732.<!-- note: thesis 3843 says "1772", which is Bishop Nitschmann's death year; corrected per ch01-evidence.md F1 (search-level). The "sold themselves into slavery" story is not repeated (E1) --> Moravian congregations later grew on other islands, and Tobago was among them [UNVERIFIED: Moravian mission in Tobago, its date and extent; thesis 3846 lists Tobago, but the evidence pack does not verify it]. My paternal grandparents came to Christianity through that church.

The Moravians believed in the spiritual equality of all people, whatever their race or social status. Unlike most other missionaries, they gave religious education to those they baptised. In the 1700s they also reached out to indigenous peoples and enslaved Africans in Pennsylvania, the Carolinas and Georgia. Yet they did not challenge the institution of slavery itself. They worked within the system, seeking to save souls, rather than to liberate bodies. Some saw their work as a way of keeping enslaved people quiet, with a Christianity that taught obedience and submission.

One woman's life shows both sides of that church. Mary Prince was born into slavery in Bermuda around 1788 and was later enslaved on Turks Island and in Antigua. In Antigua one Christmas, an enslaved woman took her to a Methodist prayer meeting at her husband's house on a plantation called Winthorps, and there Mary heard what she called "the first prayers I ever understood". The meeting, she said, "led my spirit to the Moravian church". She did not tell her mistress, knowing she would be refused leave; when she carried the children's lunch to school, she ran round to hear the teachers. The Moravian women there "taught me to read in the class".

The English Church was more guarded. It had baptised her in 1817, but the clergyman would not let her attend his Sunday school without her owner's written permission. When she married Daniel James, a free carpenter, about Christmas 1826, the wedding took place in the Moravian chapel at Spring Gardens, because, as she put it, "English marriage is not allowed to slaves."

Her owners later brought her to London, where she was forced out of their house. The only people she knew in England were the Moravian missionaries at Hatton Garden, and they took in her trunk. Her *History*, published in 1831, was the first account of a Black woman's life published in Britain. She dictated it to Susanna Strickland, who emigrated to Canada the next year, and it was edited by Thomas Pringle, whose hand on her words scholars still debate.<!-- note: Prince 1831, The History of Mary Prince (Project Gutenberg #17851, opened); context London Museum and Wikipedia (search-level), ch01-evidence.md section 7 --> The Moravians taught her, married her and stood by her in London, and all the while the law kept her enslaved.

## The mind paid a price, and the wound outlived slavery

For some enslaved Africans, Christianity was a way to win a little autonomy or some escape from the harshness of their lives. For others it was a tool of control that pacified them and suppressed their desire for freedom. They were told of an all-powerful, loving God while they lived through the harshest human injustice, and that gap could produce spiritual alienation, guilt and mental anguish.

Go back to that Christmas prayer meeting at Winthorps. The host was Henry, a Black driver and the husband of the woman who had brought Mary Prince. After the prayers and hymns, each person spoke in turn of their sorrow over their own sins. Henry confessed that he had been very cruel to the slaves, and said his master's orders compelled him. A driver, he said, sometimes had to beat his own wife or sister if his master told him to. He asked them all to forgive him, and he prayed that God would forgive him. Mary cried the whole night and was too ashamed to speak.<!-- note: Prince 1831, History (Project Gutenberg #17851, opened; passage re-read in the same file by story-injector 2026-11-11). Paraphrased; only "the first prayers I ever understood" is quoted, in section 3. Prince's word for a driver is "ranger" --> In one man you can see the guilt and the anguish: he had done what his master ordered, and he brought the sin of it to God as his own.

Missionaries spoke of a caring afterlife as the reward for suffering, and said little about the trauma of enslavement itself, which often left people of African heritage feeling helpless and hopeless. I believe that, for many, forced conversion also pushed down the African ways of coping they had brought with them: their rituals, their music and their understanding of the spirit world. Some resisted. As I read the record, seeing violence done to indigenous communities, children among them, emboldened some enslaved Africans to find ways of refusing forced baptisms, prayers, hymns and the breaking of bread.

Our ancestors left words for that pain. Douglass describes a year under a man named Edward Covey, who was hired to break him: "I was broken in body, soul, and spirit. My natural elasticity was crushed, my intellect languished, the disposition to read departed".<!-- note: Douglass 1845, ch. X (opened). The passage that follows mentions suicidal thoughts; it is deliberately not quoted (safe-messaging guidelines) --> Near the end of her story Mary Prince, in London and parted from her husband in Antigua, still hoped God would find a way to give her liberty and give her back to Daniel. "I endeavour to keep down my fretting, and to leave all to Him," she said. "Yet, I must confess, I find it a hard and heavy task to do so." Her faith held, and it did not take away the strain. If you have ever prayed your way through a hard season and still lain awake, you know that combination.

She also refused to let anyone in England pretend that the enslaved were content. "How can slaves be happy when they have the halter round their neck and the whip upon their back?" she asked. All slaves want to be free, she said, and "to be free is very sweet."<!-- note: Prince 1831 (Project Gutenberg #17851, opened) -->

The wound did not close when slavery ended. I believe the legacy of slavery and colonial rule, together with racism that has never gone away, still weighs on many people of African heritage in the UK, the USA and Canada as chronic stress and low mood, and that for some it has fed into deeper illness. Studies point most clearly to one part of it, the harm done by racism today. A large review of studies, mostly from the USA, linked experiences of racism with poorer mental health, most clearly depression, anxiety and stress [UNVERIFIED: study not opened (Paradies et al. 2015)]. A study in the UK found that people who met discrimination again and again, in more than one part of life, had worse mental health years later [UNVERIFIED: study not opened (Wallace, Nazroo and Bécares 2016)].

How the older wound reaches people today is less certain. Some researchers describe historical trauma as something carried in the stories a community tells about its past. Those stories link today's hardships to the old injury, and they can feed both distress and resilience [UNVERIFIED: study not opened (Mohatt et al. 2014)]. Families also pass hardship down through upbringing and poverty. You may have heard that trauma is written into our bodies, and I do not claim it. Researchers who have studied that question argue it has not been shown in human beings [UNVERIFIED: study not opened (Yehuda and Lehrner 2018)].

One large American survey points the other way. It found depression over a lifetime less common among African Americans and Black Caribbeans in the USA than among White Americans, though when it came it tended to last longer, weigh more heavily and go untreated more often [UNVERIFIED: study not opened (Williams et al. 2007)]. If that holds, none of us should say that our people are more depressed because of slavery. It suggests real strength, perhaps partly drawn from faith, and harm that shows less in how often we fall ill than in how long suffering lasts and how rarely it is treated.

The faith that was used to wound also gives us words for grief. In Black churches in the UK, as in African American and African Canadian churches, the biblical themes of exile, suffering and divine justice still speak to many. Communal lament, drawing on both Testaments, lets a congregation grieve together over racial injustice. It sits alongside the promise of the prophet: "he hath sent me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound" (Isaiah 61:1, KJV). Two verses later the same passage promises "the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness" (Isaiah 61:3, KJV), an old phrase that names low spirits plainly. I believe the stories of scripture can help people reread their own history within a story of redemption, and that the New Testament's emphasis on liberation and justice helps many of us work through what we have inherited. Chapter 8 turns to the practical care this history calls for, so that the trauma named here is met with help.

## The hidden church became a refuge and a force for change

Long before there were Black denominations, there was worship the masters could not see. Historians call it the "invisible institution", the phrase Albert Raboteau used for the title of his 1978 history of slave religion.<!-- note: Raboteau, Slave Religion: The "Invisible Institution" in the Antebellum South (OUP, 1978), search-level. Frazier's first use not verified (open-claims 37) --> Enslaved people met in secret to pray and sing in their own way. By affirming the image of God in every person, that worship gave people whom the law called property a sense of worth and purpose. Mary Prince heard of the same thing in the Caribbean. On Turks Island, she was told, the enslaved "built up a place with boughs and leaves, where they might meet for prayers, but the white people pulled it down twice."

That hidden church helped people cope and survive under slavery. It became an informal network among the enslaved and played a part in the work of abolition and the Underground Railroad. In time it came into the open. In Philadelphia, Black worshippers walked out of St George's Methodist Church after a trustee pulled the preacher Absalom Jones from his knees during prayer. Richard Allen, who walked out with them, went on to lead the Bethel congregation. In April 1816 the African Methodist Episcopal Church, the first independent Black denomination in the USA, was organised at Bethel, with Allen as its first bishop.<!-- note: Mother Bethel AME; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Historic St George's (search-level). Walkout date disputed (1787, 1792-93 or 1794), so no year is given; Allen not quoted (ch01-evidence.md section 2) --> Allen and Douglass both used Christian principles to argue for racial equality and to deny that slavery had any moral right to exist.

These churches went on to serve their communities. In their study of the Black Church, C. Eric Lincoln and Lawrence Mamiya describe it as both a sanctuary and a centre of social change.<!-- note: Lincoln and Mamiya, The Black Church in the African American Experience (Duke UP, 1990), search-level --> Black congregations helped to build seminaries, colleges and schools, insurance companies and banks [UNVERIFIED: Lincoln and Mamiya page not checked]. The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People was founded in 1909 by a multiracial group of campaigners, and the Black churches gave its work and the later civil rights movement much of their strength [UNVERIFIED: the churches' part in the NAACP and the civil rights movement; open-claims 32].<!-- note: NAACP founded 12 Feb 1909, Library of Congress exhibit (search-level). Thesis 1291-1295 says the churches were "responsible for the formation" of the NAACP; not supported, so not repeated --> Their community work today belongs to chapter 6.

The best-known voice of that movement was the Reverend Dr Martin Luther King Jr, a Baptist minister, whose non-violent campaign was rooted in Christian principles. Not every Black American supported his approach, and some campaigned for violent resistance to police brutality, segregation and open racism. Yet his Christian principles drew the support of people of many races. From a jail cell in Birmingham, Alabama, on 16 April 1963, he wrote, "Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere."<!-- note: King, "Letter from Birmingham Jail", 16 April 1963 (King Institute, search-level). King estate controls his words: one short line only --> That same year he gave his "I Have a Dream" speech, and his principles influenced the passing of the Civil Rights Act in 1964 and the Voting Rights Act in 1965. I believe his movement also supported the mental well-being of those who took part, by giving them a moral framework and a shared identity, and that the end of legal segregation lifted a heavy daily stress from many Black Americans. That is my own view, not a measured finding.

The struggle also produced its own theology. James Cone published *Black Theology and Black Power* in 1969 and *A Black Theology of Liberation* the year after. His Black theology insists that God stands with the oppressed, and it grew from the Black experience in America. It drew on the New Testament's teaching of love and equality, and on Galatians 3:28, to confront racial injustice in church and society. At the same moment in Latin America, Catholic bishops meeting at Medellín in 1968 and the priest Gustavo Gutiérrez, whose *A Theology of Liberation* first appeared in 1971, were developing liberation theology. That movement arose in Roman Catholicism and seeks to live out faith by helping the poor and oppressed, including through political and civic life. Cone's first book came out before Gutiérrez's, and the two movements grew up side by side.<!-- note: ch01-evidence.md E5 and section 2 (search-level). Cone not quoted --> Liberation theology found a ready hearing among Christians of the African diaspora, who hear Christ's message as one of liberation from oppression and injustice. Both movements have had to keep disentangling Christian teaching from its colonial and imperial roots.

The same double edge runs through our churches in Britain and Canada today. Many African heritage Christians, including many who came from Africa and the Caribbean, still live with the legacy of racism, in the church as well as in wider society. Pentecostal and African-initiated churches keep growing. Many are strongholds of identity and of resistance to systemic racism, and their preachers speak of spiritual empowerment, economic justice and racial equality. African heritage Christians believe that by examining the past, they gain insights into the present: Christianity, in its truest form, holds the potential to unite, heal and inspire, but only when it aligns with the values of human dignity, respect and liberation that are at the heart of the Gospels' message. I believe that too.

When I think of my grandparents' churches, I see the double-edged inheritance in one family line. Those churches had travelled with empire, and their roots ran back to the plantation. My grandparents embraced the faith all the same, and through them it came down to me. Our ancestors had done the same long before them, in the open and in hiding. Douglass and Mary Prince kept their faith and told the truth about what was done in its name. I believe that honesty is good for the mind as well as the soul. What the enslaved could not say aloud, they sang.

## Reflection and practice

Think about how Christianity reached your own family. You may know a church, an island, a grandparent's name or a hymn. You may know very little. Either way, the faith you hold, or the faith you left, came down to you through people who had to decide what it meant under pressure. Sit for a few minutes with one question: which parts of that inheritance have helped me, and which parts have I been carrying without examining them? Write down one verse that has steadied you and one that has been used to keep you quiet. Bring both to God in prayer, as our ancestors brought theirs, and notice how you feel when you set them side by side.

### For church leaders

- Preach the hard verses honestly. When Ephesians 6:5 comes up in the lectionary or in Bible study, say how it was used against our ancestors, and set it beside Galatians 3:28.
- Make room for lament. Give your congregation a regular place in worship to grieve racial injustice together, and to name it before God.

### For families

- Ask the oldest members of your family how Christianity came into the family line, and write down what they say before it is lost.
- Talk with young people about both edges of the history, so that neither "it was the master's religion" nor "it has nothing to do with us" is the only story they hear.

### For clinicians

- When a patient of African heritage speaks about faith, remember that it may carry family and community history as well as belief. Ask what it has meant to their family too.
- Take reports of racism seriously as a source of stress in themselves, and do not treat a patient's mention of history as a distraction from their symptoms.

### Questions for small groups

1. How did Christianity come into your family, as far as you know, and what difference does knowing (or not knowing) make to you?
2. Frederick Douglass loved "the Christianity of Christ" and rejected "the Christianity of this land". Where would you draw that line in the church today?
3. Mary Prince wrote that keeping down her "fretting" was "a hard and heavy task". When has your faith held while the strain remained, and who helped you carry it?
