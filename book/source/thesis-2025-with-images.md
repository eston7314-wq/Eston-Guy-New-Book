**ABSTRACT**

This thesis entitled “Do Religious Awareness, Religious Knowledge and
Religious Practice derived from New Testament Christianity influence the
Mental Health of people of African Heritage residing in the United
Kingdom (UK), United States of America (USA) and Canada?” explores the
intersection of faith and Mental Health within the African communities.

The researcher utilises a mixed-methods approach to investigate how the
teachings and practices of Jesus Christ, the Apostle Paul and others,
derived from the New Testament influence the psychological state, coping
mechanisms and general mental health among Christians of African
heritage in three particular Western countries. Christianity,
particularly its teachings, parables and miracles found in the New
Testament, has historically provided a source of hope, community spirit
and moral guidance for Christians.

By using qualitative and quantitative methodologies, including
interviews and surveys, the research aims to provide a comprehensive
understanding of the spiritual, cultural and social factors that shape
mental health developments in these communities.

The research seeks also to contribute to a deeper and more appropriate
understanding of the holistic impacts of religion, particularly
Christianity, on Mental Health and mental well-being while offering
better insights into culturally sensitive mental health support
strategies for people of African heritage communities.

At this stage, however, I must clarify how the term or words, ‘African
heritage’ is used in this thesis. The Austrian philosopher, Ludwig
Wittgenstein (1889) stated that the meaning of a word is its use. This
means that a word is defined by how it is used in daily life rather than
by any logical structure that underlies it. I shall use the term
‘African heritage’, in this study, to mean ‘African’, an individual
often referred to as: ‘black’ or ‘mixed-race’ (with one parent of
African descent). Similarly, the terms: Afro-Caribbean, African
American, African Canadian and African British will be used to refer to
people of African heritage living in specific communities like the UK,
Canada, USA, Africa and the Caribbean. Of course, the term ‘African’
refers also to people born in the African continent and those who have
migrated from Africa.

**2.1 Introduction**

The term ‘Religious Awareness’ involves a realisation and acceptance of
one’s religion. Strong religious awareness can enhance a believer’s
confidence, and provide a purpose in life. Religious awareness for
people of African heritage can instil cultural pride and determination.
Similarly, the application of “knowledge” to religious consciousness
carries serious theological and philosophical implications. In
Christianity, and particularly its articulation in the New Testament,
“knowledge” is not limited to intellectual perception, but extends into
realms of experiential knowing, deep introspection and life-transforming
discernment. In this chapter, a critique will be offered of how
confluences between philosophical constructions of “knowledge” and
theological dogma affect the mental health of people of African heritage
who follow New Testament-inspired Christianity.

This Chapter integrates a literature review that covers the works of
significant philosophers, beginning with the classical era and moving to
contemporary times, with each providing a specific position in relation
to knowledge and belief. These philosophical frameworks are essential
because they form a basis for understanding the implications of
religious knowledge for mental health, especially within diasporic
African communities in the UK, USA and Canada. In review, an analysis
will be extracted from a consideration of how a variety of
understandings of knowledge and belief provide both a basis and a
challenge for the integration of faith and psychological well-being.

Although there is a wealth of academic philosophies concerning the
problem, the scope of this investigation limits my discussion to a
selection of classical and contemporary philosophers such as: Socrates,
Plato, Aristotle, Immanuel Kant, John Locke, Rene Descartes, Edmund
Gettier, Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein, Michael Polanyi and
Alvin Plantinga.

**2.2 Philosophical Foundations of Knowledge**

**2.2.1 Epistemology and religious Knowledge**

**Socrates**, as portrayed in Plato’s dialogues, famously positioned
himself in a role of an inquiring intellectual, claiming, “All I know is
that I know nothing.” Socrates’ paradoxical affirmation mirrors the
value placed in recognising one’s epistemic limitation as a source of
actual knowing. Socrates’ use of a form of inquiry through dialogue,
invites one to investigate humanity’s boundary of knowing, specifically
in relation to divine matters. Socrates’ approach suggests that actual
knowing is not about possessing information, but about entering an
infinite search for deeper knowing – a theme seen in the New Testament’s
portrayal of spiritual knowledge as a journey, rather than a destination
(*Plato, Apology 21d, 1997*).

Plato’s *Apology* or defence tells us that Socrates offended some
prominent individuals in society because his philosophic questions
challenged the grain of society. Socrates embarrassed

many people in an attempt to question them. Was Socrates pressuring
people to humble themselves, and submit to the fact that they have no
knowledge? Perhaps, ‘yes,’ perhaps ‘No!’ One could say, at least, that
Socrates longed to expose the view that some aspects of knowing go
beyond common understanding. On the contrary, studies confirm that
Socratic Irony can inform contemporary Christian philosophy, in terms of
humility and the acknowledgement of mystery, in divine knowing. Socrates
purported that we can never know for a fact any single thing. He argued
that we have beliefs and opinions, but can never know for a fact whether
they are correct or not.

For Socrates, virtue and the well-being of the soul were inextricably
intertwined with knowledge; virtue can only be acquired through
knowledge. What that entails is a life of virtue through actual
knowledge and that a Christian believer, for instance, a church minister
/ church leader, could apply Socratic methodologies in guiding someone
to learn or acknowledge the truth, specifically when faith comes under
scrutiny.

Certainly, in Christianity, humility is a significant virtue that is
part of God’s character. It is a practice of meekness, modesty, respect
and submissiveness, and a practice of obeying. Scripture guides and
inspire in terms of words that challenge Christians to practise
humility. *Luke 14:11* (AKJV) teaches that “For whosoever exalteth
himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”
Christians are encouraged in a similar manner, to “Humble yourselves in
the sight of the Lord, and he shall lift you up” (*James 4:10*).
Scriptural guidance appeals to people of African heritage who need
concrete and psychological encouragement in terms of guidance in
Christian communities. Most importantly, when following Socrates
guidance, we can reveal our lack of awareness, in hope that in fact we
are taking a stand for humility over arrogance.

**Plato** acknowledged Socrates as a mentor, and extending Socratic
thinking, formulated the theory of knowledge as “justified true belief,”
augmented with the theory of forms, where actual knowledge pertains to
eternal, unchangeable realities and not changing perceptible reality. In
“The Republic and Theaetetus,” for example, Plato insists that knowledge
transcends empirical evidence, and in its place, it appeals to mental
apprehension of such unchangeable forms. Platonic conception aligns with
New Testament ideals where knowing and faith are windows to a reality
that is beyond empirical proofs. There arises, nevertheless, a critique
when one looks at Platonic epistemology in relation to theology in the
New Testament. Platonic dualism between perceptible and ideal fails to
capture the incarnational character of Christianity where the divine
enters perceptible reality. Doubtless, Christ’s humanity is a cardinal
doctrine in Christian faith. The phenomenon of God becoming man in the
person of Jesus Christ is a profound mystery manifesting God’s
redemption and love for humanity (*John 1:14*). Platonic ideals
therefore, seem to be supplementary, and not in contradiction with
Christian doctrine concerning knowledge.

**Aristotle (2009**), on the other hand, diverged from his mentor,
Plato, by grounding his epistemology in observations and logical
reasoning. In works like *“Nicomachean Ethics* and *Metaphysics”*, he
presents an elaborate picture of knowledge, such as episteme (scientific
knowledge) and phronesis (practical intelligence). For Aristotle, one
will have justification for knowing through organised inquiry and
observation.

This empirical foundation challenges religious knowledge to present a
justification for its claims in terms of reason, in harmony with the New
Testament’s encouragement of a faith in reason- “But sanctify the Lord
God …give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope
that is in you with meekness and fear” *(1 Peter 3:15)*. Aristotle’s
demand for empirical testing might appear in contrast with faith-based
knowledge, founded in its case in revelation and in mystical experience,
but one can make a case that the philosopher merely insists on a basis
in reason for religious knowledge in order to secure its positive
contribution to mental welfare.

**Immanuel Kant,** a German philosopher, revolutionised epistemology
with an argument for a union between a priori conceptions and sensation
in producing knowledge, in *“Critique of Pure* *Reason”* (1998). Kant’s
distinction between phenomena (things experienced) and noumena (things
in themselves) represent a critical model for describing religious
knowledge, and one in which humanity can have no full cognizance of the
divine. Kant’s epistemic humility mirrors Christian doctrine and
biblical thinking regarding divine mystery and revelation, in which full
cognizance is kept for eschatological consummation *(1Corinthians
13:12*). Kant’s critical theory holds that faith must be subjected to
critical examination and reconciled with reason in order not to become a
victim of dogmatism, but to gain mental strength. By contrast, critical
examination can allow one to harmonise faith with one’s overall
worldview, and hence mental steadiness and well-being.

**John Locke**, a British philosopher and Oxford Academic, was widely
regarded as one of the most influential of the Enlightenment thinkers;
he is considered the father of modern empiricism. His empiricism,
articulated in *“An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*” (1997),
emphasises that all information comes from sensory experience and
reflection. Locke distinguishes between demonstrative, intuitive and
sensitive knowledge, and insists that true knowledge or actual
information is always grounded in empirical observation. Empirical
observation forms the basis for real information, according to Locke,
and it subjects religious information to critical analysis rather than
taking it for granted. Lock’s model proposes that faith information /
religious knowledge should be subjected to testing in order to develop
an environment in which dissonance in cognition is less, and mental
health is encouraged through logical religious frameworks.

**Rene Descartes**, *“Meditations on First Philosophy*” (1996),
introduces methodological scepticism, advocating that only purely
certain beliefs can be considered as knowledge. His assertion, “Cogito
Ergo Sum”, translated- “I think, therefore I am,” places consciousness
at the foundation of all knowledge. This Cartesian doubt, in its
stimulation of critical examination, raises concerns for religious
knowledge, tending to rely not on experimental certainty, but faith and
revelation. In principle, according to Descartes, humanity’s abundance
of knowledge is a priori, and demonstrable through unaided reason, but
in practice, for finite human intelligence, recourse must be taken to
experience in proving any statement when demonstration through reason is
out of range. Moreover, Descartes’s emphasis on doubt and certainty
invites critical scrutiny of religious beliefs, promoting intellectual
integrity that will support mental health through the assurance that
one’s faith possesses a sound basis.

**Edmund Gettier**: As we approach a consideration of an accepted theory
of knowledge, we turn now to Gettier problem, originally set out in his
article, *“Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?”* (1963), in which he
that challenged the traditional characterisation of knowledge in terms
of justified true belief with counter-cases in which justified true
belief does not issue in knowledge. Religious epistemology, as concerned
both with the nature of divine knowledge and with justification for its
claims, is seriously challenged by such a problem, and Gettier’s work
encourages consideration of whether and in what way reliable access to
truth must underlie such claims, challenging an assessment of
justification for such claims, and posing questions regarding
justification for claims to divine acquaintance with reality.

However, numerous Christian believers detail religious experiences and
revelations attesting to the intensity of faith, but assuming Gettier’s
criticisms have merit, then in what manner must such experiences and
revelations be taken? Do they have to be examined empirically and
rationally?

Like Descartes, **Bertrand Russell**, in *“The problems of Philosophy”*
(2009), insists on logical clarity and precision in working with an
analysis of knowledge. Analytic philosophy, in its demand for a similar
level of logical and scientific articulation, insists that religious
knowledge must be addressed in a similar manner. Undeniably, this
insistence on accuracy and logical coherence can undermine at least part
of the mystical and experiential aspects that make such a strong part of
many Christian denominations.

On the contrary, this approach can serve to sharpen theological
arguments in such a manner that they become intellectually sound enough
to support mental health through a coherent and stable system of
beliefs. Philosophically, Russell’s arguments show that coherent and
logical religious beliefs can form a sound basis for mental health.

On the other hand, **Ludwig Wittgenstein’s** later writings, in
*“Philosophical Investigations*” (2009), deny a monistic language of
knowledge, and reject its existence. Religious language, according to
his “language games” theory, is under a new set of rules, in contrast to
scientific language. Religious language accommodates a contextualistic
view of religious knowledge, its social and practice-dependent nature,
together with its meaning and purpose in forms of life, and therefore
its contribution to mental health.

Wittgenstein’s observations emphasise that religious beliefs cannot be
considered simply as cognitive propositions, but are embedded in forms
of life that give them meaning and purpose, thus contributing to mental
well-being. Furthermore, Wittgenstein’s theory can underpin a more
integral view of religious knowledge; one that recognises its
epistemology in a nonreductive manner, and not simply collapsing it into
mere subjectivity and relativism.

**Michael Polanyi**, however, brought the crucial concepts of tacit
Knowledge and personal knowledge to the centre of academic inquiry. In
*“Personal Knowledge”* (1966), Polanyi promotes tacit dimension of
knowledge, recognising the personal and subjective nature of knowing.
Polanyi holds that tacit knowledge is embedded in the minds of
individuals in terms of experience and skill, but must then be
translated into explicit knowledge through language or models that can
be understood.

Polanyi proposes that individuals can know more than they are told,
therefore they must create knowledge through collaborating with others
in groups or teams, in an organisation. What this involves is tacit
knowledge, a form of personal knowing that is gained through practice
and experience by individuals in an organisation, but conveyed through
shared knowing among people. Moreover, one could say that such a
position is compatible with the experiential nature of religious
knowledge in which individuals’ direct experiences with the divine
constitutes its focal point.

Above all, Polanyi’s concern with tacit knowledge confirms that
religious knowledge, even when not necessarily empirically verifiable,
is real and meaningful to believers, like many African Christians, and
is thus beneficial for mental well-being.

In addition to this, **Alvin Plantinga’s** work in *“Warranted Christian
Belief*” (2000), denies the argument that Christian beliefs need to have
inferential evidence to be rational. He argues that belief in God can be
“properly basic” and justified in a non-inferential way. Plantinga
postulates that our basic beliefs are those foundational beliefs that
need no evidence to support them, and in terms of which our other
beliefs must be justified. His externalism in epistemology, “proper
functionalism”, is a type of epistemological reliabilism, a process that
produces a high proportion of true beliefs. Plantinga’s view therefore
posits a strong model of faith that is in perfect harmony with an
individual’s worldview that guarantees mental health through a coherent
and meaningful system of beliefs.

**2.2.2 Rationalism and Empiricism in Religious Knowledge**

At this stage, the literature reveals that rationalism and empiricism
offer contrasting yet complementary approaches to understanding
religious knowledge. Rationalists, such as Descartes argue that reason
and innate ideas are primary sources of knowledge, evident in the New
Testament’s emphasis on logos (Greek for “word” or “reason”) as a divine
principle *(John:1-31)*. This perspective suggests that divine knowledge
is accessible through rational contemplation of God’s revelation in
Christ. In contrast, empiricism, reflected in philosophical discourse
such as Locke’s, aligns with the experiential aspect of faith where
personal encounters with the divine through prayer, worship and
sacraments provide empirical evidence of God’s presence and action. This
dual epistemological framework enriches the understanding of religious
knowledge in Christianity, accommodating both rational and experiential
dimensions.

**2.2.3 Basic Religious Knowledge in the New Testament**

In addition to having a clearer understanding of the philosophical
thinking behind Religious knowledge, it is important to emphasise that
Christians generally refer to ‘religious knowledge’ as an understanding
of religious texts, doctrines and traditions. Knowledge of the New
Testament can offer moral guidance and coping strategies. For example,
understanding the principles of forgiveness and love can help believers
deal with interpersonal conflicts and reduce stress.

**2.2.4 Religious Practices**

Religious practices will be discussed in full later in this thesis, but
the concept should be understood to include rituals, worship and daily
behaviours that are rooted in faith. Regular engagement in these
practices has been linked to better mental health outcomes. For example,
practices such as daily prayer, communal worship and scripture study can
provide regular opportunities for reflection, support and relief of
stress, aiding resilience besides mental stability.

**2.2.5 Conclusion**

This philosophical exploration of knowledge and belief, spanning from
the Socratic inquiry to contemporary epistemological debates, provides a
nuanced framework for understanding the impact of religious knowledge on
mental health. By critically examining and integrating religious beliefs
with rational and empirical foundations, individuals can develop a more
coherent and stable belief system that supports mental health by
providing meaning, coping mechanisms and a sense of purpose grounded in
well-founded knowledge.

**2.3 Theological Perspectives on Knowledge**

**2.3.1 Biblical Conceptions of Knowledge**

The New Testament offers a complex view of knowledge, distinguishing
between human and divine understanding. The Greek term ‘*gnosis’*
(knowledge) and its derivatives frequently convey spiritual insight. For
example, *1 Corinthians 13:12* contrasts our current partial knowledge
with the complete knowledge that will be attained in the eschaton: “For
now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in
part; but then I shall know even as also I am known” (AKJV). This
eschatological perspective highlights the dynamic and progressive nature
of knowledge in Christian theology, suggesting that human understanding
is limited and contingent upon divine revelation which will be fully
realised in the future.

Eschatology is key to a believer’s satisfaction in the Christian life;
more so in the daily lives of those of African heritage, because of
their general experiences in society. For the Christian Church,
eschatology involves: the Second Coming of Christ, establishment of a
Kingdom of God that will last for a thousand years (a long time) over
which Jesus Christ will reign, and defeat evil with the conquering of
death. It seems therefore, that eschatological beliefs appear to be
associated with an individual’s religiosity; it is seen as key to being
loyal to Christ while offering the believer proof that God is in
control. Christians can have hope in what is to come while living in a
world that is full of practical and mental challenges. However, as we
examine the biblical context of eschatology, we note that the millennial
Kingdom of Jesus *(Revelation* *20:1-6*) will not be a literal one. Many
amillennial thinkers argue that the millennial period in Revelation 20
can be taken figuratively. They claim that after Christ’s resurrection
and ascension, he began his reign, and presently rules on Earth (the
millennial age) through his people, and will return physically at any
moment to usher in Heaven on earth. On the other hand, postmillennial
thinkers claim that the millennial age is ushered in by the unrelenting
advance of the Gospels around the world.

Furthermore, the letters of the Apostle, Paul present Christians with
the concept of personal and corporate eschatology by using phrases such
as “fallen asleep” to describe believers who have died, but still await
the return of Christ *(1 Corinthians 15: 18).* At the same time, Paul
speaks about the imminent return of Christ. The Apostle advises that the
Kingdom of God is here, now, but that Christ still is yet to come *(1
Corinthians 15:24*). This means that Christian believers live within the
tension, and wait anxiously for the return of Christ. Here, Paul
encourages Christians to be ready for the day of Christ’s return, though
they do not know the exact day or hour *(1 Thessalonians 4:13-18).* This
means that until the time of Christ’s return, the temple is no longer in
Jerusalem, but rather in the heart of the believer *(Ephesians 2:22).*

Above all, research shows that Christians accept the basic eschatology
of:

- **The Apostles Creed**: “I believe in the resurrection of the body,
  and the life everlasting. Amen…;”

- **The Nicene Creed**: “I believe in one Lord, Jesus Christ…He will
  come again in glory to judge the living and the dead, and his kingdom
  will have no end…I look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life
  of the world to come. Amen;”

- **The Athanasian Creed**: \[Christ\] rose from the dead, ascended into
  heaven, is seated in the right hand of the Father…and those who have
  done evil will go into everlasting fire.”

These Creeds are profound; Christian eschatology of hope is plain in the
Scriptures, and is mirrored clearly in the creeds. Christians believe
that Christ will come again to establish justice and peace forever.
Believers accept that Christian eschatology is in itself a form of
coping mechanism. This belief system lies in the hearts and minds of
people of African heritage who develop mental strength and resilience
when faced with injustice, prejudice, racism, uncertainty or other forms
of suffering. Christian eschatology gives them hope!

**2.3.2 Knowledge and Faith**

The interplay between knowledge and faith is a central theme in
Christian theology. Hebrews 11:1 defines faith as “substance of things
hoped for, the evidence of things not seen,” suggesting that faith
extends beyond empirical evidence, relying on trust in God’s promises.
On the contrary, the New Testament also advocates for a reasoned faith,
as indicated in *1 Peter 3:15*: “… and be ready always to give an answer
to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with
meekness and fear.” This call to articulate and defend one’s beliefs
indicates that knowledge and faith are not mutually exclusive, but
intertwined, with faith providing a foundation for deeper understanding.
Religious faith is the starting point of the Christian life, but it is
neither arbitrary nor blind. It is grounded in knowledge and reason, and
involves a belief that makes some kind of either an implicit or explicit
reference to a transcendent source, thus indicating that the basis for a
person’s faith usually comes from the authority of revelation.

In Christianity, faith and reason are seen as working together to help
people know and love God. Some say that faith and reason are like wings
that help the human spirit rise to understand the truth. Reason is used
to explore faith. Believers use reason to explore their faith, and
discover its truth. This can help believers move from an initial stage
of faith to a deeper understanding. Moreover, Christians are encouraged
to share with others, the reasons for their beliefs as a form of
fellowship. This practice is common among Baptists and Pentecostal
Christians of African heritage during prayer meetings and conventions.

**2.3.3 The Role of Revelation**

Revelation stands as a cornerstone of religious knowledge in
Christianity. The New Testament portrays Jesus as the ultimate
revelation of God, embodying divine wisdom and truth. *John 14:6*
records Jesus saying, “I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man
cometh unto the Father, but by me.” This statement positions Christ as
the focal point of all religious knowledge. However, revelation occurs
both through scripture and the Holy Spirit, with the Bible regarded as
the inspired word of God, conveying divine truths that guide believers’
understanding and conduct. Additionally, the Holy Spirit is believed to
illuminate the scriptures, enabling believers to comprehend their deeper
meanings, and apply them to their lives. This means that Christian
revelation is viewed as occurring primarily in the life, teaching, death
and resurrection of Jesus; all interpreted by the apostolic witness
under the illumination of the Holy Spirit. Revelation prepares believers
to walk with Christ through tribulation, because he will return as the
ultimate King who will restore his people, and usher in the New
Creation, the New Heaven and the New Earth.

New Testament-inspired Christians believe that revelation was meant as a
comfort to the early church, in a time when evil seemed rampant. This
was exhibited when the Apostle John, one of the last of the closest
followers of Jesus, ‘The Christ’, was exiled towards the end of the
reign of Emperor Domitian on the small Greek Island of Patmos where he
received these revelations as visions that were meant to guide and
comfort the Church. Today, many Christians among the wider denominations
like Anglicans, Quakers, Pentecostals, Baptists and Roman Catholics feel
that our world is in turmoil, and needs similar comfort, thus revelation
has much to offer right now. Believers can receive its encouraging
message, as it was intended by the Spirit of Jesus Christ through the
witness of John.

Although some 19<sup>th</sup> Century scholars state that the Book of
Revelation was written between AD 64, because of persecution under
Emperor Nero and the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, it is generally seen as
a book of hope and a book of warning. Revelation gives hope to those
Christians who are being persecuted, assuring them that their sufferings
are not in vain, while warning others of the coming events and what will
happen to them. This general view of revelation resonates with people of
African heritage, among many other communities who experience injustice,
anguish or prejudice, and need hope with spiritual strength to boost
their resilience and mental health.

**2.4 Knowledge as Spiritual Insight**

In the New Testament, knowledge is often equated with spiritual insight,
as seen in Paul’s prayer for the Ephesians when he asks the God of our
Lord Jesus Christ to give them “the Spirit of wisdom and revelation in
the knowledge of him.” *(Ephesians 1:17).* This suggests that knowing
God is not just an intellectual exercise, but involves relational and
experiential dimensions. For people of African heritage, who have
historically faced systematic oppression and discrimination, this form
of knowledge provides resilience by connecting them to a higher power
and a wider community of believers.

**2.5 Knowledge and Transformation**

*Romans 12:2* calls believers to “…be not conformed to this world, but
be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind.” This theme of
transformation through knowledge is central to the New Testament. It
suggests a process of mental and spiritual renewal that aligns with
God’s will. The transformative power of the Holy Spirit is said to have
many effects on Christians, including regeneration of the individual’s
heart, allowing people to hear the Gospel, and place their faith in
Christ.

The Holy Spirit sanctifies people by making them more like Christ,
establishing the truth while bringing clarity and understanding to God’s
word. This personal and communal process enhances mental well-being by
providing a robust foundation for coping with life’s adversities. For
African heritage communities in the diaspora, this transformation
involving the reclamation of identity and heritage embraces the power of
the New Testament teachings.

**2.6 Practical Implications for Mental Health**

**2.6.1 Definition of Mental Health**

The World Health Organisation (WHO, 2010) describes Mental health as a
component of behavioural health that includes a person’s emotional,
psychological and social well-being. It affects how we think, feel and
act. Mental health also helps to determine how individuals manage
stress, relate to others, and make healthy choices. In other words,
mental health is a key component of our overall health, and is closely
linked to physical health. It is a state of well-being that enables us
to cope with the stresses of life while realising our abilities. Our
mental health affects our state of mind or focus that enables us to
learn, work and contribute to our social groups, families and
communities.

Mental health is closely linked to physical health, therefore many types
of physical, long-lasting or chronic conditions like diabetes, heart
disease and stroke affect how we behave, generally. Similarly, the
existence of chronic health conditions can increase the risk of
developing a mental health condition.

Furthermore, mental health is influenced by several positive or negative
(risk) factors at multiple levels including individual, family,
community and society. According to the Centre for Disease Control and
Prevention (CDC, 2024), risk factors increase the likelihood of
experiencing poor mental health while protective factors increase the
possibility of experiencing positive mental health.

**2.6.2 Risk Factors that Affect Mental Health include:**

- Interpersonal and institutional discrimination,

- lack of access to housing,

- poor healthcare,

- poor education,

- lack of access to employment that leads to poverty,

- Inadequate economic opportunities,

- Adverse childhood experiences and other types of interpersonal
  violence,

- Domestic abuse,

- Social isolation,

- Alcohol and other drug abuse,

- Poor emotional well-being or poor coping skills,

- Ongoing or chronic medical conditions such as traumatic brain injury,
  cancer, diabetes,

- Physical disabilities that may include less developed mental or
  emotional skills.

2**.6.3 Protective Factors that Affect Mental Health include:**

- Access to quality employment, housing and education,

- Strong social connections with positive, stable relationships,

- Positive coping skills,

- Living in safe communities,

- Access to preventive health or medical care

- Access to adequate mental and physical health services,

- Supportive family environments,

- Supportive religious or faith environments.

The WHO (2010) notes a strong correlation between mental distress and
mental health conditions like depression and schizophrenia. Furthermore,
people with no apparent risk factors may still experience mental
distress, while others may develop a mental health condition.

Research by Johnson and Edwards (2021) indicated earlier, noted that
church-based support networks that provide spiritual guidance,
counselling and other practical assistance promote mental well-being
among people of African heritage.

**2.6.4 Resilience through Faith**

Knowledge of scripture and its promises fosters resilience among people
of African heritage. Verses such as *Philippians 4:13*, “I can do all
things through Christ which strengtheneth me,” provide a source of
empowerment when faced with adversities. Africans in the diaspora, gain
inspiration from Jesus’ words and teachings. Jesus encourages resilience
in his disciples ahead of his crucifixion, when he says, “…ye shall be
scattered, every man to his own…In the world ye shall have tribulation:
but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world” *(John 16:32-33).*

Similarly, *Hebrews 12:2* states that Jesus persevered because he kept
his focus on “the joy that was set before him.” Jesus was resilient
because he knew that the suffering would all be worthwhile in the end.
He had set his mind on the reward that awaited him; giving him the
strength to finish well. Some Christians point to the weakening of
Jesus’s resilience when he cried out on the cross, saying, “Eloi, Eloi,
lama sabachthani?” *(Mark 15:34)* which translates, “My God, my God, why
hast thou forsaken me?” However, it is argued that like Jesus, many
Christians go through a form of self-assessment of their faith, implying
a form of reaffirmation at times of extreme stress and feelings of
hopelessness.

**2.6.5 Community Support**

The communal aspect of African philosophy and New Testament teachings
emphasises the importance of support systems. Churches become places of
refuge where knowledge of God’s love and community care intertwine to
bolster mental health. The Black Church serves a myriad of functions
within the African American community (Moore, 1994, Hill, 1997). It
served as a coping and survival mechanism during slavery; initially
emerging as an “invisible institution,” an informal network among the
slaves. It was instrumental for abolitionist activity and the
Underground Railroad (Lincoln & Mamiya, 1990., Moore, 1991).

These churches provided social service functions, and were responsible
for the formation of African-American seminaries, Black colleges and
academies, insurance companies, banks, the National Association for the
Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) and the Civil Rights Movement of
the sixties (Lincoln & Mamiya, 1990). Today, the Black Churches are even
more active in promoting the mental health of the African American
communities. They have assumed the character of a ‘family house,’ a
symbol of unity and identity.

According to D. J. Roberts (1987), “the nature and mission of the Church
as an extended family made up of believers in Christ, is the context for
a viable Black ecclesiology” (p.40). Many activities are offered to the
community by mega and non-mega churches, including self-help and a
variety of support groups that deal with substance issues, HIV and AIDS,
lay health advisory networks, youth development and afterschool academic
tutorial assistance, latchkey programmes, mental health counselling
services, fatherhood initiatives, marriage and family counselling, food
and clothing outreach and a variety of other services that provide a
context for empowering the African American community. Many families and
individuals benefit from the support of the Black churches - Baptists
and Methodists, among others – by gaining access to vital services thus
reducing stress, and improving self-help while promoting better mental
health.

In a 2006 survey, the Centre for Disease Control (CDC) reported that
suicide was the third leading cause of death for African American youth
aged 15 to 24. The Youth Risk Behaviour Surveillance (YRBS) survey found
that in the twelve months preceding the survey, 16.9% of high school
students seriously considered attempting suicide, while 13% made a
suicide plan and 8.4% made a suicide attempt in 2005. Among African
American youth, 12.2% reported seriously considering a suicide attempt,
9.6% made a suicide plan and 7.6% attempted suicide. Hanging by
suffocation, self-inflicted shooting and poisoning were the commonly
used suicide methods among African American adolescents, suffering from
depression, hopelessness, delinquency or substance abuse. By 2008, there
was no established suicide prevention programme for African American
youth. Historically, the Black Church has provided for the spiritual and
emotional needs of the African American community (Lincoln & Mamiya,
1990), but although they are not monolithic, there is a common
religious/cultural ethos that underlies many churches that serve people
of African heritage, hence their reference to the “Black Church.”

Research shows that the Black Church in the USA, that is easily
accessible, widely respected, endorsed by family members, peers and the
community, provide an ideal location in which to develop suicide
interventions, because African Americans are more likely to seek help
from clergy for mental health concerns. In view of the research
findings, and despite the potential barriers to implementing suicide
prevention programmes in Black churches, it is perceived that the
collaboration of professional bodies with the Black Church to develop
suicide prevention programmes provides a promising approach to better
address the unmet needs of many African American individuals and
communities, thereby promoting better mental health.

Similarly, many people of African heritage receive support and guidance
from the UK’s ‘Racial Justice Advocacy Forum’ (RJAF), an ecumenical
network of Black and Asian Christians that seeks to speak prophetically
on behalf of ‘Black and Brown’ Christians to the Government on racial
injustice challenges and reparations. In response to the Commission on
Race and Ethnic Disparities Report (March 2021), this Forum seeks to
make recommendations to help churches begin or continue engaging with
racial injustice in the twenty-first century.

The Forum, formed on 15 February 2023, comprises representations from
various Christian institutions such as the Ascension Trust, the Baptist
Union of Great Britain, Church together in Britain and Ireland, the
Salvation Army, the Methodist Church, the Religious Society of Friends
(Quakers), the Evangelical Alliance, the United Reformed Church and the
Sam Sharp Project, and reaches out to Christians from many denominations
through their respective clergy or Church leaders on matters that may
cause stress, anxiety and damage to members’ mental health. The Forum
held a national conference on 13 April 2023, in London, to explore the
roles churches can play in building confidence with trust between black
and brown communities and the British police. It aims also to amplify
the voices of Christians while providing a platform to help shape and
influence the future of policing and criminal justice services in the
UK, hoping to reduce the level of mental anguish suffered by African
British personnel in dealing with police and the criminal justice
services.

Similarly, St. Paul’s Cathedral in London is committed to creating a
Cathedral for all to celebrate diversity in the name of Jesus,
especially the excluded, oppressed or unheard. In this respect, it has
several initiatives that support racial justice and community work for
Black people, including:

- Resources and videos to help communities address discrimination,

- A year-round programme of events on social justice and Christian
  spirituality, featuring prominent speakers and theologians, including
  Dr Martin Luther King, Dr Paula Gooder and Reverend Raphael Warnock
  who spoke on Dr King’s legacy, on the 60<sup>th</sup> Anniversary of
  King’s historic visit to St. Paul’s Cathedral in 1964,

- Faith in action resources that offer guidance for Christians who want
  to take action on racial justice and young people’s mental health, and

- Support for movements and organisations that campaign for social
  justice and equality, hence Dr King, leader of the US Civil Rights
  Movement, delivering his sermon at the cathedral in 1964.

Another display of community support by Church groups began on the night
of 14 June 2017 when a massive fire swept through the Grenfell
residential Tower block in North Kensington, West London, resulting in
the death of seventy-two individuals who were mainly from minority
ethnic groups, including people of African heritage.

Faith Groups responded rapidly, compassionately and holistically. A
church leader reported that his neighbourhood church was opened at 3
a.m. when the altar candles were lit; followed by a steady flow of
residents and volunteers who established and managed a full-scale
operation of support for survivors and relatives of victims. While
physical support and donations of tea, breakfast, fruit, biscuits and
blankets were immediately made available, emotional and spiritual care –
“pastoral first-aid”- from the clergy with other faithful brought
comfort to many. A one-hour webinar, hosted by the ‘Churches Together in
Britain and Ireland (CTBI)’ and ‘The Baptist Union of Great Britain’ on
8th September, 2020 demonstrated the community’s follow-up support for
victims who were still suffering from emotional trauma while fighting
for justice.

**2.6.6 Hope and Purpose**

Knowledge of God’s promises instils hope. *Romans 8:28* declares, “And
we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to
them who are called according to his purpose.” This assurance provides
hope and purpose for living; this is crucial for maintaining mental
well-being. Christians accept that their purpose in life is to bring
glory to God by worshipping Him, sharing the gospels with those around,
and living their lives as offerings to their Heavenly Father. Their
purpose in glorifying God is to have a restored relationship with Him,
and to love others in the same way God loves them.

For many people of African heritage who experience anxiety and stress in
attempts to gain employment, proper housing or a good education for
their children, belief and hope in the fulfilment of God’s promises help
them through life’s challenges. *1Peter1:13* advises Christians to “Hope
to the end for the grace that is to be brought unto you at the
revelation of Jesus Christ.” Hope is not wishful thinking; it is part of
faith. *Romans 10:17* says that “faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by
the word of God.” This implies that hope, like faith, is also
strengthened by the word of God. Hope comes from the promises of God
rooted in the work of Jesus, the Christ. Many Christians remember the
words of Jeremiah who proclaims that the righteous who have this
trustful hope in God, have a general confidence in God’s protection and
help (*Jeremiah 29:11*), and are free from fears and anxiety *(Psalm
46:2-3).* This resonates with Christians of African heritage who believe
“The Lord of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge” *(Psalm
46:11).* These Christians gain psychological support and resilience
through their faith in Christ and trust in God.

**2.6.7 Conclusion**

Knowledge, as understood in New Testament-inspired Christianity, is
complex and multi-faceted. It is philosophical, theological, spiritual,
experiential and practical. For people of African heritage, the communal
dimension of knowledge within the Christian faith, manifesting in
worship and fellowship, provides crucial social support and a sense of
belonging. These factors contribute significantly to resilience and
positive mental health outcomes, especially in diasporic contexts where
individuals may face unique cultural and socio-economic challenges.

This first part of the literature review has explored the philosophical
and theological dimensions of knowledge, religious knowledge and beliefs
within the context of New Testament-inspired Christianity. By examining
epistemological theories, biblical conceptions and the interplay between
faith and revelation, the research seeks to present a deeper
understanding of how religious knowledge is built up and its
implications for mental health. The second half of this chapter will
build on these insights in order to investigate the specific experiences
of people of African heritage in the UK, USA and Canada, highlighting
the transformative potential of New Testament-inspired Christianity in
their lives

**2.7 Literature Review of Christianity’s Contribution to the Mental
Health of People of African Heritage in the UK, USA and Canada.**

**2.7.1 Introduction**

The mental health of people of African heritage in diasporic contexts,
particularly in the UK, USA and Canada has increasingly attracted
scholarly attention. This section reviews the contributions of New
Testament-inspired Christianity to mental health outcomes among these
populations. By examining the literature produced by UK, USA and
Canadian experts, this review aims to contextualise and analyse the role
of religious faith, particularly Christianity, in fostering
psychological well-being, resilience and practical self-determination in
the face of contemporary challenges.

**2.7.2 Christianity and Mental Health in the UK**

**2.7.2.1 Historical Context and Demographic Overview**

The African diaspora in the UK has a long and complex history, shaped by
migration patterns from the Caribbean and Africa. Christianity,
particularly in its Pentecostal, Evangelical and Baptist forms, has
played a significant role in the community’s religious life. Scholars
such as Beckford (1998) have noted that these branches of Christianity
provide a framework for understanding suffering, resilience and hope.
Research by Watson and Sharpe (2018) found that African-Caribbean
Christians often turn to their faith in times of crisis. The New
Testament’s message of hope and perseverance provide psychological
comfort and strength, offering a counter-narrative to experiences of
marginalisation and systemic oppression. Koenig (2009) similarly argues
that understanding one’s faith and its teachings can offer a sense of
purpose, hope and resilience in facing life’s challenges.

**2.7.2.2 Empirical Studies on Religion and Mental Health**

Empirical research by Bhui et al. (2018) has demonstrated that religious
engagement among people of African heritage in the UK is associated with
lower levels of depression and anxiety. This is attributed to the
community support and coping mechanisms embedded in church activities,
such as communal worship and small group prayer meetings. Churches often
serve as safe spaces where individuals can express their struggles, and
receive both spiritual and practical support. Taylor, R., et al (2000)
highlight the role of religious leaders as informal mental health
counsellors, noting that pastors and church leaders are often the first
point of contact for individuals experiencing mental health issues.
These leaders provide counselling that integrates biblical teachings
with psychological principles, fostering a holistic approach to mental
well-being.

**2.7.2.3 Theological Reflections and Mental Health**

Theologically, the New Testament’s themes of redemption, hope and
community resonate deeply within the African diaspora. The story of
Christ’s suffering and resurrection provides a powerful framework for
understanding personal and collective adversity. Beckford (2000) argues
that these themes help individuals reframe their experiences of racism
and socio-economic challenges thereby promoting moral support and
resilience. Research highlights that people of African heritage may have
been more likely to experience higher levels of distress, anxiety,
stigma and racism during the Covid-19 pandemic, 2020-2021.

However, two studies led by Larcom et al (2020, 2021) of the University
of Cambridge suggest that the psychological distress caused by the
Covid-19 lockdowns in 2020 - 2021 (UK) and experience of the Covid-19
infection in 2021 (USA), was reduced among people of faith compared to
non-religious individuals. The studies found that people of religious
faith may have experienced lower levels of unhappiness and stress than
secular people, with the intensity of religiosity being a major factor.
Researchers found that where mental health declined, it was around 60%
worse on average for the non-religious compared to people of faith with
typical levels of religiosity. Reports indicate that People of African
heritage found comfort from fellow Christians, the church and friends
during the Covid-19 pandemic, relieving grief on the death of loved
ones, colleagues and friends.

**2.7.3 Christianity and Mental Health in the USA**

**2.7.3.1 Historical Context and Demographic Overview**

In the USA, the role of New Testament-inspired Christianity,
particularly within African American communities, has been studied
extensively. The African American (Black) church has historically been a
central institution in the lives of African Americans, providing
spiritual guidance and a foundation for social and political activism.
Lincoln and Mamiya (1990) provide a comprehensive overview of the
African American / Black church’s role in fostering resilience and
psychological well-being, arguing that the church has been a vital
source of emotional support, helping individuals navigate the stresses
of systemic racism, poverty and social injustice.

**2.7.3.2 Empirical Studies on Religion and Mental Health**

Harold G. Koenig’s (2009) research explores the relationship between
religion and mental health among African Americans, identifying several
pathways through which Christianity positively impacts mental health,
including the promotion of healthy coping strategies, the provision of
social support and the encouragement of a positive self-concept rooted
in divine purpose and worth. The example of Dr Martin Luther King Jr,
the Great Civil Rights leader and African American Baptist highlights
how New Testament teachings and Baptist rituals played a crucial role in
the Civil Rights Movement, fostering resilience and a sense of justice
among African Americans.

Dr King’s non-violent activism, deeply rooted in Christian principles,
not only promoted social change, but also supported mental well-being by
providing a moral framework and collective identity. However, Dr King’s
approach was not supported by all Black Americans; some campaigned for
violent activism in the face of police brutality, segregation and open
racism, but Dr King’s Christian principles brought the support of
multi-racial groups, and influenced the passing of the Civil Rights Act
(1964) and the US Voting Rights Act (1965). King’s “I have a dream”
speech (1963), harnessed the mixed-race religious and collective efforts
of care to achieve better mental well-being, positive mental health and
basic human rights privileges for Black Americans. The passing of these
Acts helped to reduce stress caused by rigid segregation rules and
practices, while overt racism decreased, resulting in improved mental
health for Black Americans.

**2.7.3.3 Theological Reflections on Mental Health**

Recent studies by Ali and Thompson (2019) highlight the role of
religious coping among African Americans facing mental health
challenges. Their research shows that practices such as praying and
spiritual surrender are used to manage stress and trauma, demonstrating
effectiveness in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety. Sewell and
Pargament (2011) emphasise the potential of integrating Christian
principles into therapeutic practices, especially for African Americans
who may mistrust secular mental health services. These interventions,
including pastoral counselling and faith-based support groups, have been
found to enhance treatment outcomes by aligning therapeutic processes
with the client’s spiritual beliefs.

**2.7.4 Christianity and Mental Health in Canada**

**2.7.4.1 Historical Context and Demographic Overview**

Canada’s African diaspora is diverse, with significant populations from
the Caribbean, Africa and the USA. The religious landscape is similarly
varied, with a strong presence of Pentecostal, Baptist and other
Evangelical denominations. Unlike in the UK and the USA, the
contribution of Christianity to the mental health of people of African
heritage in Canada has received less scholarly attention. However, Este
and Bernard (2003) note that the church provides a base for social
integration, cultural preservation and psychological support. As in the
UK and USA, African Canadian churches often emphasise New Testament
themes of hope, redemption and social justice, resonating with the
experiences of Canadians of African heritage. These churches provide a
sanctuary where believers can find solace from the racial challenges
they may face in the wider Canadian society.

Furthermore, the Canadian Multiculturalism Act of 1988 aims to ensure
that all Canadians are treated equally, and that the Government respects
and celebrate diversity. The Policy’s principles include equality,
cultural heritage, mutual respect, indigenous peoples’\[rights\],
official language and social equality that is guaranteed within society
and under the law, regardless of race, origin, creed or religion. As in
the UK and USA, the effectiveness of this layer of Government’s support
for people of African heritage, among others, is dependent on its
implementation.

Research shows that Canada has strong commitments to pluralism which
fall short in their implementation that varies widely across provinces
and groups. Although pluralist policies have improved the experiences of
the Quebecois, for example, notably immigrant minorities, the same is
not true for indigenous groups, Afro-Canadians and other minorities.
Yet, Este and Bernard suggested that collaboration between Church and
State could enhance the African Canadian’s well-being and mental health.

**2.7.4.2 Empirical Studies on Religious and Mental Health**

Studies by Idemudia and Williams (2014) suggest that strong religious
affiliation is associated with better mental health among African
Canadian Christians. These researchers argue that the sense of
community, the support structures within the Church and the spiritual
resources offered by Christianity contribute to lower levels of stress
and overall better mental well-being. James and Tucker (2017) found that
African Canadians actively engaged in religious practices report higher
levels of psychological well-being, attributed to the supportive
networks within churches that offer emotional support, practical
assistance and a sense of belonging.

James (2017) noted that religious participation, including church-based
programmes, moral teachings and community accountability fostered within
church communities often provide resources for dealing with stress,
anxiety, unemployment and other socio-economic issues that lead to lower
levels of substance abuse, fewer suicidal intentions, overall well-being
and positive mental health. Similarly, researchers, Johnson and Edwards
(2021) note that church-based support networks provide spiritual
guidance, counselling and other practical assistance that promote mental
well-being among people of African heritage.

**2.7.4.3 Theological Reflections and Mental Health**

The New Testament’s emphasis on love, forgiveness and community spirit
is particularly significant for African Canadians. These theological
themes are frequently invoked in sermons, religious group meetings and
religious education, reinforcing positive self-identity and social
cohesion. Este (2004) argues that such theological reflections help
individuals navigate the challenges of multiculturalism and systematic
discrimination in Canada.

**2.8 Christianity and Mental Well-being in General**

**2.8.1 Addressing Stigma around Mental Health**

Despite the benefits outlined above, stigma around mental health remains
a significant barrier. Mental health stigma refers to negative,
judgmental and/or discriminatory stereotypes and attitudes toward mental
health challenges and people experiencing mental health conditions.
Stigma can present as social stigma, self-stigma or structural stigma.
In the UK, Green and Hall (2019) found that stigma often prevent
individuals from seeking professional help or support within religious
communities. These researchers advocate for training church leaders to
recognise and address mental health issues. Research shows that many
church leaders in the African diaspora are approachable and closely
connected with their congregants, enabling them to be informed of needs
so they can react appropriately.

McKenzie et al (2015) highlight the role of Black churches in addressing
mental health stigma within African Canadian communities, advocating for
greater collaboration between mental health professionals and religious
leaders to develop culturally sensitive interventions that respect and
incorporate the spiritual beliefs of African Canadian clients.
Canadians, Williams and Carter (2020), suggest that incorporating mental
health education into church programmes can help reduce stigma and
foster open dialogues about mental health to enable churches to play a
crucial role in changing perceptions and encouraging help-seeking
behaviours.

In the USA, cultural mistrust was found to be a significant barrier to
engagement with mental health services (Williams, Yu & Jackson, 1997). A
study by Nickerson, Helms and Terrell (1994), conducted using 105 Black
college students found that greater levels of mistrust for White
counsellors was associated with lower levels of formalised mental health
and community-based service utilisation. Terrell and Terrell (1981)
present a theory of cultural mistrust which posits that black people
have developed a deep-rooted suspicion for whites in authority because
of an extensive history of racial mistreatment (Nickerson, Helms &
Terrell, 1994).

**2.8.2 Theological Reflections on Suffering and Hope**

Theologically, the New Testament offers a nuanced perspective on
suffering and hope that resonates deeply with people of African
heritage. Scriptures such as Romans 8:18 which speaks of present
suffering not comparing to future glory, provide a framework for
understanding personal and collective struggles. UK scholars like Brown
and Hughes (2017) argue that these teachings help believers make sense
of their suffering while finding hope in their faith. Canadian
theologians like Harris and Young (2018) echo these sentiments, noting
that the promise of eternal life and divine justice provides great
psychological comfort. At times of struggles, sufferings and anxiety,
many Christians reflect on the theological themes in the Gospels for
hope, perseverance, peace of mind and answers to perplexing personal
questions.

Some reflect on Mark’s narrative themes of: The Kingdom of God,
Discipleship, Christ, Faith, Glory, Judgment, Love and Satan. Scholars
believe that Mark’s Gospel was written when the Church of Rome was
suffering severe persecution under emperor Nero about 64 B.C.E.,
therefore its context of persecution resonates with the Gospel’s purpose
and narrative themes. They resonate also with the direct experiences and
complaints of people of African heritage.

Mark’s Gospel is a call to faithful discipleship in the face of
suffering and even death. Jesus told his disciples and the people
“Whosoever will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his
cross, and follow me” *(Mark 8:34).* The feeding of the five thousand,
and the healing of the despised Syrophoenician woman’s daughter give
many Christians inspiration to fight for change and social justice while
bringing communities closer together.

Above all, ‘The Kingdom of God’ is a central theme in the Gospels, and
Jesus uses parables to illustrate its mystery. The Kingdom of God is
both a present reality and a future event. Jesus told his followers,
“Seek ye first the Kingdom of God” *(Matthew 6:33),* and “Take no
thought, saying What shall we eat? What shall we drink? or Wherewithal
shall we be clothed?” *(verse* *31)*. Theologians remind Christians that
Jesus’s teachings, though viewed figuratively requires faith and belief
in God’s power to remain resilient and steadfast in their efforts to act
collectively in helping themselves and others when faced with suffering
and unfulfilled needs.

**2.8.3 Cultural and Historical Context**

The historical trauma of slavery and systematic racism continues to
impact the mental health and well-being of people of African heritage.
New Testament-inspired Christianity provides a lens through which these
experiences can be understood and managed. Collins and Smith (2018)
highlight how religious narratives help individuals reinterpret their
historical trauma within a redemptive framework. Clarke and Thompson
(2019) argue that the New Testament emphasis on liberation and justice
resonates with African Canadian communities, helping individuals process
their experiences while fostering a sense of empowerment. In the USA,
the history of slavery and segregation has shaped the development of the
African American/Black Church as a place of resistance and resilience.

**2.9 Practical Implications for Mental Health and Mental Health Care**

The literature suggests several practical implications for mental health
services. UK experts, Davies and Martin recommend partnership between
mental health professionals and church leaders to provide holistic care,
since integrating faith-based approaches into mental health care can
enhance its effectiveness. Sewell and Pargament (2011) emphasise the
potential of incorporating Christian principles into therapeutic
practices, especially for African Americans who may mistrust secular
mental health services. Canadian researchers advocate for culturally
sensitive mental health interventions that acknowledge the importance of
spirituality (Johnson and Carter, 2021).

Doubtless, the Black church has been, and continues to be one of the
most important institutions in the African American community. Hays
(2015) noted that between 2009 and 2011, states such as California,
Illinois, Nevada and South Carolina have cut more than \$1.6 billions in
general funds from their mental health budgets, impacting on
professional services thereby prompting African Americans in need of
mental health support to continue searching for informal service
providers, such as churches. Hays concludes that the burden on churches
to meet mental health needs, and encourage members to seek appropriate
professional help, may expand.

The literature indicates that structured support groups and counselling
appear to be associated with higher levels of stigmatisation for African
Americans in crisis which means that they are more likely to turn first
to other informal and familiar supports, like the church (Allen, Davey,
& Davey, 2010; Thomas et al., 1994). It was observed that well-developed
trusting relationships can make utilising clergy over formalised
services more attractive. The literature shows also, that African
Americans who sought out clergy first were less likely to follow-up with
formalised services (Taylor et al, 2000). African American Churches
offered greater satisfaction to African Americans who experienced
emotional problems, death or illness (Neighbors, Musick, & Williams,
1998).

Researchers indicate that most health care practitioners, including
nurses and social workers in the U.S.A. must be willing to identify
external systems, like the Black church, and integrate them into
treatment approaches with juvenile sex and drug offenders when
appropriate. This would include creating relationships, through training
and education, with Black churches so that members can become more aware
of what resources are available in the formalised system of care.

**2.10 Conclusion**

This literature review highlights the significant contributions of New
Testament-inspired Christianity to the mental health of people of
African heritage in the UK, USA and Canada. Through theological
reflections, communal support and practical assistance, Christianity
offers a multifaceted approach to mental well-being. The intersection of
faith, community support and cultural context creates a unique framework
that enhances resilience, addresses stigma, strengthens faith and
provides hope.

Scholars including Beckford (2016) noted that branches of Christianity
provide a framework for understanding suffering, resilience and hope as
precursors to developing better mental health. Research by Watson and
Sharpe (2018) found that African-Caribbean Christians often turn to
their faith in times of crisis.

Similarly, Sewell and Pargament (2011) emphasise the potential of
integrating Christian principles into therapeutic practices, especially
for African Americans, some of whom may distrust secular mental health
services. These interventions, including pastoral counselling and
faith-based support groups were found to enhance treatment outcomes by
aligning therapeutic processes with the client’s spiritual beliefs. For
example, the African American (Black) church has historically been a
central institution in the lives of African Americans. They provide
spiritual guidance and a foundation for social and political activism.
Lincoln and Mamiya (1990) argued that the African American / Black
church plays an important role in fostering resilience and psychological
well-being among people of African heritage. These researchers, among
others, conclude that the church has been a vital source of emotional
support, helping individuals navigate the stresses of systemic racism,
poverty and social injustice.

Furthermore, the research of McKenzie et al (2015) that outlines the
role of Black churches in addressing mental health stigma within African
Canadian communities while advocating for greater collaboration between
mental health professionals and religious leaders, provide hope for more
African Canadians. The development of culturally sensitive interventions
that respect and incorporate the spiritual beliefs of African Canadian
clients will enable them to improve their mental health. As Williams and
Carter (2020) suggest, incorporating mental health education into church
programmes can help reduce stigma and foster open dialogues about mental
health to enable churches to play a crucial role in changing perceptions
and encouraging help-seeking behaviours.

Although the scope of this study does not allow for immediate further
research to explore the long-term impacts of the contributions outlined
above, particularly in rapid changing social and cultural contexts, and
to develop strategies for effectively integrating these insights into
mental health care practices, subsequent chapters of this thesis will
build on these insights above to further explore the specific
experiences and outcomes in these contexts, providing a more developed
comprehensive understanding of the interplay between New
Testament-inspired Christianity and mental health.

**Chapter 3: Part A - The New Testament and Mental Health**

**3.1 Teachings of Jesus Christ **

**3.1.1 Love and Compassion**

Love and compassion are espoused in the New Testament’s teachings of
Jesus Christ that are recorded specifically in the Gospels. Jesus’
commandment that “Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself” *(Mark
12:31)* and his injunction to love even your enemies *(Matthew 5:44),*
provide the basis for human-to-human relationship built on empathy and
understanding. For those with African heritage who might have been
enslaved through systematic discrimination or social exclusion, these
sermons go against the grain of straightforward personal forgiveness.
Yet, Jesus’ teachings evoke a type of soul searching that test one’s
faith in God and a willingness to follow in His footsteps, leading to a
personal commitment. This personal strengthening of faith comes from
Christ’s willingness to forgive His tormentors, even at the height of
His agony on the cross, when he prayed: “Father, forgive them; for they
do not know what they are doing” *(Luke 23:34).*

This Christian attitude of being compassionate and forgiving, eliminates
racism and leads to inclusivity and dignity. Accordingly, the emphasis
on unconditional love can foster self-acceptance and reduce internalised
negative stereotypes, thus contributing to better mental health for some
African Christians.

**3.1.2 Forgiveness and Reconciliation**

Forgiveness is another central theme in Jesus’ teachings. In the Lord’s
Prayer, Jesus instructs His followers to forgive others as they seek
forgiveness from God *(Matthew 6:12).* Practising forgiveness can help
alleviate feelings of anger, resentment and bitterness that are
detrimental to mental health. For individuals like those of African
heritage facing racial injustices, intimidation and rejection, the
process of forgiving, though challenging, can be a powerful tool for
personal liberation and psychological healing. It enables individuals to
break free from the cycle of extreme anxiety, worry or trauma while
moving towards emotional well-being.

**3.1.3 Hope and Redemption**

The New Testament narratives provide a message of hope and redemption.
Jesus’ resurrection is the cornerstone of Christian faith, symbolising
victory over death and sin *(1 Corinthians* *15:55-57*). This message of
hope can be particularly resonant for people of African heritage who
have endured historical and ongoing struggles. This message of hope is
so strong in the psyche of most Africans that they turn to special
hymns, prayers and gospel music to reinforce, publicise and strengthen
their beliefs while rejuvenating their spirit. Above all, many African
Christians proclaim that a belief in redemption and the promise of a
better future, do promote a sense of purpose and optimism that are
crucial for their mental health.

**3.1.4 Community and Belonging**

Jesus’ teachings also emphasise the importance of community. The early
Christian community, as described in *Acts 2:42-47*, exemplifies mutual
support and collective well-being. The Apostles reported, “All that
believed were together, and had all things common … continuing daily
with one accord …Praising God, and having favour with all the people”
*(Acts 2: 42-47).* This sense of belonging and communal support can
provide emotional stability and reduce feelings of isolation and
loneliness. Christians continue to build and strengthen this communal
spirit in novel ways, in the diasporic communities. A report entitled
“The Church and Social Cohesion: Connecting Communities and serving
people”, published on 26 November, 2020, outlines the findings of an
eighteen-month project investigating the influence of churches on
community cohesiveness across England.

The project, commissioned by the Free Churches Group, ‘The Churches
together in England’ (CTE), recommended that churches should utilise
their assets sensibly in participation with congregations, as
institutions grow from strength to strength through local involvement
with traditional church support services.

The report was welcomed by its executive team members, its Presidents,
including the Most Revd and Rt. Hon. Justin Welby, Archbishop of
Canterbury, and Bishop Tedroy Powell, the African British National
Overseer of the Redeemed Church of God of Prophecy in the UK. This
project exemplifies how churches are charting new ways in which to
engage more widely and appropriately with their communities. This
approach resonates with Black Churches in the diasporic communities that
understand the contemporary activism of “Black Lives Matter” groups in
the UK, USA and Canada that are concerned with achieving social justice
and peace that lie at the heart of mental health.

**3.2 Parables as Moral and Ethical Guides**

As this research continues to examine the effect of Jesus’ teachings on
the lives of Christians of African heritage, it discovers that the
Parables of Jesus, found in the Gospels, are rich with moral and ethical
teachings that can guide personal conduct and community interactions.
For instance, the Parable of the Good Samaritan *(Luke 10:25-37)*
challenges social prejudices and emphasises the importance of compassion
and mercy. For individuals of African heritage who may face racial
prejudices, this parable can reinforce the values of empathy and social
justice, promoting psychological resilience.

**3.2.1 Parable of the Lost Son**

The Parable of the Prodigal Son *(Luke 15:11-32)* highlights themes of
repentance, forgiveness and unconditional love. The narrative of a
father welcoming back his wayward son can resonate with individuals who
feel estranged or marginalised, offering a powerful message of
reconciliation and acceptance. The message of this parable applies to
individuals, families and church members who may have felt rejected for
mistakes they had made, and hope for forgiveness that will foster a
sense of self-worth and belonging that are essential for promoting
feelings of well-being and better mental health. This is true of many
people of African heritage, especially the younger generations who may
have been caught up in the misuse of drugs and alcohol or may have been
isolated by parents or other elders for disapproved behaviours and
decisions like teenage pregnancy.

**3.2.2 Parable of the Sower**

Furthermore, the parable of the Sower *(Mathew 13:1-23)* illustrates the
varying responses to the message of the Kingdom of God. It underscores
the importance of a receptive and resilient heart amidst life’s
challenges in the 21st century. For people of African heritage,
understanding that their faith can grow stronger despite adversities,
can be empowering, providing a spiritual framework for coping with daily
challenges, obstacles and setbacks while maintaining a feeling of
positivity.

**3.3 Miracles as Sources of Hope and Healing**

All four Gospels, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, record that Jesus
performed “mighty works,” the term used for miracles in the Synoptics.
Even the Jewish opponents of Jesus acknowledged his unique power *(Mark
3:22).* There were four types of miracles: exorcisms (casting out
demons), healing of physical ailments, raising the dead and nature
miracles such as walking on water or stilling the storm. The Gospel of
Mark tells us that the mighty works of Jesus made a profound impression
on the people of Galilee, in his early ministry. The Gospels emphasise
that the miracles were manifestations of the power of God.

Above all, the “mighty works” were not ends in themselves; they were
signs of the kingdom’s power. Scholars attest to Jesus’ teaching that
only those who first trusted God and the power of His kingdom could
understand the meaning of the miracles. As a matter of fact, one of the
dominant features of Jesus’ miracles was his repeated emphasis on faith
as the basis of healing. “Thy faith hath saved thee” is a common theme
told in the Gospels. Christians are expected to demonstrate their faith
in God’s power, to benefit from His divine interventions.

**3.3.1 Healing Miracles – Curing the blind and the lepers**

Jesus’ healing miracles, such as curing the blind (*John 9:1-12)* and
the lepers *(Luke 17:11-19),* demonstrate His compassion and the power
of God to restore health. For believers, like those of African heritage,
these narratives affirm the possibility of divine healing and encourage
the personal search for spiritual support alongside medical treatment.
The belief in a compassionate and healing God can reduce anxiety and
promote peace of mind.

**3.3.2 Miracles of Provision: Nature Miracles, Feeding the five
thousand**

Miracles of provision, such as the feeding of the five thousand
*(Matthew 14:13-21),* emphasise God’s care and provision. These
narratives can instil a sense of security, and reduce worries about
material needs which is prominent among people facing economic hardship.
The work of many charities like ‘Christian Aid’ or the ‘Salvation Army’
is viewed as acts of God or the manifestation of God’s power and
authority, inspiring people, in churches and organisations, or
individually, to help the needy, as Jesus did. Many people of African
heritage have had experiences of anxiety and worry due to poverty,
hunger and homelessness, and had sought help from community support
groups in churches, in the wider community or in the contemporary ‘food
banks’ that were created in times of extreme needs, during the Covid-19
pandemic of 2020-2021. Some of those Christians in need, proclaimed,
“God works in mysterious ways.”

**3.3.3 Resurrection Miracles – Resurrection of Lazarus and Jesus’ own
resurrection**

The resurrection of Lazarus *(John 11:1-44)* and Jesus’ own resurrection
are central to Christian hope in life after death and the ultimate
triumph over suffering. This eschatological hope can provide a profound
sense of peace and purpose, alleviating existential anxiety and despair.
The Gospels record three resurrection miracles, including the raising of
a widow’s son, of Jairus’ daughter and of Lazarus. This shows that God,
through Christ, can save any sinner regardless of age, period of
sinfulness or the seriousness of the sin.

Jesus performed miracles of resurrection that demonstrated His victory
over sin and the grave. The miracles performed by Christ were signs that
He was approved by God *(Acts 2:22);* His resurrection sealed the fact
that His people are accepted by God *(Romans 1:4; 4:25; 1 Peter*
*1:21).* Without the resurrection from the dead, “We are all men most
miserable” (*1 Corinthians* 15:19).

Scripture makes abundantly clear that the triune God was involved in
Jesus’ resurrection. Firstly, the Father raised Him from the dead *(Acts
2:24; 3:15; 10:40; Ephesians 1:20).* Here the Father showed that He
accepted the work and Person of the Son by raising Him from the dead.
*Romans 6:4* puts it splendidly: “Christ was raised up from the dead by
the glory of the Father…”, the Holy Spirit. Christians believe that
these resurrection miracles teach that there is life after death,
besides victory over pain; they give hope and inspiration to overcome
suffering.

**3.4 The Apostolic Letters**

Furthermore, New Testament Christianity regards the Apostolic Letters as
foundational texts. The letters were written primarily to early
Christian communities by the Apostles Paul, Peter, John and other
notable individuals, and as such they contain theological insights as
well as practical guidelines for these communities. Among themes
discussed in these letters, there are faith, community, perseverance,
and hope which resonate with individuals of African heritage living in
the diaspora trying to make sense out of who they are and what
spirituality means to them. 

**3.4.1 Theme of Faith and Perseverance**

The term ‘Faith’ has been used in different contexts in this study,
therefore it is necessary to decide on a succinct definition that may
enhance our understanding of its use. Let us examine the scripture! The
Apostolic letters present faith as a resolve of staying steadfast during
trials *(Romans 5:3-5).* The Gospel of Luke presents the woman with ‘the
issue of blood,’ as one who displayed her faith by pushing through the
crowd, in the belief that she would be healed if only she touched the
border of Jesus’ garment. After she had done so, Jesus turned around and
asked, “Who touched me?” but for a while, most of His followers denied
touching Jesus, until the woman presented herself, and explained to
Jesus the nature of her suffering and the result of her action. Jesus
told her, “Daughter, be of good comfort: thy faith hath made thee whole;
go in peace” *(Luke 8:43-48).* In this case, we can see that a sign of
faith is being intentional. Faith is having a clear goal in mind that
leads one to concentrate all one’s energy on achieving it.

Similarly, the healing of the ten lepers indicates their display of
faith by going to show themselves to the priests, as Jesus instructed,
before they had visual evidence of being healed. The Apostle Luke tells
us that the lepers called out to Jesus, from a distance, “Master, have
mercy on us” *(Luke 17:13),* and as Jesus looked on them, he said, “Go
shew yourselves unto the priests” *(Luke 17:14).* The lepers were
cleansed as they walked to show themselves to the priests who had the
authority to declare them fit to re-engage in their community. The
single leper who returned to thank Jesus while glorifying God, was told
by Jesus, “Go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole” *(Luke 17:19).*

Apostle Paul taught that faith is confidence in what we hope for and
assurance about what we do not see. This is demonstrated in the
attitudes of the woman with the issue of blood and the ten lepers who
were all healed by Jesus. In the light of these teachings, Christians
believe that living faith is demonstrated by service and obedience to
God. However, according to Apostle James in his letters to the believers
in Jerusalem, “by works a man is justified, and not by faith only”
(*James 2:24).*

Mark records Jesus telling his disciples, “Have faith in God …What
things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and
ye shall have them” (*Mark 11:22-24).* This advice resonates with
Christians of African heritage who appeal to God, through prayer,
worship and meditation with confidence that he will fulfil His promises.
One can infer that these Christians have strong personal belief in God’s
power, and in Jesus as God incarnate. *John 10:38* records Jesus saying
to the Jews who challenged His authority, “Though ye believe not me,
believe the works: that ye may know, and believe, that the Father is in
me, and I in him.” This indicates that knowledge is an essential element
of Christian faith and is sometimes spoken of as an equivalent to faith.

The Apostolic letters emphasise enduring faith in the face of trials.
Christians were encouraged to “Stand and rejoice in hope of the glory of
God… We glory in tribulations also, knowing that tribulation worketh
patience; and patience, experience and experience, hope; And hope maketh
not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the
Holy Ghost, *(Romans 5:2-5).*

This thesis uses the term ‘faith’ as a strong personal belief in God’s
power and in Jesus incarnate. It is having confidence in what is hoped
for with the assurance that hopes will be realised. Having faith in
God’s power to answer prayers and satisfy spiritual requests, aligns
with the historical and contemporary challenges confronting African
heritage communities, reinforcing resilience and perseverance.

**3.4.2 Theme of Hope and Healing**

This is underscored in the Letter to the Philippians where people are
called as disciples to dwell “on whatsoever things are true, whatsoever
things are is honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are
pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good
report, if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on
these things” (*Philippians 4:8).* This letter encourages believers to
focus on positive outcomes. This emphasis on hope can be particularly
therapeutic, offering a counter-narrative to the trauma experienced by
many in the diaspora.

**3.4.3 Theme of Community Support and love**

Many letters emphasise the importance of communal bonds, “Now this I
say, that every one of you saith, I am Paul; and I of Apollos, and I of
Cephas, and I of Christ” *(1 Corinthians 12).* Similarly, in *Galatians
6:2*, Apostle Paul urges believers to “Bear ye one another’s burdens,
and so fulfil the law of Christ,” highlighting the importance of mutual
support. For Christians of African heritage, these messages reinforce
the significance of the church community that often provides a crucial
support network that fosters mental well-being amidst societal
challenges.

**3.5 How the Integration of Faith and Spiritual Practices affect Mental
Health**

**3.5.1 Faith in New Testament Christianity**

The interaction between Mental Health, faith and spirituality is an area
that has attracted significant scholarly attention to examine how faith
and spiritual convictions serve as resistance against the effects of
psychological problems. Research shows that faith and spirituality play
specific roles in the lives of Africans in the diaspora. Let us now
examine the theme of ‘spiritual Practices’.

**3.5.2. Spiritual Practices in New Testament Christianity**

In the context of New Testament Christianity, a spiritual practice is an
activity or ritual that a believer or Christian performs regularly to
strengthen their spirituality and connect with God or their inner self.
Spiritual practices may vary across cultures and branches of
Christianity, and believers can practise them for a variety of reasons
including personal growth, strengthening one’s faith, healing,
developing hope, or for guidance in making important decisions.

The main spiritual practices include prayer, worship, baptism, Bible
study, fasting, confession, meditation, observing the sabbath, hymn
singing, gospel singing, taking Holy Communion /celebrating the
Eucharist, prayer meetings, harvest celebration, thanksgiving, charity
donations and funeral services, among others.

Furthermore, Christians believe that participation in these spiritual
practices enable them to develop spiritual discipline that may lead to
the strengthening of their faith while bringing them closer to God,
thereby promoting feelings of well-being. Research shows that while most
Christians may try to participate in many of these spiritual practices,
only a few are highlighted as being commonly featured in their daily
lives. These are praying, scripture or Bible reading, hymn singing,
worship, celebrating the Eucharist, making confession, prayer meetings
and gospel singing. Let us examine how these spiritual practices
contribute to the mental health of people of African heritage in the
diaspora.

**3.5.3 Faith-based Interventions and Spiritual Practices**

Faith and spiritual practices are intertwined with each other in New
Testament Christianity; they can be seen as therapeutic interventions
for individuals and communities. Both concepts play a crucial role in
promoting mental, emotional and spiritual health by giving believers a
framework for understanding and managing suffering, pain, worry and
anxiety while enhancing communion between believers and faith Leaders or
the Clergy.

1.  **Faith as a Therapeutic Intervention**

- The Apostle, Paul tells Christians that “Faith is the substance of
  things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen *(Hebrews 11:1).*
  Christians believe that faith in God provides them with hope and a
  sense of purpose which can be crucial in facing life's challenges.
  Scriptures emphasise the importance of faith as assurance and
  conviction that can foster resilience, helping individuals to cope
  with stress and adversity.

- Faith offers psychological comfort to believers in times of distress.
  New Testament texts often encourage believers to cast their anxieties
  upon God. “In every thing by prayer and supplication …let your request
  be known unto God…And the peace of God, which passeth all
  understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus”
  *(Philippians 4:6-7).* New Testament Christians like many of African
  heritage, remember that Jesus prayed often, and sometimes alone. Jesus
  prayed alone in the wilderness for forty days and at the mount of
  Olives *(Luke 22:39).* In the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus told his
  disciples “Sit ye here, while I shall pray” (*Mark 13: 32*), therefore
  individualised prayer is an essential feature in individuals’ daily
  lives. Some believers who feel isolated, hopeless, downtrodden or
  discouraged may pray or meditate to gain comfort. Others may
  participate in singing hymns and spirituals, or may listen to gospel
  music, besides joining communal spiritual practices.

- Faith establishes a sense of identity and belonging within a
  community. The New Testament emphasises the communal aspects of faith,
  as in the *Acts of the Apostles* where believers support and uplift
  one another, reinforcing social support that is essential for mental
  well-being. This spiritual practice can alleviate feelings of
  isolation, fear and anxiety.

**3.5.3.2 Spiritual Practices as Therapeutic Interventions**

Spiritual practices as therapeutic interventions relate to activities
carried out in the context of New Testament Christianity to treat, cure
or reduce the negative effects of mental health conditions such as
severe anxiety, stress, depression, substance misuse and various forms
of emotional sufferings. These practices include the following:

- Prayer which is a primary spiritual practice in New Testament
  Christianity. It serves as a way of communicating with God, allowing
  believers to express their feelings, seek guidance, and find inner
  peace. Christians follow in Jesus’ footsteps and pray to God for
  themselves and others; “Pray one for another, that ye may be healed”
  (*James 5:16).* This suggests that prayer can bring healing, both
  emotionally and physically, reflecting the therapeutic nature of
  spiritual communication.

- Regular group meetings of Christians for worship that provide communal
  support, and reinforce collective identity. The act of worship can
  uplift believers, instilling a sense of shared purpose and hope.
  *Colossians 3:16* encourages believers to sing psalms and hymns, which
  can serve as a method of emotional expression and healing.

- Scripture Reading and Meditation that are common practices among
  Christians. Reading the Bible or studying a New Testament text
  encourages believers to reflect on their lives in the light of God’s
  word. Such practices enable them to think deeply about Jesus’
  teachings, healings and attitude towards the children of God.
  Meditation on passages such as *Matthew 11:28-30,* where Jesus invites
  the weary to find rest in Him, can promote mental and spiritual
  healing.

- Christians’ Acts of love and Service like feeding the hungry, offering
  shelter to the homeless or contributing to organisations that care for
  the homeless. Other Acts like offering unwanted personal items to
  charitable organisations, or spending time visiting the sick or
  befriending those who are lonely, resonate with Jesus’ teachings
  recorded in *Matthew 25:35-40*, “For I was an hungered, and you gave
  me meat. I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink. I was a stranger, and ye
  took me in.” Serving others can enhance the sense of fulfilment and
  purpose while also promoting positive mental health through social
  connectedness.

**3.5.4 The Role of Clergy and Faith Leaders**

This section reiterates the specific contributions of Christian Clergy
and Faith leaders to the mental health of African heritage individuals.
It reviews how the clergy and faith leaders integrate New Testament
teachings with practical support for mental health issues such as
emotional distress, grief, depression, anxiety, and trauma. The role of
the pastor is multifaceted: providing spiritual guidance, offering
counselling services, and fostering a sense of community and belonging.

**3.5.4.1 Pastoral Care and Counselling**

Pastoral care in African diaspora communities often involves more than
just spiritual guidance; it includes addressing social and emotional
issues that affect individuals' mental health. Clergy and faith leaders
are seen as figures of authority and are trusted to offer advice on both
spiritual and personal matters. Some pastors take on formal counselling
roles, either as part of their pastoral duties or in collaboration with
mental health professionals. Studies suggest that African heritage
individuals are more likely to seek mental health support from religious
leaders than from mental health professionals due to cultural factors
such as mistrust of formal mental health systems (Abdullah et al.,
2016).

**3.5.4.2 Promoting Psychological Resilience**

Christian clergy in African diaspora communities often emphasise the
themes of resilience and overcoming adversity. Scriptures such as "I can
do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me" *(Philippians
4:13),* are frequently cited to encourage individuals to persevere
through mental health challenges. This emphasis on resilience and faith
in God’s plan can be both protective and therapeutic, fostering hope and
coping mechanisms in times of psychological or emotional distress.

**3.5.4.3 Spiritual and Social Support**

Christian communities offer a unique form of social support. Through the
church, individuals receive not only spiritual enrichment, but also a
sense of belonging and community. This can reduce feelings of isolation,
which is particularly important for people of African heritage who may
experience cultural alienation or marginalization in the diaspora.
Clergy and faith leaders facilitate support groups, prayer meetings, and
community events that enhance the social fabric of these communities,
and promote collective healing.

**3.5.5 Integration**

The integration of faith and spiritual practices enhances their
therapeutic benefits. For instance, faith can deepen the significance of
prayer, making it a more profound source of comfort and strength,
personal or communal. Similarly, spiritual practices can reinforce
faith, creating a cycle of support. Through community, worship, and
mutual care, Christians of African heritage can find therapeutic avenues
for dealing with life's challenges.

Furthermore, the integration of church-based therapy with professional
services could deepen and strengthen the holistic care that would
benefit the mental health of Christians within the African diaspora.

**3.5.6 Conclusion**

The literature show that the relationship between faith and spiritual
practices in New Testament Christianity acts as a framework for
therapeutic interventions that address mental, emotional, and spiritual
well-being. This holistic approach provides believers like those of
African heritage with the tools to navigate life's challenges,
instilling hope, fostering community spirit while facilitating personal
and communal healing.

**3.6 Empirical Evidence**

**3.6.1 Positive Impacts of Religious Involvement**

Numerous studies have shown that religious involvement is associated
with various positive mental health outcomes including reduced
depression and anxiety, enhanced coping mechanisms, besides community
support, cohesion and hope through Gospel music.

**3.6.1.1 Reduced Depression and Anxiety**

Lower levels of depression and anxiety have been associated with regular
participation in religious activities and practices such as individual
and communal prayer, worship, bible study or scripture reading,
meditation, hymn singing, confession and listening to gospel music.
Further consideration will be given later to these traumatic experiences
among people of African heritage, but at this stage, it is worth noting
that a study by Smith et al (2003) found that individuals who frequently
attend religious services report better mental health than those who do
not, thus reducing the incidence of depression and anxiety.

**3.6.1.2 Enhanced Coping Mechanisms**

New Testament Christianity provides coping mechanisms that can mitigate
the impact of stress. For example, prayer and scripture reading offer
peace and hope during challenging times. Christians follow in Jesus’
footsteps; they pray to God for others and themselves. As we note again
in *James 5:16*, “Pray one for another, that ye may be healed”. Many
believers reveal that praying with lighted candles while burning
incense, help to create a spiritual and peaceful atmosphere that aids
communication with God. Many Christians of African heritage claim that
these practices help them develop resilience and emotional strength
through prayer and scripture readings.

**3.6.1.3. Community Support, Cohesion and Hope through Gospel Music**

Religious communities often provide strong social networks that offer
emotional and practical support which aid mental health. The African
diaspora regards the church as a centre of community life, fostering
cohesion and hope for peace and comfort through prayer, gospel music and
singing hymns. Therefore, it is now prudent to investigate the influence
that Gospel music continues to have on the mental health of the
diasporic communities in the UK, USA and Canada.

**3.6.1.4. Gospel Music**

Many churches in the diaspora are enhancing their use of Gospel hymns
and Gospel Music that promote mental health among people of African
heritage, through Jesus’ teachings of hope and redemption, reflected in
hymns and spirituals. Gospel Music has been one of the most articulate
expressions of Spiritual belief and Cultural identity among black people
in America, UK, Canada and the Caribbean. It has had profound influence
on black people all over the world. 

This part of the thesis discusses the origin of Gospel music; how it has
changed over time and what it has contributed to black life among
different English-speaking nations like the UK, Caribbean countries, USA
and Canada, discussing its importance as a means of cultural retention,
social change and communal cohesion. 

**3.6.1.5 Origin of Gospel Music in the USA**

Music is said to be a universal language! It expresses what cannot be
put into words, and that which cannot remain silent. Music, whether
vocal or instrumental, is being used by people in various situations,
including festivities, ceremonials, rituals, religious services and
particularly in situations of struggles and sufferings.

From the Middle Ages when rulers like King Charlemagne who enslaved
Europeans, to the year 1001 when the armies of Mahmud and Ghazna
enslaved over 100,000 Indian children and young people, music has been
used as a means of communicating inner peace while enhancing resilience
and feelings of hope. The same can be said of those Europeans, Jews,
Turkish, Indonesians, Koreans and Indigenous Aborigines who were
enslaved in the Middle Ages under the Tang Dynasty of China, and
Europeans enslaved by the Vikings. They all used music through chanting
or group songs for mental relief and tolerance.

Similarly, enslaved Africans, taken to America, beginning with twenty
men taken to Jamestown, VA., in 1619 and others taken to British
colonies over many years, used musical instruments and spirituals to
comfort themselves, and to engender group support, in times of
sufferings and struggle for freedom. The rhythmic sounds were comforting
and gave slaves mental strength to carry-on. Gradually, those spirituals
were developed into Gospel music.

Gospel music originated early in the 20th century in the United States
among the African Americans / Blacks, primarily at church. It has its
roots in spirituals, formerly called ‘Negro Spirituals’ then ‘African
American spirituals’, sung by African slaves who incorporated African
musical traditions and Christian themes. Enslaved Africans used some of
the Christian hymns that were sung in small churches by their Caucasian
slave masters or plantation owners, and blended them with African songs
to create work-songs that became known as Negro spirituals, then Gospel
songs. Those spirituals were the precursors for the development of
gospel music due to their combination of strong emotional impact
together with religious fervour. Gospel songs / Gospel hymns that
reflect the Christian teachings that people of African heritage used in
times of sufferings, or when their days were dark and dreary, include:
“Jesus taught me how to smile,” sung by sister Rosetta; “Jesus taught me
how to laugh,” by Willie Nelson; “Jesus, you taught me…every smile is a
blessing…” and “Oh Happy Day, when Jesus washed my sins away.”

**3.6.1.6 Influences and Development of Gospel Music**

Various genres influenced Gospel music including blues, jazz and hymns
reflecting the socio-political landscape of the time. That blending of
styles became possible when African Americans moved from the South to
the Northern urban centres during the Great Migration period. Churches
became one of the main institutions through which gospel music spread
and as such, they provided platforms for choirs and solo artists. Later
organisations like the Gospel Music Workshop of America (GMWA), founded
by James Cleveland in 1968, enhanced its formulation, thereby promoting
growth within musical genre that has therefore benefited successive
generation of artists. 

**3.6.1.7 The Golden Age (1930s -1960s) **

The Golden Age of Gospel Music (1930s–960s) was marked by such
outstanding African Americans as Mahalia Jackson, Thomas Dorsey and
Sister Rosetta Tharpe. Dorsey, who is often referred to as ‘Father of
Gospel Music’, combined blues elements with spiritual tunes, leading to
a distinctive sound that appealed to both church goers and secular
audiences. Similarly, Sister Rosetta Tharpe, singer and songwriter, was
the first great recording artist of Gospel music, and was among the
first gospel musicians to appeal to rhythm and blues and rock and roll
audiences.

**3.6.1.8 Gospel Music in the UK **

Gospel music entered the UK in the post-World War 11 era when Caribbean
people immigrated there. For instance, through the arrival of the
Windrush generation from Caribbean islands between 1948 and 1973, Gospel
music and Pentecostal hymns were quickly adopted by Black British
communities. Similarly, Sister Rosetta Tharpe was instrumental in
developing Gospel music in Liverpool, England where listeners welcomed
her skilful guitar playing that gave gospel music a robust and lively
expression of faith, enlivening, yet soothing the souls of listeners.

Today, UK gospel music has blossomed over recent years, featuring both
traditional and modern influences. Both local and global audiences have
resonated with this scene which has been characterised by artists like
the ‘London Community Gospel Choir’ or bands like ‘Elevation
Worship’. Contemporary Artists like American, Bebe Winans keep the
prominence of Gospel hymns with his new rendition of “Jesus (Jesus)
forever the same…that all is forgiven in Jesus name”.

Another group, ‘The Liverpool Lighthouse Choir’, formed by Liverpool
Lighthouse in the UK, during Covid-19 pandemic (2020-2021) to connect
the community through singing, and to help people stay well during
lockdown, built on the tradition of the ‘Love and Joy Gospel Choir’
(LJGC) who have been singing for over twenty years.

The LJGC, a multi-racial group that appears on several national
television platforms and radio, are also featured in a new BBC Learning
series for primary schools - ‘My Life, My Religion’. This new Liverpool
Lighthouse Gospel Choir, like many others, has added Afro-beats to many
traditional hymns and songs to make them more accessible to the younger
generation, especially those of mixed heritage. This new gospel choir,
headed by an ex-barrister, is also introducing gospel music to primary
schools, in addition to recording songs, besides engaging in church
services and national competitions, thereby spreading joy and
inspiration among multi-cultural and multi-ethnic communities.

**3.6.1.9a Gospel Music in the Caribbean: Cultural Integration and
notable figures**

In the Caribbean, gospel music intersects with indigenous music forms
resulting in unique faith expressions. Hymns from Pentecostal, Moravian
and Baptist churches are being sung to Afro-beat. Art forms like
Calypso, Reggae and Soca, incorporate Gospel themes thus reflecting an
array of spiritualities in the region. 

Some artists such as Slinger Francisco, the calypsonian ‘Mighty Sparrow’
and Garfield Blackman, the calypsonian ‘Lord Shorty’- creator of
‘Soca’-both from Trinidad and Tobago, together with Jimmy Cliff and
Robert Marley from Jamaica, have included gospel messages into their
music, thereby championing themes of hope and social justice. Bob
Marley’s “Redemption song” with its lines, “…songs of freedom
…emancipate yourself from mental slavery…,” resonate with people of
African heritage, worldwide. This region’s Gospel scene is still vibrant
and growing with modern churches going contemporary on this type of
music, thereby enhancing the exuberance with the spiritual and mental
development of people of African heritage, beside the growing
international audience.

**3.6.1.9b Gospel Music in Canada: Development, Growth and community
impact**

In Canada, gospel music has experienced significant growth, particularly
within Black churches. A rich tapestry of influences characterises the
Canadian gospel community, from traditional hymns to hip hop and Rhythm
and Blues (R & B). 

The Gospel Music Association of Canada is an example of an organization
promoting gospel music and community involvement through music artists.
For instance, this is the platform used for celebrating the achievements
of gospel musicians during GMA Canada Covenant Awards which also serves
as a stage for upcoming musicians. 

**3.6.1.9c Contributions to Social Activism: A voice for Justice **

Historically, gospel music played a role in the fight for equality among
black people who used it as a medium through which their voices could be
heard, especially during the Civil Rights movement in America. Many
spirituals and Gospel songs were sung during that period when activists,
including mixed races, advocated for justice and equality. 

Furthermore, there has been a tradition of activism regarding gospel
music which is evident even in the UK, USA, Caribbean and Canada since
it is used as a tool to address racial inequality, poverty and community
empowerment. This genre inspires movements that are aimed at changing
conditions for the better, hence it remains a powerful tool for social
transformation, and for building resilience.

**3.6.1.9d Contributions to Social Advancement: A voice for
celebration **

As we have seen, Gospel Music has been used historically as a tool for
enhancement, socially and culturally, in black communities, mainly in
the U.S.A. Celebrated artists like Mahalia Jackson, Ray Charles, Aretha
Franklyn, Jimmy Cliff, Richard Wayne Penniman known as ‘Little Richard’,
Roberta Flack and Whitney Houston , to name a few, successfully started
their careers as young children singing the hymns and spiritual tunes in
Black churches, before jazz, soul, then R & B were added to bring about
a revolution in Gospel music that helped to enhance their careers and
global reception.

**3.6.1.9e Conclusion **

Moreover, the diasporic church in the UK, USA, and Canada serves also as
an important source of social connections for African immigrants who
need community support to facilitate emotional balance while reducing
loneliness and isolation, thereby promoting mental health.

In this respect, many Black churches have incorporated Gospel Music in
their services, with Afro-beat and R&B added to progress favourite or
traditional songs, thus making them more accessible, especially to the
younger generations in different diasporic and multi-ethnic communities.

**3.6.2 Negative Impacts and Challenges**

Above all, New Testament-derived Christianity, as a dominant force in
many communities of African heritage in the UK, USA and Canada, has been
instrumental in shaping both individual and collective psychological
well-being. However, despite the numerous benefits, there are potential
negative impacts of religious involvement on mental health. For example,
religious teachings, particularly those related to sin, guilt and
spiritual warfare, often generate conflicts with contemporary
psychological practices. These conflicts can lead to a reluctance to
engage with professional mental health services, especially in
communities where faith leaders and religious beliefs hold significant
authority.

**3.6.2.1 Religious Guilt and Sin**

New Testament Christianity emphasises the concepts of sin, redemption
and divine judgment. For many believers of African heritage, especially
in the context of their religious upbringing, mental health struggles or
challenges are often deemed manifestations of personal sin or spiritual
weakness. For example, when Romans 8:17 and *1 Peter 4:12* portray
suffering as part of the Christian experience, some believers feel
guilty and ashamed if they experience anxiety, depression or other
mental health crisis.

Furthermore, in some African communities, the experience of mental
illness may be perceived as a moral failing or a spiritual issue that
requires prayers, deliverance or exorcism rather than professional
psychological intervention (Mowah and Henderson, 2016). This belief is
often reinforced by some Clergy and Faith leaders who may emphasise the
need for spiritual renewal through prayer, fasting and spiritual warfare
(fight against evil forces or demons), rather than acknowledging the
biological or psychological underpinnings of mental health. The emphasis
on personal sin can stigmatise Christians who are struggling with a
mental crisis, leading them to hide their difficulties or reject
professional mental health treatment altogether, due to perceived shame
or fear of judgment (Okeke, 2014).

**3.6.2.2 Fear of Witchcraft and Spiritual Warfare**

In several African heritage communities, particularly those from
Pentecostal, Afro-Caribbean Baptist and other charismatic traditions,
there is a prevalent belief in spiritual warfare, including the idea
that mental health issues can be caused by demonic forces or witchcraft.
This belief system often contradicts contemporary understanding of
mental health that is influenced by biology, environmental factors and
psychology. For example, depression may be attributed to demonic
oppression and/or ancestral curses, rather than being recognised as a
mental health condition that could be treated with therapy and
medication (Asamoah,2019). As a result, individuals may be afraid to
seek or accept medical, psychiatric or psychological help because they
think that could result in spiritual attack or abandoning their faith.

Such fears may discourage believers from seeking professional therapy or
counselling, because they view mental health professionals as outsiders
or even enemies of their spiritual well-being. This may be true in some
immigrant communities where additional cultural alienation exists, or
distrust of Western medical systems prevails. In instances where someone
may have had a relative or friend sectioned under the Mental Health Act
(1983) or the Mental Health Act (2007) without understanding the need
for urgent care, may have publicised disdain for such support.
Furthermore, the fear of spiritual harm can compound peoples’ reluctance
to engage with conventional mental health practices, with church leaders
often advising congregants to rely mainly on prayer and faith for
healing (Olayiwola,2020).

**3.6.2.3 More Conflicts between religious teachings and modern mental
health practices**

**3.6.2.3a Stigma, Faith and the Church**

There are cultural and religious conflicts that plague many Christians
of African heritage within diasporic communities that experience a
profound sense of cultural pride and solidarity. This pride and
solidarity are often tied to religious identity. Consequently, mental
health challenges can be seen as a personal or familial failure, leading
to further stigma. One recalls that in many African Christian contexts,
the church is not only a place of spiritual healing, but also a central
institution that maintains social order and community cohesion.
Believers often turn to the church for leadership, even in some aspects
of making personal decisions about seeking help.

The reluctance to engage with secular mental health services is
compounded by the belief that modern mental health support /treatment
may undermine the believer’s relationship with God, which is often seen
as the ultimate source of healing (Akinbobola, 2017). In these
communities, mental health professionals may be viewed with suspicion,
particularly if they recommend treatments or interventions that
challenge deeply held religious convictions.

For example, counselling techniques that focus on self-empowerment or
address personal issues through introspection may be regarded as being
incompatible with Christian humility which encourages submission to
God’s will. Such conventional /professional practices might be seen as
‘worldly’ or ‘secular’ approaches that detract from the divine power of
prayer and the Holy Spirit (Olorunnisola, 2018). In extreme cases,
individuals may feel that seeking professional help is an indication of
weak faith or lack of trust in God’s power to heal.

**3.6.2.3b The impact of African Cultural Contexts**

The African heritage diaspora in the UK, USA and Canada is diverse,
encompassing individuals from a wide range of cultural backgrounds. In
spite of the geographical differences, the religious attitudes towards
mental health tend to be shaped by a common African worldview that
emphasises communalism, spiritualism and an interconnectedness between
the spiritual and physical realms. This cultural context further
exacerbates the uneasiness between Christian religious teachings and
modern mental health practices, because traditional beliefs about
illness and healing may differ significantly from contemporary
psychological approaches. For example, many African heritage communities
believe in the interdependence of family and community in managing
mental health, rather than relying on professional, individualistic
model of care. In this context, seeking external help may be regarded as
abandoning one’s familial or communal responsibilities, reinforcing the
belief that such issues should be handled within the church or extended
family networks (Adams,. 2020). The individual’s mental health struggles
are thus considered within the wider community context which can lead to
a reluctance to seek professional care that may be perceived as
undermining local traditional collective healing.

**3.6.2.3c Conclusion**

New Testament Christianity’s emphasis on guilt, sin, spiritual warfare
and community cohesion creates a complex and occasional conflicting
relationship with modern mental health practices. African heritage
communities in the UK, USA and Canada that may be influenced by these
teachings or beliefs, could struggle with feelings of guilt, fear and
shame when dealing with mental health issues. This cultural and
religious framework often leads to the rejection of professional mental
health services in favour of prayer and spiritual remedies, including
water baptism and fasting. Furthermore, this preference may deter some
individuals from seeking the professional care that they need.

A more nuanced understanding of these dynamics is necessary for mental
health professionals to bridge the gap between religious beliefs and
therapeutic practices, and to create a more culturally appropriate
approach to the care of people of African heritage.

**3.6.2.4a Specific Mental Health Conditions**

As stated earlier, the relationship of religious beliefs and mental
health has been a subject of increasing interest in psychological and
sociological research, hence this thesis. For people of African heritage
living in the UK, USA and Canada, New Testament-derived Christianity
brings significant influence in shaping worldviews, coping strategies
and addressing mental health problems such as anxiety, depression,
post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), substance abuse, schizophrenia,
suicide and domestic abuse. Furthermore, Christianity, in its various
forms, has been essential in providing spiritual support for African
diasporic communities bearing historic memories of trauma, systematic
oppression, and marginalisation. However, the extent to which religious
awareness, knowledge and practice influence mental health outcomes in
these populations constitutes a major multifaceted area for
consideration, as this study shows.

This research continues to explore the impact of New Testament-derived
Christianity on the mental health of people of African heritage living
in specific Western countries, examining the beneficial and detrimental
effects of religious engagement.

In this respect, previous findings show that the impact of Christianity
on mental health can be broken down into three main interrelated
components:

- Religious Awareness: The recognition and acknowledgement of one’s
  faith.

- Religious Knowledge: The depth of understanding individuals/believers
  have of their religious texts, doctrines and practices.

- Religious Practice: Religious activities such as praying, worshipping,
  scripture reading, Bible Study and celebrating the Eucharist.

In the context of Christianity, these components derive mainly from the
New Testament teachings on suffering, hope, salvation, fellowship and
intervention outlined in the writings of the Apostle Paul and the books
of Philippians, Romans and Corinthians. Additionally, these writings
provide believers with more insights into coping with suffering and
mental distress through a positive and trusting relationship with Christ
and the Church. Furthermore, the Gospels’ accounts of Jesus’ healing
ministry present a constant leitmotif in Christian understandings of
mental and psychological restoration.

A closer examination of some of these mental health conditions that are
most prevalent in African heritage communities, often exacerbated by
historical trauma, racial discrimination, socio-economic factors and
cultural dislocation, will be done here.

**3.6.2.4b Anxiety and Depression**

The New Testament offers comfort for believers struggling with anxiety
and depression. *Philippians 4:6-7* encourages Christians to “Be careful
for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with
thanksgiving, let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of
God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds
through Christ Jesus.” This passage demonstrates the Christian practice
of prayer as a means of combating anxiety and emotional distress
(depression). It also offers hope. Similarly, the Letter of James 1:2-4,
“My brethren …the trying of your faith worketh patience …let patience
have her perfect work …wanting nothing…”, encourages perseverance in
trials, suggesting that sufferings can produce spiritual growth and
resilience. These teachings and practices resonate with African
believers in the diaspora who have experiences of severe anxiety and
depression.

3**.6.2.5 Post Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)**

Post Traumatic Stress Disorder is widespread among people of African
heritage due to the legacy of slavery, forced migration and ongoing
racial trauma. It may manifest in signs and symptoms that include:

- vivid flashbacks,

- nightmares, disturbed sleep or lack of sleep,

- intrusive thoughts or images,

- anxiety disorders,

- palpitations,

- irritability or aggressive behaviour,

- panicking and/or being jumpy,

- headaches, stomach aches and dizziness,

- physical sensation such as pain, sweating and nausea,

- being easily upset,

- misuse of alcohol and drugs,

- poor concentration, poor memory,

- neglect of self with malnutrition,

- overwhelming feeling of shame, guilt, anger or sadness,

- self-harm,

- depression,

- suicidal feelings and dissociation.

It is normal to experience confusing and upsetting thoughts after a
traumatic event, but most people improve naturally within a short time –
days or a few weeks. However, when these abnormal patterns of behaviour
persist, and individuals become incapable of managing themselves or
their daily activities, PTSD may have developed. Individuals showing
signs of PTSD, or those experiencing symptoms of the disorder, may seek
medical or spiritual advice, or may be referred by a friend or relative.

The literature shows that Christianity offers a platform for healing
through spiritual forgiveness, redemption and hope for reparation*.
Romans 12:2* advises Christians not to be “Conformed to this world; but
be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what
is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God.” Here,
Christians are called upon for a transformation of the mind with
emphasis on Christ’s power to heal and restore brokenness, in keeping
with Jesus’ assurance, “Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy
laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me;
for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your
souls. For my yoke is easy, and your burden is light” *(Matthew*
*11:28-30).*

However, some Christians may find it difficult to reconcile their pain
and suffering with a belief in a benevolent God, leading to a crisis in
faith. In such circumstances, some believers may confess their doubts
and/or anxiety to their priests or faith leaders who may lead them in
prayer, or develop a counselling session to help the believers reassert
their faith and belief in God. Similarly, personal prayer and communal
worship often help individuals to strengthen their faith and resolve,
leading to trust in the power of God to protect the hopeful.

**3.6.2.6 Substance Abuse**

“Substance abuse is the use of illegal drugs or the use of prescription
or over-the-counter drugs or alcohol for purposes other than those for
which they are meant to be used, or in excessive amounts” (NCI). Many
individuals in the UK, USA and Canada, including some of African
heritage, resort to the use of illegal drugs and the excessive use of
alcohol due to socio-economic, cultural and psychological factors. This
has raised social and health concerns about managing and overcoming
substance dependence, thus enhancing the interplay between Christian
beliefs and professional substance abuse recovery services, focusing on
how faith-based interventions complement critical care, and in some
cases, offer a pathway to healing in ways that resonate with the
cultural and historical experiences of people of African heritage.

Some people in African heritage communities have experienced systemic
oppression, historical trauma, segregation, extreme stress and
socio-economic disadvantages, and have turned to the church, especially
those of the Pentecostal, Evangelical and Baptist branches for spiritual
and practical support in managing and overcoming substance dependence.
This practice is revealed in studies which indicate that African
Americans, African Canadians and British Africans often face barriers
related to healthcare access due to stigma surrounding mental health and
mistrust of institutional systems (Harding, S. 2017; Baker, J.; Bell, H.
2020).

Above all, the theological framework of sin, redemption and healing
resonate deeply with individuals who seek spiritual solutions to their
struggles. However, many Christians who say that prevention of the abuse
of drugs and alcohol is better than cure, point to various passages in
the Bible that offer guidance. *1 Peter 5:8* states, “Be sober, be
vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh
about, seeking whom he may devour.” Here, the call to be sober implies
both physical and spiritual sobriety. Substance abuse can cloud one’s
judgment, and make a person more vulnerable and susceptible to spiritual
attack. Sobriety is seen as a safeguard against harm – personal
integrity and spiritual health.

Similarly, *Ephesians 5:18* advises, “And be not drunk with wine,
wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit.” This verse provides a
clear admonition against drunkenness, highlighting the destructive
nature of the excessive use of alcohol which can lead to debauchery.
However, the emphasis on being “filled with the Spirit” offers a
spiritual alternative to the intoxication of wine, urging Christians to
seek a higher form of influence and control, the Holy Spirit rather than
substances.

In his letter to the *Romans*, Paul said, “I beseech you therefore,
brethren, by thy mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living
sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reason-able service.
And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the
renewing of the mind, that ye may prove what is good, and acceptable,
and perfect, will of God” (*Romans12:1-2).* This passage urges believers
to turn to a life of transformation, beginning with a renewal of the
mind. For those struggling with addiction, this transformation can
include changing one’s thought patterns, desires and general behaviour
to align with the will of God, thus seeking a spiritual renewal, knowing
that in Christ, profound personal and spiritual change is possible.

Furthermore, *Galatians 6:1-2* urges, “Brethren, if a man be overtaken
in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such one in the spirit of
meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted. Bear ye one
another’s burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ.” Here, the
community’s role in supporting those who are struggling with addiction,
is emphasised. Christians are called to be gentle in trying to restore
those who have fallen into sin, like abusing substances, because
everyone is vulnerable to temptation. Doubtless, the church is seen as a
place where people can find support, accountability, grace and a loving
environment for recovery.

In the USA, the Black Church has traditionally been a central
institution that provides spiritual care and social support, especially
with substance abuse cases. Baptist and Pentecostal churches empathise
direct personal encounters with God, often through prayer, fasting \[and
adult baptism\], as a way to overcome life-controlling behaviours
(Bryant & Peck, 2015). These churches offer a strong sense of community,
belonging and spiritual guidance, where believers find both emotional
and practical support in their battle against substance abuse.

Similarly, in the UK and Canada, churches that cater for people of
African and Caribbean diasporic communities have developed special
programmes that integrate Christian principles with evidence-based
therapeutic practices. These programmes emphasise spiritual renewal as a
form of emotional and psychological restoration. For example, some
Pentecostal churches in London and Toronto offer drug rehabilitation
programmes grounded in Christian teachings that are culturally
sensitive, addressing not only addiction, but also the unique racial and
identity struggles of people of African heritage (Norris & Walters,
2019).

These passages outlined above, show that The New Testament provides
ample guidance to people of African heritage on the dangers of substance
abuse, encouraging moderation, self-control while avoiding behaviours
like being under the influence of excessive alcohol or illegal or
prescription drugs that can lead to spiritual and moral decay.

**3.6.2.7 Schizophrenia**

Schizophrenia is a complex psychotic disorder characterised by emotional
blunting, intellectual deterioration, social isolation, disorganised
speech and behaviour, delusions and hallucinations. Since many of these
symptoms are like some religious experiences, schizophrenia has received
great interest from prominent psychiatrists, other professionals and
church leaders who are keen to clarify how this disorder is perceived,
so it can be managed and treated more appropriately.

This thesis explores the intersection of mental health, Christian
beliefs and psychiatric care of schizophrenics within the African
diasporic communities in the UK, USA and Canada, looking closely at how
Christian teachings, practices and church involvement interact with
professional mental health services to shape recovery and coping
strategies.

For people of African heritage in the UK, USA and Canada, the experience
of schizophrenia is viewed often as a complex relationship between
professional medical/psychiatric practices and traditional, culturally
specific healing approaches. These cultural responses are often rooted
in Christian teachings, particularly Pentecostalism, Evangelicalism and
other denominations that shape the understanding, treatment and
management of schizophrenia. In other words, many Africans view
schizophrenia through both medical and spiritual lens. For example, some
people believe that schizophrenia may result from demonic possessions,
ancestral spirits or punishment from God. Others accept the
medical/psychiatric model that defines schizophrenia as a
neurobiological disorder that is mainly influenced by genetic,
environmental and social factors.

Above all, historically, in several African cultures, mental illness
such as schizophrenia, was understood not as a psychiatric or medical
condition, but as one resulting from supernatural or spiritual
interactions. Now, the growing shift from traditional or cultural to
Western systems of care has often caused tension; some people in the
African heritage diaspora regard symptoms such as auditory
hallucinations as hearing voices that are messages from the divine (God)
or spirits, rather than symptoms of a psychiatric disorder (Madhav, V.
2015).

Moreover, adopting Christian practices, particularly in its Pentecostal,
Baptist and other Evangelical forms, is seen often as an effective way
to address the spiritual causes of mental illness. The belief in prayer,
fasting, exorcism, water baptism and the laying on of hands provide a
framework for understanding and dealing with schizophrenia that aligns
with spiritual healing practices. Many people of African heritage attend
church or seek prayers from church leaders while others undertake adult
baptism as an essential path of their recovery.

In the UK, churches with African congregations, such as the Nigerian-led
Redeemed Christian Church of God, have been forthright in providing
support for individuals suffering from schizophrenia. These
congregations often host prayer meetings and healing services where
participants seek divine interventions for a range of issues, including
mental health conditions (Williams, R. 2018). Similarly, in the USA,
African American churches have been involved in mental health advocacy,
for decades, with pastors being intermediaries between medical
professionals and churchgoers (Gibson, D. 2017).

Furthermore, an increasing number of mental health professionals in
Canada, are beginning to recognise the value of integrating spiritual
beliefs with psychiatric care, resulting in several clinics
collaborating with Christian organisations to offer holistic care where
patients are encouraged to continue their faith practices while also
receiving appropriate medical treatment (Johnson, M. 2020).

It is noted that schizophrenia presents unique challenges and
opportunities for integrating Christian teachings and practices with
professional mental health care in the African heritage communities in
the UK, USA and Canada. This is partly because Christianity remains a
central influence in shaping the attitudes and behaviour of individuals
with schizophrenia, but the research indicates that this influence
should align with evidence-based psychiatric and medical care.
Collaboration between medical and faith-based communities seems to offer
a more holistic and culturally sensitive approach to managing
schizophrenia in these diasporic populations.

**3.6.2.8 Suicide**

The intersection between New Testament-derived Christianity and the
issue of suicide presents a complex and contentious discourse. African
heritage Christians are taught to regard life as a sacred gift from God,
thus suicide would be viewed through a lens of moral judgment, and could
be regarded as a sin that would lead to spiritual consequences. However,
in contemporary Christian discussions, there is a shift towards greater
compassion, understanding and mental awareness. This section of research
will examine some traditional Christian views on suicide, contemporary
theological thinkings and the role of the Black Church in addressing the
mental health aspects while supporting believers who are struggling with
suicidal ideation. To have a meaningful exploration of this issue of
suicide, we must first agree on a definition.

Suicide is the act of deliberately killing oneself (WHO, 2024). In most
countries, particularly in the UK, USA and Canada, suicide is a crime,
but ironically, if successful, there is no one to punish. However,
attempted suicide can be a punishable crime that is seldom charged
against a person surviving the attempt. Rather, most survivors are often
sent for psychiatric investigation and support. On the other hand,
assisted suicide is usually treated as a crime, either specifically, as
in some US States, or as a form of homicide (second degree murder or
manslaughter), even when it is done as an act of kindness to a loved one
who in terminally ill, and in extreme pain. In Switzerland, assisted
suicide is legal under specific conditions, and the UK Parliament is now
debating a Bill that could soon legalise assisted dying.

Yet, in spite of one’s views on suicide, Christianity teaches that life
is a gift from God, and is central to the mission of Jesus who said,
“The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy: I
am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more
abundantly” *(John 10:10*). Historically, Christian doctrine regarded
suicide as a sin; this is deeply rooted in Biblical prohibitions against
murder that goes against the sanctity of life. Suicide was viewed in the
same way as murder. For example, the Sixth Commandment, “Thou shalt not
Kill” *(Exodus 20:13),* was interpreted by early Christian theologians
like Augustine and Aquinas to extend to self-inflicted death. Suicide
was regarded as a violation of God’s commandment and also as a rejection
of the divine gift of life. St. Augustine argued that suicide violated
God’s sovereignty over life and death (Augustine of Hippo. 2003).

This view was held by most Christians throughout the Middle Ages and
well into modern times, but the emergence and development of psychology
and psychiatry threw new light on suicide, bringing a shift in
understanding through scholarly debates that focused on possible
emotional distress and sufferings like depression and mental illness
that were viewed as causative factors.

Debates on the possible causes of suicide continued, as attitudes were
changing and thinking of prevention became uppermost in the minds of
some Christians. For example, the Roman Catholic Church changed its
views on suicide, emphasising that those who take their own lives may
not have acted responsibly nor rationally due to mental illness or
emotional distress. The Catechism of the Catholic Church states, “Grave
psychological disturbances, anguish, grave fear of hardship, suffering,
or torture can diminish the responsibility of the one committing
suicide” (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1997). That perspective
emphasised the need to view suicide or attempted suicide with compassion
while examining the emotional or psychological state of the individual
before he/she committed or attempted suicide.

Similarly, Protestant denominations that subscribed to a more
progressive theological approach began to adopt a more empathetic stance
on suicide. Several Protestant Church leaders then stressed that those
who die by suicide could still achieve God’s grace and mercy. In this
view, God’s love is not contingent upon one’s actions, and salvation is
not forfeited by the act of suicide itself (Pope, R. 2016). That new
shift in attitudes to suicide continued to evolve.

In the United Kingdom, suicide rates declined from the mid-1990s, with
Government interventions such as the National Suicide Prevention
Strategy for England (2002) playing a pivotal role (Foley, M. 2004).
However, today, suicide remains one of the most tragic and pervasive
mental health crises globally, and recent studies suggest that the
suicide rates within minority ethnic groups, including those of African
heritage, are rising. The African diaspora in the UK, USA and Canada
that consists of diverse communities from sub-Saharan Africa, the
Caribbean and other regions, presents a unique context in which to
understand suicide trends among Black people. Contemporary statistics
reveal important age and sex-based differences in suicide rates,
underscoring the complex intersections of gender, race and mental
health.

Currently, in the UK, suicide among people of African heritage,
including those of African and Caribbean descent, is generally lower
compared to the White British population. Yet, disparities exist when
examining suicide rates across different genders and age groups. The
data from the Office for National Statistics (ONS, 2020) shows that
suicide rates among Black men are notably higher than those among Black
women, particularly in the younger age brackets.

In 2020, the UK’s suicide rate for Black men aged 20-34 was
approximately 10.7 per 100,000, which was higher than the general male
population rate of 9.2 per 100,000. In contrast, suicide rates for Black
women, especially those aged 35 and older, are significantly lower, with
rates of about 3.6 per 100,000. Understandably, suicide among Black men
is receiving greater attention, now.

Similarly, while suicide rates in the USA have been rising steadily
across all racial and ethnic groups, the African American population has
shown unique patterns in these trends. According to the Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention (CDC, 2021), Black Americans,
particularly men, face disproportionately high suicide rates in
proportion to other ethnic groups. In 2021, the overall suicide rate for
Black Americans was 9.9 per 100,000 compared to the national average of
14.3 per 100,000.

Suicide was the third leading cause of death for African American youth
aged 15 to 24 (CDC, 2006), after the rate peeked at 12.5 per 100,000 in
2021. While completed youth suicides represented a major public health
problem in the USA, they represented a small fraction of suicidal
behaviours. The Youth Risk Behaviour Surveillance (YRBS) survey found
that in the 12 months preceding the survey, 16.9 % of high school
students seriously considered a suicide attempt, 13% made a suicide
plan, and 8.4% made a suicide attempt in 2005. Among African American
youth, 12.2% reported considering a suicide attempt, seriously, 9.6 %
made a suicide plan, and 7.6% made a suicide attempt. In 2003, African
American (and Hispanic) male High school students surpassed White
American males for the first time in suicide attempts that required
medical attention (CDC, 2004). The common methods of suicide remained
the use of firearms, suffocating by hanging and poisoning (Joe, S. and
Marcus, S. 2003).

In the 2000s, studies suggested that risk factors for suicide ideation,
attempts and/or completed suicides among African American adolescents
included depression, hopelessness, delinquent behaviour and substance
abuse (Ialongo et al, 2004; O’Donnell, O’Donnell, Wardlaw, and Stueve,
2004). More Christians who regard life as a precious gift from God,
began to focus on the prevention of suicide, leading to a new awakening
in Black churches. In the UK, USA and Canada where Christian populations
formed a significant portion of the society, churches had increasingly
become institutions where mental health issues could be addressed. Many
church leaders advocated for early intervention and treatment,
encouraging individuals to seek medical help for mental health concerns
while also providing spiritual support (Sweeney, J. 2011). The Mental
Health and Church initiative which began in the 1980s, and had grown
across denominations, aimed to equip pastors and church leaders with the
necessary tools to address mental health issues, including suicidal
thoughts, within their congregations.

In the absence of suicide prevention programmes aimed specifically at
African American youth, the CDC (2004) suggested developing easily
accessible ones that will address multiple risk factors while being
attractive to youth and readily endorsed by peers, family members and
the broader community. Such a proposal resonated with the African
American churches that were already providing several social services to
the African American community. Research suggested that two-thirds of
the social service programmes provided by churches pertain to families
and youth (Rubin, Billingsley & Caldwell, 1994). Qualitative studies had
found that African American pastors and churches were willing to adopt
mental health interventions in church contexts, particularly when the
programmes were aimed at strengthening families and youth (Molock, S.D.
2005). Furthermore, despite the potential barriers to implementing
suicide prevention programmes in Black churches, the American
Association of Suicidology believed that collaborating with the Black
Church to develop suicide prevention programmes did provide a promising
approach to better address the unmet needs of many African American
individuals and communities.

Notably, the Christian teaching on mental illness and suicide continues
to evolve significantly, with increasing recognition of the profound
effects that mental health conditions could have on individuals.
Depression, anxiety and other mental health conditions are viewed today
by many in the Black Church, not merely as spiritual failings, but as
complex biological, psychological and social issues. This new shift is
part of a bigger trend within Christian communities to incorporate more
holistic approaches to health, particularly mental health, acknowledging
the interconnection among faith, body and mind.

This new approach is being welcomed in the three diasporic communities,
especially since the suicide rates show the continuing need for
prevention. Currently, Canada’s suicide rates sit between those of the
UK and the USA, with significant variation across provinces. For
example, Canada’s national suicide statistics also reflect a concerning
pattern for African Canadians. Statistics Canada shows that the overall
suicide rate in Canada is about 11.1 per 100,000, but this varies
significantly by race and ethnicity. However, the suicide rate among
African Canadians tends to be lower than that of the wider population,
but significant gender and age differences are noted. For example,
younger Black men, aged 15-34, show the highest suicide rates,
estimating 10.8 to 12.0 per 100,000, compared to their White
counterparts in the same age group where the rate is approximately 8.4
per 100,000. Yet, Black women in Canada, like those in the UK and USA,
have much lower suicide rates, often averaging about 3.5 per 100.000 for
those in their reproductive years (ages 25-44). These rates demonstrate
the continued need for a multi-agency approach to reduce suicide
completion through prevention education and counselling that involves
the Church and the State/professional bodies.

A Canadian study of 2019 found that churches were among the most trusted
institutions to provide support for mental crises in smaller and more
rural communities where mental health services are often scarce (Lund,
T., & Ross, M. 2019). This trust, however, places a great responsibility
on the Black Church leaders to ensure that their responses to suicide
prevention are both compassionate and informed by contemporary
psychological understanding.

Now, it is becoming more evident, that the Christian communities across
the African diaspora in the UK, USA and Canada have embraced the
importance of mental health, and have developed specific ministries
aimed at providing support to those at risk of suicide. These ministries
often focus on prayer, pastoral care and community support besides
integrating professional counselling, besides therapeutic services. For
example, the Christian Suicide Prevention Network (CSPN), a
non-denominational organisation in the USA, works closely with mental
health professionals to offer resources, including crisis hotlines and
suicide prevention training for church leaders (CSPN, 2020). Similarly,
the Suicide Prevention and Bereavement Ministry within the Anglican
Church in the UK provides both spiritual and psychological support for
individuals who have lost loved ones to suicide. Individuals and
families of African heritage benefit from these provisions.

Today, the church has a unique role in suicide prevention due to its
widespread influence in communities and its potential to reach out to
people at risk before a crisis develops. Believers can be proactive in
recognising signs of distress in themselves and others, although this
will require a change in attitudes to stigma towards mental health.
Christians are encouraged to maintain an awareness of the signs of
suicidal thoughts which include withdrawal from community, feelings of
hopelessness, sudden mood changes or speaking about death (Van der Meer,
A., 2012).

More congregations are adopting a dual approach to suicide prevention:
spiritual counselling is combined with a referral to medical or
psychological services. This allows the church to act as a bridge for
those who might be reluctant to seek mental health care due to stigma or
lack of information. Programmes like Hope for the Hurting (USA) and The
Pastoral Care Project (UK) have been very effective in providing both
faith-based and evidence-based interventions to prevent suicide
(Sweeney, J., 2011).

Furthermore, the Christian African heritage communities are being
encouraged to continue building strong supportive networks, especially
since in Matthew 18:20, Jesus promises, “For where two or three are
gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them.” The
church is a unique institution or community that can continue to provide
care in the form of prayer, solidarity and fellowship.

Consequently, the New Testament-derived Christianity plays a dual role
in shaping attitudes towards suicide in the African heritage communities
in the UK, USA and Canada. However, the message of the New Testament
itself is life, resurrection and redemption while offering a framework
for hope and recovery. Yet, the stigma in the communities surrounding
suicide and mental health often prevents free discussion that affect
would-be suicide prevention strategies. Fortunately, churches, as
primary spiritual/healing communities are becoming more competent in
addressing the complexities of mental health and trauma, using both
theological insights and advances in the understanding of psychology and
psychiatry, especially in dealing with suicide.

**3.6.2.9 Domestic Abuse**

This research has noted that many Christians turn to the Church for
support in times of suffering, especially in cases of extreme anxiety,
depression, substance abuse and suicide, but one problem that is
currently receiving a wave of attention is domestic abuse. Experts in
the fields of psychology, theology, social work, education, law,
psychiatry and psychotherapy have called for a new approach to the
incidence of domestic abuse that has be misunderstood and tolerated for
too long.

A new approach seemed necessary especially since some interpretations of
Christianity within the African diaspora, have established some
justification for and tolerance of domestic abuse, resulting in an
unhealthy perpetuation of the practice for a long time. Scripture such
as *Ephesians 5:22*, “Wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands,
as unto the Lord,” and *Colossians 3:18*, “Wives, submit yourselves unto
your own husbands, as is fit in the Lord,” are often used as
justification in support of traditional gender roles in marriage where
the male’s general behaviour is tolerated. This view has become
problematic, especially when husbands use these readings to justify
controlling or violent behaviour. Additionally, the New Testament’s
emphasis on forgiveness and turning the other cheek *(Matthew 5:39)*
could encourage sufferers/victims to endure abuse as part of their
Christian duty or expectation, leading to a high degree of
victim-blaming in some communities (Frazier, M., 2014). Furthermore, one
questions the role of Christian religious leaders who may inadvertently
perpetuate abuse by reinforcing the teachings of forgiveness, especially
by ‘turning the other cheek,’ to discourage divorce or separation to
preserve the sanctity of marriage. However, a scholarly discussion of
the Christian attitude to domestic abuse warrants an agreed definition
or use of the term.

Domestic abuse or domestic violence comprises of broad categories of
behaviour including physical or sexual abuse, violent or threatening
behaviour, controlling or coercive behaviour resulting from economic,
psychological or emotional factors. The UK’s government definition of
domestic violence as outlined by the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS,
2024) is: “any incident of threatening behaviour, violence or abuse
\[psychological, physical, sexual, financial or emotional\] between
adults who are or have been intimate partners or family members
regardless of gender or sexuality.” The CPS is concerned with criminal
offences that occur in a domestic context whatever the age of the
victims and abuser.

This research will use the CPS’s definition of the term, ‘Domestic Abuse
/ Domestic violence’ to mean any incident of threatening behaviour …
displayed by intimate partners or family members irrespective of gender
or sexuality. Men, women and children can all be victims of domestic
abuse that occurs amongst people of all gender identities, ethnicities,
sexualities, ages, disabilities, immigration status, religious beliefs
and socio-economic backgrounds.

The issue of domestic abuse or domestic violence warrants a national
exploration to present appropriate data to understand the problem fully,
but the scope of this thesis, enables only a closer look at this
multifaceted problem in the African diaspora communities in the UK, USA
and Canada.

In the UK, 1 in 5 teenage girls have experienced domestic abuse in their
dating relationship, as reported by the charity, SafeLives, in 2017. In
England and Wales, police recorded 1.3 million domestic abuse related
crimes in the year ending 2020, an increase of 4% on the previous year.
Moreover, in England and Wales, approximately, 2.1 million people aged
16 and over, will experience some form of domestic abuse, annually, so
in 2021, 2.4 million adults were victims, including 1.7 million women
and 699,000 men (National Centre for Domestic Violence, 2021).
Similarly, the police recorded 1,500.369 domestic abuse-related
incidents and crimes in England and Wales in the year ending March 2022.
This shows that the number of domestic abuse-related crimes increased by
7.7% compared to 2021, thus requiring a significant increase in the cost
of supporting victims. For example, the social and economic cost of
domestic abuse in England and Wales between 2016-2017, was estimated to
be 66 billion pounds sterling, including 14 billion pounds being the
cost to the country’s economy arising from lost output due to time off
work (Home Office, 2017).

Additionally, the research shows that 37% of incidents occurred within
the victim’s home, with 5% occurring in the suspected perpetrator’s home
while 36% of incidents occurred in other dwellings. Furthermore, the
National Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children (NSPCC,
2019), reported that child abuse and neglect was also on the increase.
Additionally, the latest Office for National Statistics figures
(2022/23) show that one in three victims of domestic abuse are male
equating to 751,000 men (3.2%) and 1.38 million women (5.7%). From this,
483,000 men and 964,000 women are victims of partner abuse (ONS
2022/23). Mankind Initiative, a charity that conducts research and
provides statistics on male victims of domestic abuse, goes on to state
that one in seven men (13.9%) and one in four women (27%) will be a
victim of domestic abuse in their lifetime (ONS figures 2022/23).
However, unlike women, many male victims neither report their abuse nor
seek help until situations become dangerously uncontrollable. Some
victims report that they suffer in silence because they believed that
domestic violence was taken seriously only if the perpetrator was a man.
Similarly, while domestic abuse charities, such as Women’s Aid treat
this as a gendered crime – because females are far more likely to be
sexually abused over a longer period, and murdered by a partner or
former partner, the cases of female abusers will remain stifled and
hidden.

In 2020, the Daily Mail published an article entitled, ‘Hidden Shame of
Male Victims’ abused by women they love. In the same year, Data obtained
under the UK’s Freedom of Information Act revealed that the number of
domestic attacks carried out by women, more than tripled in a decade.
Between 2009 and 2018, the number of cases reported to the police grew
from 27,762 to 92,408, according to the Sunday Telegraph. There is also
the incidence of male perpetrators, in same sex relationships. The
helpline of the UK charity, Mankind, receives calls from all men- from
dustmen to doctors, from bakers to bankers, from teachers to tradesmen-
right across the UK and across the full range of backgrounds,
ethnicities and sexualities.

In some cases, reports are made to the police or religious leaders by
the mothers or by close friends. While it remains difficult to determine
a precise number of male victims of domestic abuse or violence within
the African diasporic communities in the USA, UK and Canada, the safety
and treatment of victims across the diaspora cannot be ignored.

The Domestic Abuse Act (UK,2021) offers more Statutory protection to
men, women and children, but notwithstanding Government’s intervention
and provisions, the Church and Charities saw the urgent need to increase
their support to cope with the growing needs of victims of domestic
violence and abuse.

Britain’s African and Caribbean Pentecostal Church created the ‘Black
Church Domestic Abuse Forum (BCDAF) that was officially launched online
in 2020. The Primary aim of the forum is to train church leaders to make
the most appropriate responses to domestic violence and abuse, therefore
a toolkit was developed to provide information on how churches can
improve their responses to domestic violence and abuse. The BCDAF was
found to be very useful during the Covid-19 lockdowns and restrictions
in the UK, between 2020-2021, when domestic violence increased.

Christian Church Leaders have been very open to receiving training, and
it is apparent that many are keen to be appropriately trained to respond
compassionately and practically to church members, together with others
within the wider community who have experienced domestic abuse and
violence.

Today, domestic abuse services provide a wide range of information and
support including refuge accommodation, helplines, outreach support,
floating support, resettlement support, specialist children and young
people services, Domestic Abuse Prevention Advocates and
drop-in-support. There were 236 domestic abuse service providers running
401 local services listed on ‘Routes to Support’ (An online Database on
local domestic abuse services owned and managed by ‘Women’s Aid) in
England in May 2023. 271 of these services include refuge and 238
include dedicated services for children/young people (Women’s Aid, 2024)

Domestic violence/abuse is influenced by socio-economic stressors,
patriarchal cultural norms, immigration challenges, besides the accepted
interpretation and application of religious teachings. Furthermore, as
outlined above, New Testament-derived Christianity plays a multifaceted
role in both perpetuating and potentially addressing the problem of
domestic violence within the African diaspora. For example, one African
American domestic abuse survivor, Brittney Guary, reported that “They
told me God hates divorce, and I’d go to hell if I broke up my home.” In
fact, when she disclosed signs of abusive behaviour during her
premarital counselling, Brittney was encouraged to get married, and
ignore the signs that should have dissuaded her. Furthermore, like many
other Christian women, she stayed in an abusive relationship because she
felt it would be unchristian to leave, and because she did not find the
kind of support that she needed in her church.

Finally, as Brittney (2023) wrote in her book, ‘Breaking free: healing
from Abuse and Trauma’, she knew that she had to leave her abusive
relationship when her husband picked up her son by the throat and threw
him, causing head injury. She vowed not to allow him to abuse their
children. “It was bad enough what he had done to me,” Brittney said.
Subsequently, She found a new church that helped her do all she needed
to get away. The Church found counselling for her; gave the assurance
that while God hates divorce, he certainly does not hate the people
involved, and definitely did not want her to stay in that relationship.
Certainly, this book is an asset to many victims of domestic
violence/abuse.

The Black Women’s Health Project in the USA (2023), states that 40% of
black women will experience domestic abuse across their lifetime,
compared to 30.2 % of white women. This increased level of domestic
violence in the African diaspora communities in the UK, USA and Canada,
is linked to unemployment, poverty, adverse racism, inadequate housing,
substance abuse, together with inadequate access to proper health and
education.

Economic hardship creates an environment in which tension and
frustration prevail, often triggering violent behaviours in the home or
within families. Similarly, the stress and anxiety created by poverty,
especially in male-dominated households, can lead to feelings of
inadequacy that are sometimes vocalised through aggression or arguments.
Unfortunately, unemployment and the underemployment of Black women
creates more financial dependencies that could trap victims in abusive
relationships (Carter, E., 2016). However, some women of African
heritage are more likely to disclose experience of domestic abuse in
prayer group meetings, or to a close friend at church, especially when
seeking urgent financial help or after a sudden incident of physical
abuse that involves children. In such cases, Church leaders are often
called upon for urgent support.

Recent government studies from Statistics Canada shows that the rates of
abuse of women remained high between 1999 and 2009. They report that 98%
of intimate partner violence is committed by men, but because many women
do not report domestic violence, the statistics is not conclusive. Many
women fear retaliation, or they minimise the nature or severity of the
abuse to preserve the relationship or the family setting for their
children. For example, South Asian immigrants have cultural standards
around what it means to be a ‘good wife’. In some cases this can lead to
them being submissive and self-sacrificing to the point of not reporting
even extreme cases of domestic violence and abuse.

Nason-Clark, a sociologist at the university of New Brunswick, suggests
that pastors are not listening when women come forward with reports of
spousal violence. She suggests that church leaders need more training.
Moreover, the Women’s Inter-Church Council of Canada (2024), reported
that many women of African heritage are immigrants or newcomers, or have
refugee status, and fear losing their sponsorship. They face many
burdens as they try to leave abusive relationships; some are often
dependent on their partners. Some of these women are involved in joint
sponsorship of their own and their spouse’s extended family members, so
threats of deportation can hang over their heads, as can the fear that
if they report cases of abuse, they will suffer social disgrace, and the
breakdown of a sponsorship.

Above all, the Canadian Constitution enables Provincial and Territorial
governments to make specific laws relating to family violence,
including: the ‘Prince Edwards Island: Victims of Family Violence Act’,
‘Alberta: Protection Against Family Violence Act’, ‘New Brunswick:
Intimate Partner Violence Intervention Act’, ‘British Columbia: Child,
Family and Community Service Act’ and the ‘Saskatchewan: Victims of
Domestic Violence Act,’ to name a few. These civil statutes are designed
to complement protections in the Criminal Code of Canada. They offer
further protection to victims of domestic violence, including children
who are abandoned or sexually abused. Similarly, the Government passed
Bill 173 – The Intimate Partner Violence Epidemic Act of 2024 that seeks
to:

- recognise intimate partner violence (IPV) as an epidemic in the
  Province of Ontario,

- increase public awareness of IPV,

- boost prevention efforts, and provide support for survivors.

Yet, in spite of Statutory provisions, the work of charities and
churches to protect and safeguard victims of domestic violence and abuse
cannot be underestimated. Above all, the current literature shows that
faith-based counselling services have become an increasingly important
part of dealing with domestic violence in African heritage communities.
For example, in the

UK, many Christians of African heritage seek out counselling that
integrates their religious beliefs with psychological and emotional
support. These programmes comply with worldwide views of the victims,
and offer solutions that are framed within a religious context.
Furthermore, programmes that teach both men and women about mutual
respect, non-violence and forgiveness, while emphasising the equality of
spouses in Christ, have shown promise in changing attitudes about gender
and abuse (Baldwin, C., 2018).

Moreover, more Black churches and faith-based organisations are now
collaborating with statutory agencies, social services, specific
charities and shelters to provide holistic support for victims of
domestic violence/abuse. In the United Kingdom, the USA and Canada,
where domestic violence is a recognised issue among African heritage
communities, churches have become key institutions that offer culturally
sensitive services that deal with the emotional and legal needs of
victims. This collaboration is essential in bridging the gap between
faith-based approaches and secular legal frameworks. Christian leaders
who understand the cultural nuances of their congregations do play an
important role in guiding individuals towards statutory support services
without the fear of cultural or religious alienation (Carter, E. 2016).

As shown above, domestic violence or domestic abuse within the African
diasporic communities is multifaceted, and is influenced by
socio-economic challenges, cultural norms and religious beliefs. While
New Testament-derived Christianity has been used sometimes to justify
the subjugation of women and men within marriage and similar
relationships, it also offers appropriate measures for intervention and
healing. However, when traditional interpretations of scripture are used
to perpetuate abusive behaviours, challenges occur, but they are reduced
greatly by the way religious teachings are applied within African
heritage communities, and by the willingness of faith leaders to engage
with statutory frameworks while advocating for the dignity and safety of
all members of their congregations.

**3.7 Socioeconomic, Cultural and Systematic Factors**

Now, this thesis has shown that Mental health challenges within African
heritage communities in the UK, USA and Canada are inextricably linked
to systematic issues such as socioeconomic disparities, cultural stigma,
historical trauma and pervasive racism. For many people in these
communities, Christianity serves as an enduring source of resilience and
hope, providing not only spiritual comfort, but also psychological and
social mechanisms to face these challenges.

This study continues to examine the way Christianity – especially
practices and doctrines derived from the New Testament – has been
employed by African congregants and faith leaders to address mental
health difficulties. Through spiritual teachings, worship and communal
support, the Christian faith offers a pathway for healing and resistance
against the composite adversities that these communities encounter.

**3.7.1. Socioeconomic Disparities and Faith-Based Coping Mechanisms**

Economic hardship has long been a defining reality for African heritage
communities across the UK, USA and Canada, thus the enduring impact of
poverty often results in heightened levels of stress, anxiety and
depression due to financial instability and restricted access to
resources. Within these situations, Christianity provides a lifeline
through its teachings of hope, resilience and divine provision. For
instance, *Matthew 6:25-34* advises Christians to “Take no thought for
your life, what you shall eat, or what you shall drink, …but seek ye
first the Kingdom of God…”. Here, Christians are given psychological
relief and reassured of God’s care with the encouragement to shift their
focus from worldly concerns to their faith in divine providence.

In congregational settings, ministers and church leaders frequently
emphasise the importance of maintaining hope during financial struggles.
The “prosperity Gospel,” though controversial, has gained traction among
segments of the African diaspora, instilling a sense of empowerment
through the belief in divine intervention for material success (Mbiti,
J. 1991). This view is echoed by Meyer (2004) who states that for many
African Christians, economic struggles are framed as spiritual tests,
encouraging individuals to see hardship not as an insurmountable
obstacle, but as an opportunity for personal and spiritual growth. This
theological perspective acts as a buffer against despair, offering a
sense of divine solidarity and purpose amidst financial adversity.
However, in addition to spiritual encouragement, churches often provide
practical support, such as job training schemes, financial assistance
and community outreach which reinforce both resilience and communal
solidarity (Gifford, P. 2004).

**3.7.2 Cultural Stigma and the Christian Faith’s Approach to Mental
Health**

As shown earlier, mental health issues are often shrouded in cultural
stigma, within African heritage communities. They may be perceived as
personal failings, or even attributed to spiritual forces, such as
demonic possession. This entrenched stigma often deters individuals from
seeking professional mental health care, thus exacerbating their
suffering. However, Christianity provides an alternative narrative that
blends spiritual healing with psychological support: there is greater
collaboration between church and statutory bodies, today.

Customarily, faith leaders often address mental distress through prayer
and deliverance, drawing upon New Testament accounts of Jesus’ healing
miracles like that of the woman with the issue of blood to whom Jesus
said, “Daughter, thy faith hath made the whole; go in peace, and be
whole of thy plague” *(Mark 5:34).* Practices like laying hands on the
sick, communal prayer meetings, and prophetic declarations serve to
bridge traditional cultural views of illness with contemporary mental
health paradigms, fostering holistic well-being that addresses body,
mind and spirit (Tanyi, P. 2015).

Increasingly, African Christian leaders have integrated contemporary
mental health practices into their pastoral work, because they recognise
the compatibility of spiritual care and psychological support. By
promoting both deliverance and professional mental health care, they
challenge stigma and encouraged nuanced understanding of mental illness
(Kanu, I. 2016). This approach helps to create a more inclusive
framework for addressing mental health within these African diasporic
communities.

**3.7.3 Systematic Racism, Historical Trauma and Christian Resistance**

The collective psyche of African heritage communities in the UK, USA,
Canada \[and the Caribbean\] has been shaped profoundly by the legacy of
slavery, colonisation and institutional racism. These historical and
ongoing injustices have left deep scars, manifesting as chronic stress,
and depression that have led to post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD),
among some individuals. In this context, Christianity, particularly
liberation theology, offers a framework for addressing these traumas.

Emerging in Latin America during the late 20<sup>th</sup> century,
liberation theology, a religious movement that arose in Roman
Catholicism, seeks to apply religious faith by helping the poor and
oppressed through involvement in political and civic affairs. This
movement has resonated powerfully with African diasporic Christianity,
emphasising Christ’s message as one of liberation from oppression and
injustice. Teachings / Passages like St. Paul’s letter to the Galatians,
“There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there
is neither male nor female; for ye are all one in Christ Jesus”
(*Galatians 3:28)* are invoked frequently to challenge racial
inequality, and affirm the inherent dignity of all people (Cone, J. H.
1997).

In Black churches in the UK, like African American and African Canadian
churches, biblical themes of exile, suffering and divine justice
resonate deeply. Practices like communal lamentation – drawing on Old
and New Testaments – provide a means for congregants to express
collective grief over racial injustice, offering both catharsis and
spiritual solace. Combined with the biblical promise of liberation,
“…The lord God … hath sent me …to proclaim liberty to the captives…”
(*Isaiah 61: 1-3),* these practices cultivate resilience and hope,
reinforcing faith as a tool for both healing and activism (West, C.
2004).

Churches serve often as dual spaces for spiritual refuge and activism,
as shown in the fight for civil rights. Here, collective prayer merges
with social justice initiatives, empowering congregants to resist racial
injustice while finding personal healing. By affirming the image of God
in every individual, Christianity nurtures a sense of worth and purpose,
inspiring African communities to seek both personal transformation and
societal change (Graham, D. 2010).

**3.7.4 Conclusion**

New Testament-derived Christianity had been a vital resource for African
heritage communities in the UK, USA and Canada, addressing their mental
health challenges through spiritual practices and teaching of hope,
healing and collective strength. By drawing on New Testament principles,
African congregants and their faith leaders have cultivated resilience
against socioeconomic hardship, cultural stigma and racial trauma.
Whether through prayer, communal support or activism, the Christian
faith provides a multidimensional framework that integrates spiritual
and psychological well-being. Consequently, it fosters both individual
and collective empowerment, enabling these African diasporic communities
to navigate systemic inequalities more appropriately, with faith, hope
and determination.

**Chapter 4: Theological Discussion on New Testament Christianity During
the Colonial**

**and Missionary Eras and Its Impact on People of African Heritage**

**4.1 Introduction**

The spread of New Testament-derived Christianity left an indelible mark
during the colonial and missionary periods from the early
16<sup>th</sup> century to the late 18<sup>th</sup> century, especially
on communities of African heritage within the British Empire and its
colonies, from the Caribbean to North America, including the United
States of America and Canada. It was a complex, often contradictory
phenomenon, because the teachings and practices of Christianity served
not only as a tool of the Empire, colonial governance, but also as
transformative force that led to a theological framework for liberation
by Black churches. However, the historical and contemporary impacts of
these periods are crucial for understanding the role of Christianity in
shaping the mental health of people of African heritage in those
regions. This study integrates theological analysis with historical
context to provide a comprehensive view of Christianity’s influence on
Africans’ well-being and mental health during the colonial and
missionary eras.

**4.2 Christianity’s Role in Colonisation and Missionary Work**

During the colonial era, Christianity was often presented as an
essential companion to European imperialism. From the 17<sup>th</sup>
century, plantations were established and missionaries established
schools, hospitals and churches, contributing to social infrastructure
(Hastings, A.,1994). However, most missionaries, inspired by New
Testament passages such as the Great Commission, “Go ye therefore, and
teach all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father, and the
Son, and the Holy Ghost: …I am with you always…” (*Matthew 28:19-20),*
believed it was their divine duty to convert and civilise or save people
of African heritage who were perceived as a heathen and primitive
people. So, Christianity was used to justify the enslaving of Africans,
for *Ephesians 6:5* states, “Servants, be obedient to them that are your
masters according to the flesh…”

In other words, slaves were urged to obey their earthly masters, as part
of God’s ordained order. Above all, Christianity served as a tool for
imperial powers to justify their actions, yet it offered a message of
hope and liberation to the oppressed (Fanon, F., 1961). The imposition
of Western cultural norms through missionary work often led to the
erosion of indigenous cultures and belief systems (Bediako, K., 1995).

On the contrary, African slaves and their descendants were not passive
recipients of this imposed theology. Rather, they used the same
scriptures that were meant to subjugate them to develop a framework of
cultural resistance and liberation. The story of the *Exodus*, though
rooted in the Old Testament, resonated deeply as a narrative of divine
deliverance from enslavement or bondage. Above all, in the Caribbean,
USA and Canada, enslaved Africans infused Christian worship with
elements of their African spiritual heritage, forming unique expressions
of faith that preserved their cultural identity, and resisted colonial
domination. For example, one could recall how enslaved Africans used
some of the Christian hymns that were sung in small churches by their
White slave masters on plantations in the southern states of America,
and blended them with African songs to create work-songs that became
known as Negro spirituals, then Gospel songs that were used for
spiritual and mental upliftment, resilience and protest.

**4.3 Christianity, Race, Theology and Resistance in the African
Diaspora**

**4.3.1 Missionary Activities in the Colonies of the British Empire**

Again, Christian missionary activity was central to the work of European
colonialism, providing British missionaries and their supporters with a
sense of justice and moral authority. Missionary activities that began
earnestly in the 17<sup>th</sup> century, sought to transform indigenous
communities into imperial archetypes of civility. Across its
territories, from the Caribbean to North America, the British Empire’s
mission was not only to establish itself as a global economic and
military force, but also to spread Christianity to transform the
indigenous peoples. Unfortunately, many indigenous populations were
decimated by disease and violence, thus the Empire turned to Africa for
slaves to work on the plantations. European Missionaries followed
closely behind, tasked with civilising and “saving” the souls of both
enslaved Africans and the remnants of indigenous communities like the
Arawaks and Caribs in the Caribbean, or the Cree, Iroquois and Ojibwa of
Canada.

**4.3.2 The Caribbean Context**

As a result, Christianity continued to evolve in the Caribbean, both as
a tool of colonial control and a source of resistance. Enslaved Africans
embraced Christianity, and reinterpreted Christian doctrines in ways
that aligned with their struggle for social justice and political
freedom. Consequently, the struggle for change gained momentum with the
Liberation theology movement which developed in Latin America in the
mid-20<sup>th</sup> century, resonating strongly in the Caribbean
Christian thought.

As discussed earlier, by framing Christ’s message as a call to overturn
oppressive structures, Caribbean theologians, like Percy Hintzen (2002),
highlighted the Gospel’s radical potential to address and abolish racial
and social injustices. Increasingly, Christianity was infused with
African spiritual traditions, creating syncretic practices that offered
a sense of identity and community (Murrell, N.S., 2010). The New
Testament’s emphasis on justice and redemption provided a theological
basis for resisting oppression and envisioning a liberated future.

Furthermore, missionary groups such as Anglicans, Catholics and
Baptists, though often self-serving, provided education and rudimentary
health care, although enslaved Africans and indigenous peoples had their
own cultural methods of fighting diseases and ailments, particularly
with herbs. However, the work of those missionaries was deeply interwind
with colonial interests, as religion was used to reinforce hierarchical
social structures and justification for slavery. British missionary
efforts in the Caribbean thus served both as spiritual and colonial
instruments, aligning with the Empire’s economic priorities.

On the other hand, Moravian missionaries became the first large-scale
Protestant missionary movement to send unordain or ‘lay’ people to reach
out to enslaved communities in the Caribbean. Two of the first notable
young Moravian brethrens, Johann Leonhard Dober and David Nitschmann
from Herrnhut, Germany, undertook slave labour in St. Thomas and St.
Croix to be allowed to work with enslaved Africans in 1772. Historical
records show that Moravian missionaries baptised around 13,000 converts
in the islands of St. Croix, St. Thomas, St. Johns, Jamaica, Antigua,
Tobago, St. Kitts and Barbados, before any other missionaries arrived on
the Caribbean scene.

The Moravians believed in the spiritual equality of all people,
regardless of their race or social status. That belief led them to
engage actively with enslaved Africans in the southern colonies of the
USA. Unlike most of the other missionaries, they provided religious
education for those who were baptised.

**4.3.3 Christianity in the USA**

On the other hand, the Spanish Empire sponsored the earliest missionary
activity in the West of America when the Franciscans established
missions in California in 1541 and New Mexico in 1581. Friar Junipero
Serra founded nine California missions between 1769 and 1782, beginning
with Mission San Diego. Evidence points to the convert of indigenous
Indians, sometimes by force, while teaching them farming, ranching,
agricultural, handicraft and manufacturing skills.

The Franciscans were sent as spiritual shepherds, but also as tools for
the Spanish colonial expansion. Newsom (2005) states that their main
mission was to convert and civilise. Enslaved Africans brought to the
Americas by the Spanish, became part of the intricate web of
colonisation in missions across Florida, Texas and California. They were
not merely subjects of conversion, but labourers who worked the land
with indigenous peoples to maintain the missions.

The British Empire, however, regarded slavery as a way of supplying
labour for their plantation economy, and their missionaries were an
integral part of that enterprise. Like the Spanish Franciscans, the
British missionaries worked to save the souls of the enslaved people who
were being integrated into an institution of slavery where they were
mere chattels. The British colonies grew rich off tobacco, rice and
cotton produced on plantations that demanded an ever-growing labour
force that comprised mainly of enslaved Africans and the remaining
indigenous peoples who had survived the violent decimation of their
communities.

Another group of British missionaries were the Puritans, a
16<sup>th</sup> century Protestant group who wanted to reform the Church
of England, and make it more Protestant, by purifying it of Roman
Catholic practices. They first arrived in New England in the early
1600s, driven by a desire to create a “city on a hill,” a model
Christian society. Although their mission was one of religious purity
according to Calvinist doctrine, they soon engaged with African slavery
as part of their colonial enterprise.

Puritan missionaries who established colonies in the Massachusetts Bay,
worked to convert Native Americans, and established “praying towns”
while participating in slavery. Although they were not initially
involved in the transatlantic slave trade to the same degree as the
British Caribbean or Southern colonies, they did participate in slavery,
particularly in the latter half of the 17<sup>th</sup> century. In the
North of the United States, Puritans used enslaved Africans in homes, on
farms and in some of their missionary-run institutions. It was evident,
therefore, that while the Puritans’ theological emphasis on salvation
was evident in their missionary work, their views on enslaved Africans
were coloured by racial hierarchies, seeing them not as souls to be
saved, but as labourers in an economy that survived on slavery.

On the other hand, in the 1700s, Moravians missionaries were the only
ones who reached out to the Indigenous peoples and enslaved Africans in
Pennsylvania, the Carolinas and Georgia with the primary intention of
converting and baptising while emphasising spiritual equality. However,
Moravian missionaries did not challenge the institution of slavery
itself. Instead, they worked within the system, seeking to save souls,
rather than to liberate bodies. Their evangelical work was seen by some
as a method of pacifying enslaved populations, offering them a form of
Christianity that promoted obedience and submission. Yet, it is said
that David Nitschmann assisted the British missionary, John Wesley, to
found the Christian mission at Bethlehem, Pennsylvania in the USA.

Other Protestants who established missions in the Trans-Mississippi
West, were the Methodists who built their first mission in Oregon’s
Willamette Valley in 1834. Presbyterians arrived in Oregon Country in
1836.

Above all, the Franciscans and British missionaries were complicit in
the mechanisms of colonialism and slavery. Conversion to Christianity
was not just about salvation, it was about conformity- shaping those
populations into tools that would benefit colonial power.

**4.3.4 The Canadian Experience**

Moreover, the 17<sup>th</sup> and 18<sup>th</sup> centuries saw the
British Empire’s colonisation of Canada as a crucial stage of its
expansion of European influence in the Americas. Catholicism and
Anglicanism were the dominant branches of New Testament-derived
Christianity that were been propagated by the British and French
colonial powers to reinforce their control over land and people. That
religious mission was deeply intertwined also with the system of slavery
that was introduced to Canada, at first in the Atlantic regions where
enslaved Africans were taken to work on farms, in homes and in several
areas of industry.

Enslaved Africans had varied experiences in Canada. Missionaries, often
with the approval of colonial authorities, were tasked with the
conversion of enslaved people, including indigenous communities to
Christianity, as a moral and civilising duty. It was believed that the
lives of enslaved people would be improved by aligning them with Western
notions of ‘order’ and ‘obedience’. However, enslaved Africans
experienced on-going mental anguish through racism that was exacerbated
by being openly viewed as inferior and fundamentally different to
Europeans in colonies within Nova Scotia and Quebec.

Above all, the Mental health of enslaved Africans in Canada, as in other
parts of the Americas, was strongly affected by a forced conversion to
Christianity. Missionaries gave the impression that they were offering
spiritual comfort, but most enslaved Africans noted that their cultural
identities and coping mechanisms were being repressed. The promise of
spiritual salvation through Christianity, contrasted sharply with the
brutal reality of their lives.

Furthermore, the witness of violence against the indigenous communities,
including children, emboldened African slaves to find ways of rejecting
forced participation in Christian rituals- whether they were baptisms,
prayers, singing hymns or ‘breaking bread’. Many African rituals were
skilfully woven into Christian baptisms, prayers and hymns while the use
of drums and other instruments were seen as secret forms of
communications. Like in the USA, the ‘negro spirituals’ developed in
that context, as a form of resistance and resilience.

For some enslaved Africans, Christianity was seen as an avenue for
achieving some semblance of autonomy or an escape from their harsh
conditions of living, but for others, it was a tool of control,
pacifying and suppressing their desires for freedom. On the one hand,
enslaved Africans were told of an omnipotent, loving God, but on the
other, they experienced the harshest human injustices that resulted in
spiritual alienation, guilt and mental anguish. Missionaries spoke of
the caring afterlife as a reward for suffering, but ignored the
immediate trauma of enslavement that often led to people of African
heritage feeling helpless and hopeless in Canada.

**4.4 Contemporary Implications: - UK, USA, Canada and the Caribbean**

Today, Christianity continues to be a significant cultural force in the
African diaspora, but the theological tensions that were rife during the
colonial and missionary periods remain. In the UK, like the USA, Canada
and the Caribbean, African heritage Christians, including many
immigrants from Africa and the Caribbean, try to deal with the
historical legacies of racism within the church and the wider society.
In the UK, for example, the Pentecostal and African-derived churches
continue to grow, as Black churches, serving as institutions against
racial discrimination, inequality and cultural erasure. Black faith
leaders preach and emphasise themes of spiritual empowerment, economic
justice and racial equality. These ideas resonate strongly with the
historical experiences of African heritage communities.

As one reflects on the historical situation of colonisation and
missionary activities in former British Colonies – the Caribbean and the
Americas- one must note the ongoing psychological impacts on
contemporary African heritage communities. The historical legacy of
Christianity’s role in the oppression of people of African heritage can
still be felt today.

The tensions between Christianity’s oppressive and liberative legacies
continue to shape African heritage communities in the UK, USA, Canada
and the Caribbean. In the UK, Canada and the Caribbean,
African-initiated and Pentecostal churches thrive, often serving as
bastions of identity and resistance against systemic racism. These
congregations emphasise themes of spiritual empowerment and economic
justice, echoing the struggles of earlier generations.

Similarly, in the USA, Black Liberation Theology, championed by figures
like James Cone (1986), explored the relationship between faith and the
struggle for Black liberation, offering profound insights into the ways
theology empowered oppressed communities. This movement has become a
cornerstone of African American religious thinking. It emphasises that
God stands with the oppressed, drawing heavily on New Testament
principles of love and equality, as in “…Thou shalt love thy neighbour
as thyself. There is none other commandment greater than these…” *(Mark
12:31*) and “There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor
free, …for ye are all one in Christ Jesus” *(Galatians 3:28),* to
confront the racial injustices entrenched in both church and society.
Yet, even as these movements progress, they must contend with the
ongoing challenge of disentangling Christian teachings from their
colonial and imperial roots.

In the United States, The Black Church continued to emerge as both a
sanctuary and a central organisation for social change and political
activism (Lincoln, C.E., and Mamiya, L.H.,1990). Leaders like Richard
Allen, founder of the African Methodist Episcopal Church and Frederick
Douglass, the former slave who became an eloquent abolitionist, utilised
Christian principles to argue for racial equality, thus challenging the
moral legitimacy of slavery. The New Testament’s vision of the “Kingdom
of God,” with its promise of equality and justice, offered a compelling
counter-narrative to the racial hierarchies of that time

Increasingly, churches are integrating mental awareness into their
ministries. Initiatives such as pastoral counselling, mental health
workshops and partnerships with mental health professionals are becoming
more prevalent (Pargament, 1997). Such activities demonstrate a holistic
approach to well-being that aligns with New Testament principles of
caring for the whole believer’s body, mind and spirit.

The USA’s Civil Rights Movement of the 1060s, drew heavily on New
Testament principles of justice and equality, with leaders like the
Reverend Dr Martin Luther King Jr. framing their struggle in biblical
terms (King, M.L.,1963). Above all, the New Testament’s vision of the
‘Kingdom of God,’ with its promise of equality and justice, offered a
compelling counter-narrative to the racial hierarchies of the colonial
and missionary eras.

**4.5 Conclusion**

New Testament-derived Christianity, as introduced in the British
colonies and the Americas during the colonial and missionary eras,
played a multifaceted role in the lives of people of African heritage.
While it was wielded often to uphold systems of exploitation and
control, African communities across the diaspora reclaimed and
reinterpreted its teachings to resist oppression and affirm their
humanity. This ongoing process of theological reimagining highlights the
enduring relevance of faith in the struggle for justice, equality and
freedom. Additionally, as collaboration between churches and mental
health professionals increase, many more Christians are becoming better
able to confront the realities of ancestral struggles while striving to
remain mentally and spiritually resilient.

African heritage Christians believe that by examining the past, they
gain insights into the present: Christianity, in its truest form, holds
the potential to unite, heal and inspire, but only when it aligns with
the values of human dignity, respect and liberation that are at the
heart of the Gospels’ message.

Today, African heritage communities in the UK, USA, Canada and the
Caribbean are learning from the lessons of their enslaved ancestors by
turning to Christianity as espoused in the Black church, as a source of
resilience and strength. These Churches are instrumental in fostering
community solidarity, and advocating for social justice (Walker, 1980).
The teachings of love and community in the New Testament resonate
strongly among African congregants who promote inclusive and supportive
environments.

**6.2 Analysis of Survey Data**

**6.2.1 Quantitative Analysis**

The survey data were analysed using statistical software, Statistical
Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) to identify patterns and
correlations. Descriptive statistics summarised the demographic
characteristics and response frequencies. Inferential statistics,
including chi-square tests and regression analysis were employed to
study relationships between religious practices, beliefs and mental
health outcomes.

- **Descriptive Statistics**: Frequencies and numbers provided an
  overview of participants’ demographics, religious practices and mental
  health status. For example, the

distribution of church attendance frequency and the prevalence of
self-reported mental health conditions were charted.

- **Inferential Statistics:** Chi-square tests assessed the association
  between categorical variables , such as -country of residence and
  perceived importance of faith. Regression analysis explored the
  predictive value of religious practices on mental health outcomes,
  controlling demographic variables such as age and socioeconomic
  status.

**6.2.2 Key Findings**

- **Religious Practices and Mental Health:** Religious awareness,
  regular church attendance and personal religious practices were
  significantly associated with better self-reported mental health.
  Participants of African heritage who engaged more frequently in
  religious activities reported lower levels of anxiety, stress and
  depression.

- **Perceived Support:** The perceived support from African heritage
  religious communities was positively correlated with mental
  well-being. Participants who felt supported by their religious
  community, reported lower levels of stress.

- **Stigma and Help-Seeking:** In spite of the positive impact of faith,
  stigma around mental health persists. A substantial number of
  participants had shown reluctance to seek professional help because of
  stigma within their religious communities and family groups.

**6.3 Analysis of Interview Data:**

**6.3.1 Qualitative Analysis:**

Thematic analysis was conducted on the interview transcripts to identify
recurring themes and patterns. NVivo software facilitated the coding and
organisation of the qualitative data.

- **Coding Process:** Initial codes were created inductively from the
  data, capturing significant statements and phrases. These codes were
  then grouped into broader themes to reflect the participants’
  experiences and perspectives.

- **Theme Development:** Themes were developed iteratively, with
  constant comparison to ensure consistency and coherence. Some of the
  Key themes included the role of the New Testament teachings, community
  support, stigma, historical and cultural influences on mental health.

**6.3.2 Key Findings:**

- **New Testament Teachings:** Participants, people of African heritage,
  highlighted specific New Testament teachings such as forgiveness, hope
  and community involvement, as coping mechanisms. Scriptures like, “Be
  careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication
  with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God…shall keep
  hearts and minds through Christ Jesus.” (*Philippians 4:6-7)* were
  cited frequently as sources of comfort, hope and resilience.

- **Community Support:** The role of the Church communities in providing
  emotional or psychological and practical support were emphasised.
  Participants generally described their churches as havens where they
  could find peace, support and understanding.

- **Cultural and Historical Context:** The historical trauma of slavery
  and the continuing systemic racism, were recognised as significant
  stressors that impacted mental health. Participants professed that
  their faith provided a framework for understanding and managing these
  challenges.

- **Stigma and Mental Health:** Religious communities continued to be
  plagued with stigma. Participants, as people of African heritage,
  expressed the urgent need for more open discussions about mental
  health. They advocated for church leaders receiving training on mental
  health issues to improve their support of congregants while
  collaborating more with mental health professionals.

**Chapter 7: Further Findings and Discussions**

**7.1 Integration of Quantitative and Qualitative Findings:**

The heart of this research lay in this multi-layered, yet unique
question: “Do religious awareness, religious knowledge and religious
practice derived from New Testament Christianity heal the mind?” Among
people of African heritage living in the United Kingdom, United States
of America and Canada, the answer - though layered and complex – rings
out clearly from the data, the stories, perspectives and silences
between words; “YES, they do!”

The integration of survey and interview data provided a comprehensive
understanding of the impact of New Testament–derived Christianity on the
mental health of people of African heritage. The quantitative data
underscored the positive correlation between religious engagement and
mental health, while the qualitative data enriched these findings with
personal narratives and contextual insights.

New Testament Christianity, as lived and practised within these
diasporic communities, does much more than echo ancient doctrines; it
speaks to contemporary pain and sufferings, offering a language for
grief. It presents songs for sorrow and rituals for resilience. Through
scripture, prayer, communal worship, confessions and service, Christians
of African heritage find a framework to navigate life’s crises and
challenges. For many, the Black Church is not just a place or an
institution; it is an anchor, a shield, a sanctuary.

Religious awareness nurtures hope. Religious beliefs strengthen faith.
Religious knowledge develops identity. Religious practices – consistent,
shared and sacred – offer a path through the shadow, through
ambivalence. Faith that is vibrant, alive and authentic, is much more
than belief; it is the breath of life. Such faith restores, reorients
and reminds believers that they are the children of God, even when
others seem to deny it.

Yet, within this powerful revelation there lies a tension: guilt versus
healing, silence versus truth. The same faith that uplifts, can, if
misapplied, restrain – discourage the vulnerable, delay professional
treatment, or confuse genuine suffering with spiritual failure or sin.
Above all, the findings of this research show a duality: New
Testament–derived Christianity strengthens mental health through
community, meaning and resilience, but when separated from common
understanding and empathy, it can also inhibit help-seeking, thus
promoting internalised shame, guilt or blame.

**7.2 Limitations of this Research**

This Study is rich in narrative and nuance, but it has its limitations.
The sample size – fifty participants – though diverse and
demographically balanced, cannot speak for all Christians of African
heritage across the three major communities. The reliance on
self-reporting introduces bias; memory could have been flawed, and
social desirability often shapes what is said or left unsaid.

Moreover, while this research explored philosophy, theology, religious
and some mental health practices, it did not examine fully, the
denominational differences, or the theological nuances that may impact
mental health outcomes. Similarly, this study did not trace long-term
mental health projections or conduct clinical assessments which would be
necessary to draw salient diagnostic conclusions.

Furthermore, language also created a boundary; faith is often felt
before it is understood, and mental health is lived before it is named
or identified. The term ‘religious knowledge’ meant different things to
different people; to some, it meant the words of God found in the
scriptures, but to others, it meant their religious beliefs. Many
participants used terms like “spiritual warfare” or “breakthrough,”
where others said “trauma “ or “healing.” Translating these across
secular and spiritual lines required great care, but inevitably,
somethings could have been lost in translation.

**7.3 Implications for African Heritage Christians**

Doubtless, African heritage Christians in the United Kingdom, United
States of America and Canada can surely gain clarity and receive a
message from this study: “Your faith has been your fortress, but it
should not be your prison. The Church must remain a place of deliverance
– not only from sin, but also from suffering. It must continue to grow,
speak, and above all, listen.” This research shows that the practice of
praying need not replace therapy – it can precede it, support it, and
strengthen it. Ministers, Pastors and Faith Leaders must become allies
of counsellors and other mental health professionals. At the same time,
Faith leaders must teach the truth that mental illness is not moral
failure; rather, it is an experience of modern living, no different from
physical illness and no less deserving of compassion and care.

Above all, Christians of African heritage, shaped by the Gospels and
their ancestors’ struggle, carry within them, a well of strength,
resilience and courage. Yet, strength must be shared. The New Testament
urges believers to bear one another’s burdens – not to suffer silently,
but to heal collectively.

**7.4 Implications for Mental Health Practice and Religious
collaboration.**

So far, this research serves as a reminder for mental health
professionals, especially those working in multicultural and multifaith
settings: healing must proclaim the language of the people. The findings
suggest the potential for faith-based interventions to support mental
health within African heritage communities; churches should develop
mental health ministries and integrate mental health education into
their programmes. This could also enhance service delivery and community
outreach.

Furthermore, it is necessary to reiterate what the findings emphasise:
the need to address stigma within religious communities. Initiatives to
foster open dialogue about mental health and training for religious
leaders could mitigate stigma and encourage help-seeking behaviours.

**7.5 Recommendations for Future / Further Research**

This study contributes to the understanding of the intersection between
faith and mental health, but it has by no means exhausted the topic of
the influence of New Testament-derived Christianity on the mental health
of people of African heritage. It lays the groundwork for further
research that should go deeper and broader to explore:

- **Denominational Differences**: How do Pentecostal, Anglican, Baptist
  (including Spiritual Baptists) and non-denominational traditions shape
  mental health responses differently?

- **Youth Experiences:** How are younger African heritage Christians
  negotiating faith and mental health in increasingly secular societies?

- **The Role of Gender**: Do women and men experience religious coping
  and stigma differently in these communities?

- **Longitudinal Studies**: What is the long-term impact of religious
  coping versus professional mental health interventions?

- **Interfaith Comparison**: How do the findings of Christians of
  African Heritage compare with those of African Muslims or traditional
  African spiritualists in the diaspora?

Doubtless, more studies, including both qualitative and clinical
approaches, are needed to measure psychological outcomes, trace
therapeutic journeys, and design integrative models of care.
