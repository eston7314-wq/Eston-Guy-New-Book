# Songs for Sorrow

What the enslaved could not say aloud, they sang. On the plantations of the American South, the people who owned them sang Christian hymns in small churches. The people they owned took some of those hymns, blended them with the songs of Africa and sang them as their own, for comfort and for protest. This chapter asks what that singing has done for the minds of the people who carried it. I follow the song from the plantations into the churches, then outwards to Britain, the Caribbean and Canada. Wherever our people went, they took it with them, and they used it to carry sorrow and to keep going.<!-- note: story pass 2026-11-14: the plantation hymns (thesis 2456-2462, 3781-3786) moved up from section 1 to open the chapter; ch01's last line kept as the first sentence -->

Music is said to be a universal language. Someone once put it in words close to these, though I cannot now remember who: music expresses what cannot be put into words, and that which cannot remain silent.<!-- author-confirmed 2026-11-14: thesis 2430-2431 is "paraphrased in part, from someone who I cannot remember"; framed as his paraphrase, no source named, no quotation marks (open-claims 52) --> Whether it is sung or played, music goes with people to festivals and ceremonies, into rituals and religious services, and particularly into times of struggle and suffering.<!-- note: thesis 2431-2434 -->

Some people hear gospel music as a Sunday style, or as entertainment that happens to mention God. I believe singing has been one of the ways our people have looked after their minds for more than 400 years, and that it still is. That is a belief, drawn from history and from the songs themselves. Nobody in my research measured it: the survey I gave to fifty people asked no question about music, so I have no numbers to offer here, only the story of the songs as I have come to know it. Researchers elsewhere have studied religious song, choir singing and mental health, but I have not yet weighed what they found, so I make no claim from their work.<!-- note: ch02-evidence.md sections 3 and 8.3 (studies existence-confirmed only; findings not described, CLAUDE.md fact-checking rule 2). --><!-- note: "four hundred years" rests on 1619 (thesis 2446-2447). The church is where the songs have lived. Religious communities often give people strong networks of emotional and practical support, and that support helps mental health. Across the African diaspora the church is a centre of community life, and much of the cohesion it builds, and the hope of peace and comfort it offers, comes through prayer, gospel music and the singing of hymns. Gospel has been one of the clearest expressions of spiritual belief and cultural identity among Black people in the USA, the UK, Canada and the Caribbean, and its influence has reached Black people all over the world. ## Our ancestors made their masters' hymns into songs of their own

Our part of this story begins in 1619, when 20 African men were taken to Jamestown, Virginia. Over many years others were taken to Britain's colonies. They used musical instruments and spirituals to build support for one another, in their suffering and in their struggle for freedom. The rhythms comforted them and gave them the mental strength to carry on.<!-- note: thesis 2446-2451 (author's statement; no source in the thesis; ch02-evidence.md D3) -->

The spirituals began with those plantation hymns. The blend of the masters' hymns and African songs made work-songs, which became known first as "Negro spirituals" and later as African American spirituals. They joined African musical traditions to Christian themes, and our ancestors sang them for spiritual and mental upliftment, resilience and protest.<!-- note: thesis 2456-2462 (history; "Caucasian slave masters or plantation owners") and 3781-3786 (purpose phrase, "spiritual and mental upliftment, resilience and protest"); ch01 cut-list row 10, carried forward to ch02 by author approval 2026-11-13 -->

This is how the Exodus became song. In chapter 1 I described Moses going to Pharaoh with God's demand, "Let my people go" (Exodus 5:1), and a people in chains who heard it and knew at once that it was about them. One of their spirituals, "Go Down, Moses", sets that story to song.<!-- author-confirmed 2026-11-14: the author names "Go Down, Moses" for the Exodus promise from ch01. Further north the story was the same. In Canada, too, the spirituals grew up among the enslaved as a form of resistance and resilience. Chapter 1 told how African rituals there were woven into baptisms, prayers and hymns, and how drums could carry messages the masters could not read.<!-- note: thesis 3964-3965 (Canada section, 4.3.4); ch01 cut-list row 22, carried forward to ch02 by author approval 2026-11-13. Those hymns came first from the people who enslaved our ancestors, and some of our ancestors were forced to sing them. In chapter 1 I wrote about enslaved people who looked for ways to refuse forced hymns and forced baptisms, and about others for whom Christianity became a tool of control that pacified them and suppressed their desire for freedom. That leaves a hard possibility: that the comfort of a hymn was part of the control, a way of making suffering bearable so that no one rose against it. As I read it, "Go Down, Moses" answers that. People who sang the story of a slave people led out of Egypt were not singing themselves into silence. They kept the words of faith, set them to African sound and turned them towards freedom. Here the double-edged inheritance is in the music itself: hymns sung by the masters came to carry the comfort and the protest of the people they owned.<!-- note: counterweight from ch02-evidence.md section 8.1: thesis 3958-3961 (forced participation in hymns), already used in ch01; referred back --><!-- note: dev edit 2026-11-14: thesis 3966-3970 (Christianity as "a tool of control, pacifying and suppressing their desires for freedom"), used in ch01 section 4; referred back. If you grew up in a Black church, you may have sung spirituals long before anyone told you where they came from. Knowing their history can change how you sing them. The words in your mouth were first sung by people in chains.

Those spirituals were the forerunners of gospel music, because they joined a strong emotional impact to religious fervour.<!-- note: thesis 2462-2464 -->

## Gospel carried the spirituals into the cities and onto records

Gospel music began early in the twentieth century in the USA, among African Americans, and mostly in church. Blues, jazz and hymns all shaped it, and the blend reflected the social and political life of the time. That blend became possible when African Americans moved from the South to the cities of the North in what is called the Great Migration. There, churches became one of the main ways the music spread, and they gave choirs and solo artists a platform. So the music travelled first with people who had left home, as it would again.<!-- note: thesis 2454-2455, 2473-2478. No dates for the Great Migration in the thesis; none added (ch02-evidence.md D5) -->

Organisations carried it further. James Cleveland founded the Gospel Music Workshop of America in 1968, and it helped to shape the music and to grow it, to the benefit of one generation of artists after another.<!-- note: thesis 2479-2482 -->

The years from the 1930s to the 1960s are called gospel's Golden Age. Mahalia Jackson, Thomas Dorsey and Sister Rosetta Tharpe stand out among the African Americans who made it. Dorsey, who is often referred to as the "Father of Gospel Music", brought elements of the blues into spiritual tunes and made a sound that appealed both to churchgoers and to listeners outside the church. Tharpe, a singer and songwriter, was the first great recording artist of gospel music, and among the first gospel musicians to reach audiences for rhythm and blues and for rock and roll.<!-- note: thesis 2484-2494 (author's statement; no source in the thesis). "1930s-1960s" corrected from the thesis's own heading at 2484 (2486 reads "1930s–960s"). Existence only, content not checked: Harris, The Rise of Gospel Blues (OUP; sources.md); Wald 2003, American Quarterly 55(3), doi 10.1353/aq.2003.0031 -->

Many of the best-known singers of the last century began in the same pews. Mahalia Jackson, Ray Charles, Aretha Franklin, Little Richard (Richard Wayne Penniman), Roberta Flack, Whitney Houston and Jimmy Cliff, to name a few, started their careers as young children singing hymns and spiritual tunes in Black churches. Later, jazz, soul and then R&B were added, and that revolution in gospel music helped to carry their careers around the world. I count this among gospel's gifts to our communities: it lifted them socially and culturally, and it gave them a voice for celebration.<!-- note: thesis 2585-2593 (author's statement; "mainly in the U.S.A."; Jimmy Cliff is from Jamaica, 2543, so no country is given for the churches). ## The songs crossed the ocean and found new homes

Gospel came to Britain after the Second World War, with the Caribbean people who settled there. The Windrush generation arrived from the Caribbean islands between 1948 and 1973, and Black British communities quickly took up gospel music and Pentecostal hymns.<!-- note: thesis 2498-2502; "post-World War 11" read as the Second World War (ch02-evidence.md D8) -->

Sister Rosetta Tharpe, gospel's first great recording artist, came to Britain too. She first toured Great Britain late in 1957, performing at the London Palladium on 15 December and also in Wolverhampton and Bradford. In the Caribbean, gospel meets the islands' own music and makes new expressions of faith. Hymns from Pentecostal, Moravian and Baptist churches are sung to Afrobeat, and calypso, reggae and soca take up gospel themes, so that the region's music reflects many kinds of spirituality. Two of those churches, the Moravian and the Pentecostal, run through my own family's story, which I told in chapter 1.<!-- note: thesis 2535-2539; family line is the author's testimony (2026-11-11), ch01 opening --> The scene there is still growing, as modern churches take up contemporary styles and the music finds a wider audience abroad, and I believe it adds to the spiritual and mental development of people of African heritage in the region.<!-- note: thesis 2548-2552, framed as the author's view (config rule 5) -->

Some artists have put gospel messages into their music and championed hope and social justice. Among them are the calypsonians Mighty Sparrow (Slinger Francisco) and Lord Shorty (Garfield Blackman), the creator of soca, both from Trinidad and Tobago, and Jimmy Cliff and Bob Marley from Jamaica. In "Redemption Song", Marley calls on his listeners to free their own minds from mental slavery, and he describes what he is singing as songs of freedom. That call speaks to people of African heritage all over the world.<!-- note: thesis 2541-2548. Lyric paraphrased, not quoted (author decision 2026-11-13; ch02-evidence.md L6) -->

Marley's line sits close to the question of this book: whether faith can help to make a person whole in mind, and whether it can sometimes do the opposite. Chapter 1 described a wound that outlived the chains, and "Redemption Song" asks its listeners to take part in their own freeing. I believe faith can help with that work of the mind, and later chapters show where it needs other help beside it.

In Canada, gospel has grown strongly, particularly in Black churches, and it draws on everything from traditional hymns to hip hop and R&B. The Gospel Music Association of Canada promotes gospel and involves communities through its artists. Its GMA Canada Covenant Awards celebrate what gospel musicians have achieved, and they also give a stage to musicians who are just starting out.<!-- note: thesis 2557-2566 -->

## Song gave our people a voice for justice

Gospel has long been one of the ways Black people make themselves heard in the fight for equality. Chapter 1 told the story of the civil rights movement in America through Dr King. The movement also had its songs. Many spirituals and gospel songs were sung in those years, when activists of different races campaigned for justice and equality, and the spirituals among them had come down from the plantations. I think it matters that they sang. People singing together in a crowd can find a courage that each of them might not find alone.<!-- note: thesis 2570-2574. Ch01 owns Dr King; one clause only (blueprint v2) -->

That tradition has not ended. In the UK, the USA, the Caribbean and Canada, gospel is still used to speak against racial inequality and poverty and to build up communities, and it inspires movements that aim to change conditions for the better. It remains, as I see it, a powerful tool for social transformation, and for building resilience.<!-- note: thesis 2576-2581, framed as the author's view -->

## Old hymns and prayers still hold our people up

The churches of the African diaspora in the UK, the USA and Canada are an important source of social connection for African immigrants, who need the support of a community to keep their emotional balance. I believe that by reducing loneliness and isolation, these churches promote mental health. Music is a large part of how they do it. Many Black churches have brought gospel into their services and added Afrobeat and R&B to favourite and traditional songs, so that younger people in our diaspora and in multi-ethnic communities can take them up.<!-- note: thesis 2597-2605, mental-health claim framed as the author's belief (config rule 5) -->

If you came to Britain, the USA or Canada from Africa or the Caribbean, or your parents did, you may know what that support is worth. A new country can be lonely, and a church where the songs are familiar can be the first place where someone knows your name.

During the Covid-19 pandemic of 2020-2021, the Liverpool Lighthouse, in North Liverpool, formed a gospel choir to connect the community through singing, and to help people stay well during lockdown. It is led by Anu Omideyi, who was a criminal barrister in London from 2001 and in 2015 moved to music and performance full-time. She is Music Director at the Liverpool Lighthouse, where she leads the gospel choirs. ## Reflection and practice

Think of a song that has carried you: a hymn from childhood, a gospel song from a hard year or a chorus someone in your family sang around the house. This week, sing it or play it once, slowly, and listen to the words as if you were hearing them for the first time. Notice what the song says about God, and what it says Jesus taught. Notice too how your body and your mood respond. If no song comes to mind, ask an older member of your family or your church which songs kept them going, and learn one of them.

### For church leaders

- Tell the story behind the songs. When your choir sings a spiritual or an old gospel hymn, say in a sentence or two where it came from and what it meant to the people who first sang it.
- Use music to reach people who are alone. A choir, a singing group or a midweek hymn gathering can draw isolated members and newcomers from abroad into the life of the church.

### For families

- Pass the songs down. Sing with your children and grandchildren the hymns and choruses that carried your family, and tell them when you needed them.
- Let the young bring their music. When younger members set the old hymns to Afrobeat or R&B, welcome it as the same faith in a new sound.

### For clinicians

- Ask about music. A patient of African heritage may use hymns, gospel or sung prayer to cope with distress. What they sing, and when, can tell you a good deal about their supports.
- Treat singing as a support alongside care. If a patient is trying to sing or pray their way through symptoms that are getting worse, gently explore what other help they would accept.

### Questions for small groups

1. Which song has carried you, or someone you love, through a dark time? What did its words give you?
2. Our ancestors took hymns sung by the people who enslaved them and made them songs of comfort and protest. What does that say to you about a faith that has been misused?
3. In "Redemption Song", Bob Marley calls on his listeners to free their own minds. What might that mean for you, or for your church, today?
