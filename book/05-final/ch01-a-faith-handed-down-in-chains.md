# A Faith Handed Down in Chains

The question I left at the end of the preface begins, for me, in my own family. Christianity came into my family line from my paternal grandparents who embraced Christianity through the Moravian Church in the Caribbean island of Tobago, while my maternal grandparents followed Christianity through the Anglican Church. My wife, children and I also embraced the Church of England practices while paying homage also to the Pentecostal and Spiritual Baptist practices. <!-- author-confirmed 2026-11-11 -->

Two of those churches, the Anglican and the Moravian, were carried to the Caribbean from Europe, and when I follow them back far enough I come to the plantations and to the ships that brought our ancestors there. What did our ancestors do with a faith that the people who enslaved them also claimed?

The answer has two edges: the faith was used against our ancestors, and they took it back and made it their own. I call this the double-edged inheritance, and I believe that holding both truths at once is part of being well. Many of us were raised on only one half of the story. Some were told that Christianity was the slave master's religion and can only hurt us. Others were told that our faith has nothing to do with that history at all. The record shows something more complicated, and more hopeful, than either.

## The faith arrived in the company of empire

Christian mission moved with European empire from the sixteenth century, and in the British colonies from the seventeenth century until emancipation in 1834. Britain built plantations across the Caribbean and North America, and missionaries followed, building schools, hospitals and churches. Those were real goods. They were also part of an enterprise that ran on stolen labour. The British colonies grew rich on tobacco, rice and cotton, and the plantations that grew them demanded an ever larger workforce.

Disease and violence had killed many of the indigenous peoples of the Caribbean and the Americas, so the empire turned to Africa for people to enslave. About 12.5 million Africans were put on ships for the Americas, and about 10.7 million arrived. Most of the difference is the people who died at sea.<!-- note: Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, SlaveVoyages, "Estimates", https://www.slavevoyages.org/assessment/estimates (modelled estimates; search-level, ch01-evidence.md T1) --> Missionaries came close behind, sent to "save" the souls of the enslaved and of the indigenous people who had survived: the Arawaks and Caribs in the Caribbean and the Cree, Iroquois and Ojibwa in Canada.

Many of those missionaries took their charge from the last words of Jesus in Matthew's Gospel: "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world" (Matthew 28:19-20). Christians call this the Great Commission. In the hands of empire it became a duty to convert and "civilise" people of African heritage, who were seen as heathen and primitive.

Other verses were put to darker use. "Servants, be obedient to them that are your masters according to the flesh, with fear and trembling, in singleness of your heart, as unto Christ" (Ephesians 6:5). The same instruction appears in two other letters (Colossians 3:22; Titus 2:9). Preached to enslaved people, these verses taught that obeying an earthly master was part of God's ordained order, and so Christianity was made to justify the enslaving of Africans.

Frederick Douglass saw what that looked like in practice. Canada had its own part in this history. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the British and French colonial powers spread Catholicism and Anglicanism to strengthen their hold over land and people. Slavery came too, first in the Atlantic regions, where enslaved Africans worked on farms, in homes and in trades. In Nova Scotia and Quebec, enslaved Africans were openly treated as inferior, and that racism added to their anguish. ## Our ancestors took the book back

Enslaved Africans and their descendants heard the same sermons and drew something else from them. They took the scriptures that were meant to keep them subdued and found in them reasons to resist, as a people, and to hope for liberation.

The story of the Exodus, though rooted in the Old Testament, spoke to them as the story of God bringing a people out of bondage. In it God says, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; for I know their sorrows" (Exodus 3:7). Then Moses carries God's demand to Pharaoh: "Let my people go" (Exodus 5:1). A people in chains did not need anyone to explain what that story meant. Chapter 2 tells how they turned it into song.

The New Testament gave them another verse to hold up against the master's verses. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). In the King James wording, "bond" means a slave. Here was Paul putting the slave and the free side by side, as equals in Christ.<!-- author-decision 2026-11-13: #1 narrowed (config rule 9) -->

The teaching of Jesus about the Kingdom of God gave them a whole story to set against the plantation. The Kingdom promised equality and justice, and the plantation divided people into owners and property. If God's Kingdom had no such order, then the plantation was not God's will, whatever the preacher said on Sunday.

Our ancestors also carried their African spiritual heritage into Christian worship. As I read this history, across the Caribbean, the USA and Canada, many African rituals were woven into baptisms, prayers and hymns. Drums and other instruments could carry messages the masters could not read.<!-- note: thesis 3961-3964. ## Missions differed, and even the Moravians worked within slavery

The work of Anglican, Catholic and Baptist missionaries was bound up with colonial interests.<!-- author-decision 2026-11-13: #3 cut (config rule 8; cut-list row 37) --> Religion propped up the social order, and British missions in the Caribbean served the empire's economy as much as they served God.

On the American mainland, most missions worked among Native American peoples. Spanish Franciscan friars entered New Mexico with an expedition in 1581, and Junípero Serra later founded nine missions in California, beginning with San Diego in 1769. They converted indigenous people, sometimes by force; enslaved Africans brought by the Spanish laboured alongside them in missions from Florida to California. The Puritans, Protestants who wanted to purify the Church of England of Roman Catholic practices, came to New England early in the seventeenth century hoping to build "a city on a hill". They set up "praying towns" for Native Americans and kept enslaved Africans in their homes and on their farms. Methodists and Presbyterians reached Oregon in the 1830s.<!-- note: ch01-evidence.md E4 (California 1769, not 1541) and section 2, Oregon (search-level). One woman's life shows both sides of that church. Mary Prince was born into slavery in Bermuda around 1788 and was later enslaved on Turks Island and in Antigua. In Antigua one Christmas, an enslaved woman took her to a Methodist prayer meeting at her husband's house on a plantation called Winthorps, and there Mary heard what she called "the first prayers I ever understood". The meeting, she said, "led my spirit to the Moravian church". She did not tell her mistress, knowing she would be refused leave; when she carried the children's lunch to school, she ran round to hear the teachers. The Moravian women there, she said, "taught me to read in the class".

The English Church was more guarded. It had baptised her in 1817, but, as she understood him, the clergyman would not let her attend his Sunday school without her owner's written permission. When she married Daniel James, a free carpenter, about Christmas 1826, the wedding took place in the Moravian chapel at Spring Gardens, because, as she put it, "English marriage is not allowed to slaves".

Her owners later brought her to London, where she was forced out of their house. The only people she knew in England were the Moravian missionaries at Hatton Garden, and they took in her trunk. Her *History*, which appeared in 1831, was the first account of an enslaved Black woman's life published in Britain. ## The mind paid a price, and the wound outlived slavery

For some enslaved Africans, Christianity was a way to win a little autonomy or some escape from the harshness of their lives. For others it was a tool of control that pacified them and suppressed their desire for freedom. They were told of an all-powerful, loving God while they lived through the harshest human injustice, and that gap could produce spiritual alienation, guilt and mental anguish.

Go back to that Christmas prayer meeting at Winthorps. The host was Henry, a Black driver and the husband of the woman who had brought Mary Prince. After the prayers and hymns, each person spoke in turn of their sorrow over their own sins. Henry confessed that he had been very cruel to the slaves, and said his master's orders compelled him. A driver, he said, sometimes had to beat his own wife or sister if his master told him to. He asked them all to forgive him, and he prayed that God would forgive him. Mary cried the whole night and was too ashamed to speak.<!-- note: Prince 1831, History (Project Gutenberg #17851, opened; passage re-read in the same file by story-injector 2026-11-11). Paraphrased; only "the first prayers I ever understood" is quoted, in section 3. Prince's word for a driver is "ranger" --> In one man you can see the guilt and the anguish: he had done what his master ordered, and he brought the sin of it to God as his own.

Missionaries spoke of a caring afterlife as the reward for suffering, and said little about the trauma of enslavement itself, which often left people of African heritage feeling helpless and hopeless. I believe that, for many, forced conversion also pushed down the African ways of coping they had brought with them: their rituals, their music and their understanding of the spirit world. Some resisted. As I read the record, seeing violence done to indigenous communities, children among them, emboldened some enslaved Africans to find ways of refusing forced baptisms, prayers, hymns and the breaking of bread.

Our ancestors left words for that pain. Douglass describes a year under a man named Edward Covey, to whom his master hired him out to be broken: "I was broken in body, soul, and spirit. The faith that was used to wound also gives us words for grief. ## The hidden church became a refuge and a force for change

The struggle also produced its own theology. James Cone published *Black Theology and Black Power* in 1969 and *A Black Theology of Liberation* the year after. It drew on the New Testament's teaching of love and equality, and on Galatians 3:28, to confront racial injustice in church and society. At the same moment in Latin America, Catholic bishops meeting at Medellín in 1968 and the priest Gustavo Gutiérrez, whose *A Theology of Liberation* first appeared in 1971, were developing liberation theology. That movement arose in Roman Catholicism and seeks to live out faith by helping the poor and oppressed, including through political and civic life. Cone's first book came out before Gutiérrez's, and the two movements grew up side by side.<!-- note: ch01-evidence.md E5 and section 2 (search-level). Cone not quoted --> Liberation theology found a ready hearing among Christians of the African diaspora, who hear Christ's message as one of liberation from oppression and injustice. Both movements have had to keep disentangling Christian teaching from its colonial and imperial roots.

The same double edge runs through our churches in Britain and Canada today. Many African heritage Christians, including people who came from Africa and the Caribbean, still live with the legacy of racism, in the church as well as in wider society. I have observed that the Black churches are increasingly becoming centres of community life in Britain and the USA as they are in the Caribbean and Africa, regions from which people migrate, today. I see many of them as strongholds of identity and of resistance to systemic racism, where preachers speak of spiritual empowerment, economic justice and racial equality.<!-- author-decision 2026-11-13: #10 author's observation (config rule 9) --> African heritage Christians believe that by examining the past, they gain insights into the present: Christianity, in its truest form, holds the potential to unite, heal and inspire, but only when it aligns with the values of human dignity, respect and liberation that are at the heart of the Gospels' message. I believe that too.

When I think of my grandparents' churches, I see the double-edged inheritance in one family line. Those churches had travelled with empire, and their roots ran back to the plantation. My grandparents embraced the faith all the same, and through them it came down to me. Our ancestors had done the same long before them, in the open and in hiding. Douglass and Mary Prince kept their faith and told the truth about what was done in its name. I believe that honesty is good for the mind as well as the soul. What the enslaved could not say aloud, they sang.

## Reflection and practice

Think about how Christianity reached your own family. You may know a church, an island, a grandparent's name or a hymn. You may know very little. Either way, the faith you hold, or the faith you left, came down to you through people who had to decide what it meant under pressure. Sit for a few minutes with one question: which parts of that inheritance have helped me, and which parts have I been carrying without examining them? Write down one verse that has steadied you and one that has been used to keep you quiet. Bring both to God in prayer, as our ancestors brought theirs, and notice how you feel when you set them side by side.

### For church leaders

- Preach the hard verses honestly. When Ephesians 6:5 comes up in the lectionary or in Bible study, say how it was used against our ancestors, and set it beside Galatians 3:28.
- Make room for lament. Give your congregation a regular place in worship to grieve racial injustice together, and to name it before God.

### For families

- Ask the oldest members of your family how Christianity came into the family line, and write down what they say before it is lost.
- Talk with young people about both edges of the history, so that neither "it was the master's religion" nor "it has nothing to do with us" is the only story they hear.

### For clinicians

- When a patient of African heritage speaks about faith, remember that it may carry family and community history as well as belief. Ask what it has meant to their family too.
- Take reports of racism seriously as a source of stress in themselves, and do not treat a patient's mention of history as a distraction from their symptoms.

### Questions for small groups

1. How did Christianity come into your family, as far as you know, and what difference does knowing (or not knowing) make to you?
2. Frederick Douglass loved "the Christianity of Christ" and rejected "the Christianity of this land". Where would you draw that line in the church today?
3. Mary Prince said that keeping down her "fretting" was "a hard and heavy task". When has your faith held while the strain remained, and who helped you carry it?
