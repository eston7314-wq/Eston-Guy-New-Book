## Verdict first

The book has a real, unoccupied position. None of the five comparable titles I checked covers the UK, the USA and Canada together. None reads the New Testament texts one by one. None joins the colonial history, gospel music and an honest account of harm in a single book for lay readers.

Its weakest point is the one the old plan built a whole part around: the "fifty voices". Thesis line 4624 says the fifty responses "were generated". Chapter 6 of the thesis contains no participant quotations, and its 27 charts did not survive conversion: only the captions remain (4734-4857). The consent wording covers "academic research purposes" and "academic journals" (4139, 4585, 4590), not a trade book. Until the author resolves this, the research cannot carry a chapter. So this blueprint gives it none, and it treats every findings-based statement as `[RESEARCH NEEDED: confirm survey data are from real participants]`.

## The promise (one sentence)

This book shows people of African heritage, and the families, church members and professionals around them, how the teachings, practices and community life of the New Testament can support mental health alongside professional care, and how to recognise and refuse the ways the same faith is sometimes used to wound.

## Positioning for a general reader

**Shelf:** Christian living / pastoral care / Christianity and mental health. The book-marketer confirms the category.

**Angle:** a Black British educator, with 30 years of pastoral care in North London schools behind him, reads the New Testament, the history of slavery and mission, and gospel music to ask one question: when does faith heal the mind, and when does it harm? He answers warmly, from inside the church, with practical help at the end of each chapter for families, church leaders and clinicians.

## Part One: Roots

### 01. A Faith Handed Down in Chains

**Word budget:** 4,500

**Essential arguments**
1. The faith arrived as a companion of empire. The Great Commission (Matthew 28:19-20) became a mandate to "civilise", and Ephesians 6:5 ("Servants, be obedient...") was used to justify slavery (3749-3770).
2. Enslaved Africans did not simply receive the faith; they took it back. They read Exodus as deliverance, Galatians 3:28 as equality and the Kingdom of God as a counter-story to racial hierarchy (3772-3781, 3686-3690, 4029-4031).
3. Missions differed. The Moravians taught spiritual equality yet worked within slavery; the colonial churches served the empire's economy (3828-3853, 3906-3916). The Spanish Franciscans, the Puritans and the Oregon missions (3855-3921) shrink to one paragraph: they are not about African-heritage communities.
4. The mental cost. Forced conversion repressed African ways of coping and promised an afterlife while ignoring present trauma: "spiritual alienation, guilt and mental anguish" (3940-3976). The trauma outlives slavery (1749-1761, 3672-3677, 3993-3998).
5. From "invisible institution" to the Black Church: Richard Allen, Frederick Douglass, Dr King and liberation theology (James Cone). The church became both refuge and resistance (1284-1295, 1564-1583, 3678-3709, 4008-4046).

**Owns / does not:** owns the history of mission and slavery, the Black Church's origins, Dr King, liberation theology, Galatians 3:28 and Ephesians 6:5. It does not tell how spirituals were made (ch02 owns that; 3781-3786 duplicates 2458-2462, so cut it here and point forward), and it does not describe the Black Church's community work today (ch06).
**Shift:** "Christianity was the slave-master's religion, so it can only hurt us" / "Our faith has nothing to do with that history" -> "The faith was used against our ancestors and reclaimed by them; holding both truths is part of being well."
**Story slot:** must prove that this history lives on in families. Source candidate: 98-101 (Father Perkins first inspired the author to study religion). Also needed: [AUTHOR STORY NEEDED: how Christianity came into your own family line, such as a grandparent's church in the Caribbean or Africa, or a family memory of being told to "obey"]. **Open the chapter on the story, not on a date** (see Structural risks).
**Hook into ch02:** What the enslaved could not say aloud, they sang.
**Promises made:**
- We will hear how these same scriptures became songs of freedom (paid off in ch02).
- The trauma named here will be met with practical care, not left as history (paid off in ch08).

**Fact-check flags for the researcher:** Dober and Nitschmann reached St Thomas in 1732, not "1772" (3840-3843). "1060s" should read 1960s (4040). The period given, "early 16th century to the late 18th century" (3734), conflicts with the 17th-century start at 3752. A Fanon citation supports a claim about hope (3768). Hastings, Bediako, Murrell and Graham are not in the bibliography.

### 02. Songs for Sorrow

**Word budget:** 4,000

**Essential arguments**
1. Music "expresses what cannot be put into words, and that which cannot remain silent" (2430-2431). Enslaved Africans blended their masters' hymns with African song into spirituals, for comfort and for group support (2446-2464).
2. Gospel was born of the spirituals and spread through the Great Migration, through Thomas Dorsey, Sister Rosetta Tharpe and Mahalia Jackson, and through the Gospel Music Workshop of America (2454-2494).
3. It crossed the Atlantic three ways. With Windrush it came to Britain, where the London Community Gospel Choir and Liverpool's lockdown choir carried it on (2496-2530). In the Caribbean it met calypso and reggae, as in Marley's "emancipate yourself from mental slavery" (2532-2552). In Canada it is celebrated at the GMA Covenant Awards (2554-2566).
4. Song served as protest and as resilience, in the civil rights era and in activism since (2568-2581).
5. Today, Afrobeats and R&B bring younger and mixed-heritage people back to the old hymns. Music is social connection that eases isolation (2524-2530, 2597-2605).

**Owns / does not:** owns spirituals, gospel's history and the Marley line. It does not cover hymn-singing as a weekly spiritual practice (ch05 owns that, with Colossians 3:16) or Dr King (ch01).
**Shift:** "Gospel music is a Sunday style or entertainment" -> "Singing has been a mental-health practice for our people for four hundred years, and still is."
**Story slot:** must prove a song can carry a person through. Source candidate: 2515-2530, the Liverpool Lighthouse choir in lockdown (verify). Also needed: [AUTHOR STORY NEEDED: a hymn or gospel song that carried you or your family through a dark time, or a school choir or assembly moment from your teaching years].
**Hook into ch03:** The songs carried the teachings of Jesus; now open the teachings themselves.
**Promises made:**
- These songs drew on the teachings of Jesus, which the next chapter opens (paid off in ch03).

**Flags:** Cut or verify 2436-2444 (Charlemagne, Mahmud of Ghazni and Tang slavery used as a music claim; unsourced). Verify that "Jesus taught me how to laugh" is by Willie Nelson (2467-2469). Elevation Worship is a US band, not a UK one (2510-2511). Verify Tharpe's Liverpool role (2502-2505). **Lyric permissions:** "Redemption Song", "Oh Happy Day" and the BeBe Winans line need clearance or paraphrase.

## Part Two: What the New Testament Offers

### 03. The Way Home: The Teachings and Parables of Jesus

**Word budget:** 4,200

**Essential arguments**
1. Love of neighbour and of enemy (Mark 12:31; Matthew 5:44) is hardest for those who have been wronged. Yet unconditional love can build self-acceptance and loosen internalised negative stereotypes (1867-1888). Soften "eliminates racism" (1884-1885) to what a tradition holds.
2. Forgiveness frees a person from anger and resentment: the Lord's Prayer, and "Father, forgive them" on the cross (1880-1882, 1890-1901). **Guardrail:** forgiving is not the same as staying in harm's way. Plant it here; ch09 pays it off.
3. The Good Samaritan crosses prejudice with mercy (1956-1960).
4. The Prodigal Son: a father runs to a child who is estranged. Speak to young people cut off over drugs, alcohol or teenage pregnancy, and to the families who cut them off (1962-1976).
5. The Sower and the Kingdom of God: faith that grows in stony ground, and "take no thought... seek ye first the Kingdom" as relief from worry (1978-1987, 1738-1747, 3617-3621).

## Part Three: When Faith Hurts

### 07. When Faith Becomes a Burden: Guilt, Demons and Stigma

**Word budget:** 4,600

**Essential arguments**
1. Guilt and sin: some believers read a mental illness as personal sin or spiritual weakness, so they hide it or refuse treatment (2621-2643).
2. Spiritual warfare and witchcraft: depression is read as demonic oppression or an ancestral curse. Some fear that treatment invites spiritual attack, and families who have seen a relative sectioned carry fear of services (2645-2671).
3. Stigma takes three forms, social, self and structural (1669-1675), and is compounded by a cultural mistrust of services rooted in history (1695-1704, 2676-2704).
4. Communal culture can make outside help feel like betraying family or church (2706-2726). The prosperity gospel frames hardship as a spiritual test (3623-3637).
5. The turn: some leaders now pair deliverance with professional care (3661-3668). The same faith that uplifts can, if misapplied, restrain (4895-4903). **Mental illness is not moral failure** (4942-4944).

## Structural risks

### The three biggest

1. **The research cannot yet carry weight.** The book's distinctive asset, original survey and interview research across three countries, is unusable until the author answers line 4624 and the consent question. If the answer is bad, the positioning line "original research from 50 participants" in the config must go, and the book stands on its scripture, history and pastoral experience.
